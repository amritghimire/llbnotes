## Start here: what is "property"?

In daily speech, "property" means a thing. "This phone is my property."

Lawyers mean something slightly different. Property is not the phone itself. It is the set of **rights** you have over the phone, which the law protects against everyone else:

- You can **use** it.
- You can **stop others** from taking or using it.
- You can **sell**, gift or lend it.
- You can **leave it** to your children when you die.

If you lend the phone to a friend, she holds it, but it is still your property. If someone steals it, he holds it, but it is still your property. So property is a **legal relationship** between a person and others about a thing.

Some property has no body at all. A song you write, a company share, a bank deposit, or a trademark like a company logo is property, though you cannot touch it.

## 8.1 Basic concept of property

### Plain meaning

Property is anything, material or not, over which a person can have rights that the law protects and that have economic value. It usually can be used, enjoyed, transferred, or bring benefit.

### Definitions by jurists

| Jurist | Definition (standard textbook wording or paraphrase) |
|---|---|
| **Blackstone** | Property is "that sole and despotic dominion which one man claims and exercises over the external things of the world, in total exclusion of the right of any other individual in the universe." |
| **Austin** | Property (or ownership) is a right over a thing that is "indefinite in point of user, unrestricted in point of disposition, and unlimited in point of duration." |
| **Bentham** | "Property is nothing but a basis of expectation": the expectation of getting certain advantages from a thing, which the law secures. |
| **Salmond** | Uses the word in several senses, from all legal rights of a person down to only the ownership of material things (see below). |
| **Proudhon** | Attacked private property: "Property is theft" (*What is Property?*, 1840). |

### Salmond's four senses of property

Salmond explained that "property" is used in four senses, from the widest to the narrowest. This list appears in almost every textbook.

| Sense | What it covers | Example |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Widest | **All the legal rights** of a person, of every kind | Life, liberty, reputation, land, money. This is Locke's use: people unite to protect their "lives, liberties and estates", which he called property |
| 2. Proprietary rights only | Rights that have **money value** (a person's "estate"), not personal rights | Land, debts, shares. Excludes rights to life, liberty, reputation (the person's "status") |
| 3. Proprietary rights **in rem** only | Money-value rights good **against the whole world**, not rights against one person | Ownership of land, copyright. Excludes a debt, which is a right against one debtor only |
| 4. Narrowest | Only the **ownership of material things** (corporeal property) | Ownership of a house, a car, a cow |

In law today, "property" usually means sense 2 or 3: rights of economic value. A right **in rem** is a right against everyone (I own this land; nobody may trespass). A right **in personam** is a right against a particular person (Ram owes me Rs 10,000).

### Essential features of property

1. **Subject (owner):** a person, natural or legal (a company, a trust, the state).
2. **Object:** a thing or right, material or immaterial.
3. **Economic value:** it can be valued in money.
4. **Exclusiveness:** the owner can keep others out.
5. **Transferability:** it can usually be sold, gifted, mortgaged or inherited.
6. **Legal protection:** the state protects it through civil remedies and criminal law (theft, trespass).
7. **Subject to limits:** the law can tax, regulate or acquire it in the public interest.

### Property and ownership

The two words overlap. **Ownership** is the fullest right over a thing. **Property** is both the thing owned and the rights over it. Ownership is studied in Unit 11; possession, which is often the first step towards ownership, in Unit 10.

### Nepal: the legal meaning

The **Muluki Civil Code 2074** (National Civil Code), Section 251, says any cash, goods or work is property if it can be used or transacted, if its title can be transferred by sale or otherwise, or if any benefit can be derived from it. "Goods" means a physical property that can be bought or sold.

This fits the modern view: property is defined by **use, transfer and benefit**, not by having a physical body.

The **Constitution of Nepal 2015, Article 25**, Explanation: "property" means all type of movable and immovable property, and the word also includes intellectual property.

## 8.2 Importance of property

Property is one of the oldest and most important ideas in law. A large part of every legal system (contract, tort, succession, land, tax, company, criminal law) exists to create, protect or transfer property.

| Importance | Explanation |
|---|---|
| **Survival and security** | Food, shelter and land are property. Without secure property, people cannot plan their lives. |
| **Incentive to work** | People work and save when they know they will keep the fruits. Locke's labour theory rests on this. |
| **Liberty** | Property gives independence from others and from the state. Locke saw protecting property as a main purpose of government. |
| **Personality** | Hegel: property is the outward expression of a person's will and personality. |
| **Economic development** | Markets, credit (mortgage) and investment need clear property rights that can be traded. |
| **Social order and peace** | Clear rules on "mine and thine" prevent disputes and violence. Bentham: property and law are born together and die together. |
| **Family continuity** | Inheritance lets families pass on wealth across generations. |
| **State revenue** | Property and income from it are main sources of tax. Article 25(1) allows progressive tax on property and income. |
| **Basis of other legal rights** | Contract, mortgage, lease, trust and succession all deal with property. |
| **Social justice** | Because property can be unequal, laws on land reform, land ceilings and rights of tenants are used to share it more fairly. |

### The other side

Property is also a source of inequality and conflict. Proudhon called it theft; Marx saw private ownership of the means of production as the root of exploitation. Duguit argued that property is a **social function**, not an absolute right. Modern law therefore protects property but also limits it.

### Nepal angle

- Article 25 makes the right to property a **fundamental right**.
- Article 51(e) (state policies) calls for scientific land reform ending dual ownership of land, and for discouraging absentee land ownership.
- Article 40(5) says the state will provide land once to landless Dalits, according to law.
- Article 48(d) makes it a duty of citizens to protect and conserve public property.

## 8.3 Kinds of property

Textbooks classify property in several ways. Learn the tables.

### (a) Corporeal and incorporeal property (Salmond)

**Corporeal property** is the right of ownership in a **material object**: land, a house, a cow, a book.

**Incorporeal property** is every other proprietary right in rem. Salmond divides it into two groups:

| Group | Meaning | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| ***Jura in re aliena*** (rights in another's thing; encumbrances) | A right over a thing owned by someone else | Lease, servitude or easement (right of way over a neighbour's land), mortgage, usufruct |
| ***Jura in re propria*** (rights in one's own immaterial thing) | Ownership of something that has no body | Patent, copyright, trademark, goodwill of a business |

| Point | Corporeal | Incorporeal |
|---|---|---|
| Object | Material thing | A right, or an immaterial product |
| Can be touched | Yes | No |
| Examples | Land, car, gold | Copyright, patent, easement, mortgage, shares |
| Possession | Physical control | Continued use or enjoyment of the right |

**Important:** in strict theory, all property is a right and so is "incorporeal". We call a car "corporeal property" because the right is over a physical thing. Salmond explains that we identify the right of ownership with the thing itself, for convenience.

### (b) Movable and immovable property

This is the most important division in practice, and in Nepal's law.

| Point | Movable | Immovable |
|---|---|---|
| Meaning | Can be moved from place to place without losing its identity | Land and things permanently attached to land |
| Examples | Cash, gold, vehicles, furniture, shares, negotiable instruments, intellectual property | Land, buildings, mines, standing trees, crops in the land, natural water |
| Transfer | Often by delivery | Needs a registered deed (in Nepal, at the Land Revenue Office) |
| Adverse possession in Nepal | After 3 years | After 30 years (not for government, public, community or trust land) |
| Which law governs (conflict of laws) | Often law of the owner's domicile | Law of the place where the land lies |

**Nepal's law (Muluki Civil Code 2074):**

- **Section 252:** every property is treated as **either movable or immovable**, whether it is physical or non-physical, tangible or intangible.
- **Section 253(1):** immovable property includes:
    - a building, land or structure fixed to it;
    - goods permanently attached to a building or land;
    - a mine, stone or mineral embedded in land;
    - natural water, surface water and underground water;
    - a permanent building or structure made to float on a river, lake or pond;
    - a standing tree, plant or fruit tree, its fruit or flowers, or crops in the land;
    - a movable property attached to an immovable property.
- **Section 253(2):** if anything fixed to immovable property is detached, it becomes movable. A tree is immovable; the timber cut from it is movable.
- **Section 254:** movable property includes:
    - cash, and goods that can be transacted as cash or foreign currency;
    - gold, silver, jewellery, precious stones and ornaments;
    - other goods that can be moved;
    - bonds, securities, promissory notes, bills of exchange, letters of credit and other negotiable instruments;
    - **intellectual property**;
    - a right in security;
    - trade goodwill or franchise;
    - any property other than immovable property.

So in Nepal, intellectual property and goodwill are **movable**, even though they cannot be touched.

### (c) Tangible and intangible property

| Tangible | Intangible |
|---|---|
| Has a physical body; can be touched | Has no physical body |
| Land, house, car, gold | Copyright, patent, shares, debts, goodwill, digital assets |

This is close to corporeal and incorporeal. Section 252 of the Civil Code says tangible and intangible property are both classed as movable or immovable.

### (d) Real and personal property (English law)

| Real property | Personal property |
|---|---|
| Land and interests in land (freehold) | Everything else: chattels, money, shares, debts |
| Called "real" because in old English law the owner could recover the thing itself (*res*) by a "real action" | Could only be compensated in money by a "personal action" |

Leaseholds were an odd case: they concern land but were treated as personal property, called **chattels real**. Personal property is further divided into **choses in possession** (things you can physically hold, like a watch) and **choses in action** (rights you can only enforce by legal action, like a debt, shares or an insurance claim).

### (e) Private and public property

| Private property | Public property |
|---|---|
| Owned by a person, natural or legal | Held for the use of the public or owned by the state |
| Owner can use and transfer it, subject to law | Cannot normally be sold or registered in a private name |
| House, savings, salary, inherited land | Roads, temples, public wells, pasture land, rivers |

Roman law used a similar scheme: *res privatae* (private things), *res publicae* (public things like roads and rivers), *res communes* (things common to all, like air and the sea), and *res nullius* (things owned by nobody, like wild animals).

**Nepal: Section 255** classifies property by ownership and use into **seven kinds**:

| Kind | Section | Meaning in plain words |
|---|---|---|
| Private property (निजी) | 256 | Property of one person: earned by own skill or effort, received by donation, bequest or succession, lottery or gift, salary, pension, insurance, royalty, a woman's property from before marriage or from her parents' side at marriage (*daijo*), property given to her with an exclusive right (*pewa*), etc. The owner may deal with it exclusively (s. 256(2)). |
| Property in common (सगोलको) | 257 | Property of the family coparceners: ancestral property, property of coparceners that is not private, and earnings from common farming, trade or business. Property earned by husband or wife is common to both unless separated by law. |
| Joint property (संयुक्त) | 258 | Property owned by two or more persons, other than coparceners living in a joint family; shares are as in the deed, or equal if no deed |
| Community property | 301 | Land held by a community for its use, and structures or other property it owns |
| Public property | 300 | Property for public purposes, e.g. old roads and lands used since ancient times, wells, water conduits, ponds, pasture land, cremation and burial grounds, inns (*pauwa*), temples, monasteries, stupas, churches, *chautari*, fair and market grounds, private property given for public use. Ownership vests in the public body or the Government of Nepal (s. 300(2)). |
| Government property | 299 | Property owned or controlled by federal, provincial or local government: government buildings and land, roads and railways, forests, rivers, lakes, canals, barren land, mines and minerals, Himalayas, mountains, public gardens, and any property not owned by anyone else |
| Trust property | 314 onward | Property given to a trust to be managed by trustees for beneficiaries; a trust may be public or private (s. 315) |

Sections 304 to 306 protect government, public and community property: everyone must protect it, it cannot be registered in an individual's name, and nobody may occupy or cultivate such land.

### (f) Other classifications

| Basis | Kinds |
|---|---|
| Who it belongs to (Salmond's sense 3) | Proprietary rights **in rem** (against all, e.g. ownership) and **in personam** (against one person, e.g. a debt) |
| Use | Consumable (food, fuel) and non-consumable (land, a house) |
| Divisibility | Divisible (land, money) and indivisible (a painting, a horse) |
| Fungibility | Fungible (can be replaced by an equal: rice, money) and non-fungible (a specific house) |
| Origin | Ancestral (inherited) and self-acquired |
| Nature | Material (corporeal) and intellectual (patent, copyright, trademark, design) |

## Theories of property

Textbooks (Paton, Mahajan, Tripathi) discuss these theories under the question "what is the origin and justification of property?". They are not listed in the syllabus line, but they are standard and examinable.

### 1. Natural law theory (occupation theory)

- **Idea:** at first all things were common or owned by nobody. A person who first **took possession** of an ownerless thing became its owner.
- **Thinkers:** Grotius and Pufendorf. Grotius said property began through occupation, backed by an express or tacit agreement among people to respect each other's holdings.
- **Roman root:** *res nullius* (a thing without an owner) belongs to the first person who takes it, e.g. a wild animal caught by a hunter.
- **Criticism:** it explains how the first owner got the thing, not why later generations should accept that. It favours whoever came first. Most things today are already owned, so occupation explains little.

### 2. Labour theory

- **Idea:** a person owns their own body and labour. When they mix their labour with an unowned thing, the thing becomes theirs.
- **Thinker:** John Locke, *Second Treatise of Government* (1689), chapter V "Of Property". "Every man has a property in his own person."
- Locke added limits: a person may take only as much as they can use before it spoils, and must leave "enough, and as good" for others.
- **Influence:** Adam Smith and Ricardo built the labour theory of value on it; Marx later used it to argue that workers are robbed of the value they create.
- **Criticism:** much property is acquired without labour (inheritance, gifts, lotteries). Labour in a modern factory is shared, so whose labour made the product? It cannot say how much "mixing" is enough.

### 3. Metaphysical (personality) theory

- **Idea:** property is the outward expression of a person's **free will and personality**. A person needs things to exist as a free person.
- **Thinkers:** Kant and especially **Hegel** (*Philosophy of Right*, 1821). For Hegel, a person puts their will into a thing, and so it becomes theirs. Property is necessary for freedom.
- **Criticism:** abstract. It does not explain why property should be unequal, or why companies own property.

### 4. Historical theory

- **Idea:** property was not created at one moment by reason or contract. It **grew slowly** over history.
- **Thinkers:** Sir Henry Maine, *Ancient Law* (1861), and others of the historical school (Unit 2). Early property was mostly **collective**, held by the family, clan or village. Individual ownership developed later. Long possession turned into ownership.
- **Nepal fit:** the coparcenary family property (सगोलको सम्पत्ति) and community property (sections 257 and 301 of the Civil Code) show that collective forms still exist alongside private property.
- **Criticism:** describes how property grew, but does not justify it.

### 5. Psychological theory

- **Idea:** property comes from the human **acquisitive instinct**, the natural desire to collect and keep things.
- **Bentham's version:** "Property is nothing but a basis of expectation." People expect to gain from things they hold, and the law protects that expectation.
- **Criticism:** an instinct does not explain the legal rules on property, which differ greatly between societies.

### 6. Positive (legal) theory

- **Idea:** property exists only because the **state's law** creates and protects it.
- **Bentham:** "Property and law are born together, and die together. Before laws were made there was no property; take away laws, and property ceases."
- **Support in Nepal:** Section 266 of the Civil Code: a person's ownership is recognised when they acquire a right in property **in accordance with law**.
- **Criticism:** it ignores that customs of property existed before formal state law.

### 7. Sociological (social function) theory

- **Idea:** property is not an absolute individual right. It is a **social institution** that must serve society.
- **Thinkers:** **Leon Duguit**: property is a social function; the owner has a duty to use it for social good. **Roscoe Pound**: property is an interest that law balances against other social interests (social engineering, Unit 2). Harold Laski argued that property is justified only as far as it serves social welfare.
- **Effects:** land ceilings, land reform, compulsory acquisition for public purposes, taxation, zoning and tenancy laws.
- **Nepal fit:** Article 25(2) to (4) allow acquisition in the public interest and land reform; Article 51(e) aims at scientific land reform.
- **Criticism:** "social function" is vague and can be used to justify heavy state control.

### 8. Socialist (Marxist) theory

- **Idea:** private ownership of the **means of production** (land, factories) lets one class exploit another.
- **Thinkers:** **Proudhon**: "Property is theft" (1840). **Marx and Engels**, *Communist Manifesto* (1848): the theory of the Communists can be summed up in one sentence, "Abolition of private property". This means abolition of private ownership of the means of production, not of personal belongings.
- **Criticism:** states that abolished private property generally did not achieve prosperity, and many later restored private property in some form.
- **Nepal fit:** Article 4 calls Nepal "socialism-oriented", but Article 25 still protects private property. Nepal follows a mixed approach.

### Summary of theories

| Theory | Property comes from | Main thinkers |
|---|---|---|
| Natural law / occupation | First taking of ownerless things | Grotius, Pufendorf |
| Labour | Mixing one's labour with things | Locke |
| Metaphysical | Expression of free will and personality | Kant, Hegel |
| Historical | Slow growth from collective to individual | Maine |
| Psychological | Acquisitive instinct; expectation | Bentham |
| Positive (legal) | The law of the state | Bentham |
| Sociological | Social function and social interest | Duguit, Pound, Laski |
| Socialist | Class exploitation; should be abolished | Proudhon, Marx, Engels |

## Modes of acquiring property

Salmond lists **four modes** of acquiring property: **possession, prescription, agreement and inheritance**. Textbooks also group modes into **original** and **derivative**.

| Original acquisition | Derivative acquisition |
|---|---|
| The right is **new**; it does not come from an earlier owner | The right **passes** from an earlier owner |
| Taking an ownerless thing, creating something, prescription | Sale, gift, exchange, inheritance |
| Free of the earlier owner's burdens (usually) | Takes the right with its existing burdens: "no one can give a better title than he has" (*nemo dat quod non habet*) |

### 1. Possession

- **Ownerless things (*res nullius*):** a person who takes possession of a thing that belongs to nobody becomes its owner. Examples: wild fish caught in the open sea, wild animals hunted where hunting is allowed.
- **Things already owned:** possession by itself gives a title that is good against **everyone except the true owner** or a person with a better title. A finder of a lost ring can keep it against everyone except the person who lost it.
- **Creation:** a person who makes a new thing from their own materials, or creates an intellectual work (a song, a book, an invention), acquires property in it. Copyright arises from creating the work.
- **Accession:** the owner of the main thing becomes owner of what is added to it or produced by it: the calf of one's cow, crops from one's land, land added by a river's slow deposit.
- **Nepal:** Section 268 of the Civil Code: a person who lawfully holds a property with the intention to possess it has a **possessory right** over it. Sections 269 to 272 cover when such a right is acquired, who can acquire it, its rights and how it ends.

### 2. Prescription

**Prescription** means the effect of the **lapse of time** in creating or ending rights.

| Positive (acquisitive) prescription | Negative (extinctive) prescription |
|---|---|
| A person **gains** a right because they have used or possessed it for a long time | A person **loses** a right, or the remedy for it, because they did not use or enforce it in time |
| Adverse possession of land; acquiring an easement by long use | Limitation: a suit to recover a debt becomes time-barred |

**Why the law allows it:**

1. Long possession is good evidence of title; old documents get lost.
2. It brings certainty and ends old disputes.
3. "The law helps the vigilant, not those who sleep on their rights."
4. It encourages productive use of land.

**Nepal: Section 273 (adverse possessory right):**

- A person who has possessed someone else's **movable property for more than 3 years**, or **land for more than 30 years**, as if it were their own, has an adverse possessory right over it.
- They may then obtain **ownership** in their name (s. 273(2)).
- **Limits:**
    - no adverse possession over **government, public, community or trust land**, however long the possession (s. 273(1), proviso (1));
    - it does not apply if the property was possessed **secretly, forcibly or without the owner's knowledge** (s. 273(3));
    - a contract or other law with separate provisions prevails (s. 273(1), proviso (2));
    - the period counts only **from the commencement of the Civil Code** (s. 273(4)).

Section 373 also gives a **servitude of passage** (right of way): if the old passage to a house or land is destroyed by a disaster, the person who used it gets a reasonable passage to the public road, over the land with the shortest route. Servitudes are a form of incorporeal property.

### 3. Agreement (transfer by act of parties)

This is the most common mode today. One person transfers property to another by their own will **during their lifetime** (inter vivos).

- **Forms:** sale, exchange, gift, donation, mortgage, lease.
- Salmond distinguishes:
    - **assignment**: transfer of an existing right (selling your land);
    - **grant**: creating a new right over your property for someone else, e.g. a lease or a mortgage.
- **Rule:** a transferor cannot give a better title than they have (*nemo dat quod non habet*). Exceptions exist, e.g. a buyer in good faith of money or negotiable instruments.
- **Formalities:** transfer of land in Nepal needs a registered deed (राजीनामा) at the Land Revenue Office.

**Nepal (Muluki Civil Code 2074, Part 4, Chapter 11 "Transfer and Acquisition of Property"):**

- **Section 413:** property is transferred when a person sells, donates, gifts or otherwise transfers property in which they have right and ownership.
- **Section 416(1):** on transfer, the transferor's ownership ends and the transferee becomes owner from the date of transfer.
- **Section 406 (Chapter 10):** a **donation** (दान) is granting property free of cost to another person or for a religious, social, public or community purpose. A **gift** (बकस) is granting property free of cost as a reward, or out of family love or affection. Either may take effect immediately, after a period, or after the maker's death (testamentary donation or gift).
- **Section 419:** property in common cannot be transferred without the **written** consent of the coparceners (s. 420 lets the head of the family sell, for household needs, all common movable property and half the common immovable property without consent).

### 4. Inheritance (succession)

When a person dies, their property passes to others.

- **Intestate succession:** no will; property goes to heirs in an order fixed by law.
- **Testamentary succession:** by a will or testament.
- **What passes:** Salmond notes that **proprietary** rights generally survive death and pass to heirs, while **personal** rights (e.g. a right to reputation, a marriage) die with the person. The heirs take both assets and liabilities.

**Nepal (Muluki Civil Code 2074, Part 3, Chapter 11 "Succession"):**

- **Section 237:** succession opens on a person's death. "Succession" means devolution of liabilities and rights in regard to debt, wealth and property of the deceased upon the heirs according to law.
- **Sections 238-243:** heirs entitled to succession and their order of preference, including heirs who maintained and cared for the deceased.
- **Section 248:** if there is no heir, the Local Level receives the property.
- **Partition (अंशबण्डा), Part 3, Chapter 10:** family members become **coparceners** (s. 205) and are equally entitled to a partition share (s. 206). Partition is how family members get their share during life.

### Other modes mentioned in textbooks

- **Government grant:** e.g. land given by the state; under Article 40(5), land for landless Dalits.
- **Operation of law / court order:** e.g. property passed in bankruptcy or by a court decree.
- **Treasure trove and lost property:** usually vest in the state under special law.

## Property as a "bundle of rights"

### Plain meaning

Imagine ownership as a **bundle of sticks**. Each stick is a separate right: to use, to sell, to rent out, to mortgage, to exclude others, to leave by will. The owner holds the whole bundle. But the owner can take out one stick and give it to someone else, and still remain owner.

- Rent your house: the tenant takes the "right to possess and use" stick.
- Mortgage it: the bank takes a "security" stick.
- Grant a right of way: your neighbour takes a "passage" stick.

You are still the owner because you hold the rest of the bundle and the **residuary** right: when the lease ends, the stick comes back.

### Origin of the idea

- **Wesley Hohfeld** (1913 and 1917, *Yale Law Journal*) showed that a "right" is really several different legal relations: **claim-right, privilege (liberty), power and immunity**. Property is a bundle of such relations between the owner and many other people. A right "in rem" is really many separate rights against each person in the world.
- **The American legal realists** used the "bundle of rights" image to argue that property is not a natural, fixed thing but a set of rights the state can define and limit.
- **A.M. Honoré**, "Ownership" (in *Oxford Essays in Jurisprudence*, 1961), listed the standard **incidents of ownership** in mature legal systems.

### Honoré's eleven incidents of ownership

| No. | Incident | Plain meaning |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Right to possess | Physical control; to exclude others |
| 2 | Right to use | Personal use and enjoyment |
| 3 | Right to manage | Decide how and by whom it is used |
| 4 | Right to the income | Rent, interest, fruits |
| 5 | Right to the capital | Consume, waste, destroy, or transfer it |
| 6 | Right to security | Protection from being taken away (except by fair process) |
| 7 | Transmissibility | It can pass to heirs |
| 8 | Absence of term | Ownership does not end on a fixed date |
| 9 | Prohibition of harmful use | Duty not to use it to harm others |
| 10 | Liability to execution | It can be taken to pay the owner's debts |
| 11 | Residuary character | Rights return to the owner when lesser rights (lease, mortgage) end |

Honoré said no single incident is necessary; ownership is the **greatest possible interest** in a thing in a mature legal system.

### Why the "bundle" idea is useful

1. **It explains split ownership.** Landlord and tenant, mortgagor and mortgagee, trustee and beneficiary each hold some sticks.
2. **It explains incorporeal property.** A lease or easement is one stick separated from the bundle.
3. **It explains regulation.** The state can remove or limit sticks (land ceiling, zoning, tax, acquisition) without abolishing property.
4. **It fits the law.** Statutes list the owner's rights one by one.

### Nepal: the bundle in the statute

**Section 267 of the Muluki Civil Code 2074** lists the rights of an owner, subject to law. It reads almost like a bundle of rights:

1. to use the property;
2. to sell or otherwise transfer title to another person;
3. to mortgage or pledge it;
4. to deal in it;
5. to take benefits from it in any manner;
6. to build a structure, wall or fence, mark a boundary, change its form or otherwise protect it;
7. to use the land, the part below its surface and goods in it, and the sky above it;
8. to destroy or dispose of it;
9. to bring legal action to acquire or secure it.

**Article 25(1)** of the Constitution similarly protects the right of every citizen, subject to law, to acquire, enjoy, own, sell, have professional gains from, and otherwise deal with and dispose of property.

Other Civil Code chapters show the sticks being separated: **usufruct** (Part 4, Chapter 7: right to use and enjoy another's property), **servitude** (s. 368 onward), **mortgage** (Chapter 12), **house rent** (s. 383 onward) and **trust** (Chapter 6).

### Criticism of the bundle idea

- **Loses the "thing".** Critics such as J.E. Penner argue that property is basically a right to a **thing**, centred on the right to exclude. Calling it a loose bundle hides this core.
- **No limit.** If property is just any bundle, it is hard to say when someone stops being an owner.
- **Encourages over-regulation**, some say, because each stick looks easy to remove.
- **Still useful:** despite criticism, the image remains the standard way to explain divided rights in property, and Nepal's s. 267 follows it.

??? example "Q1. Explain the various ways of acquiring property. Examine the concept of property as a 'bundle of rights'. (2078 Q5, 20 marks)"
    **Introduction**

    Property is a central concept of law. In ordinary speech it means a thing that someone owns. In law it means the rights a person has over a thing, which the law protects against others. Blackstone described property as "that sole and despotic dominion which one man claims and exercises over the external things of the world, in total exclusion of the right of any other individual in the universe". Bentham said that "property is nothing but a basis of expectation", meaning the expectation of advantages from a thing, secured by law. Austin described ownership as a right "indefinite in point of user, unrestricted in point of disposition, and unlimited in point of duration".

    Salmond used the word in four senses: all legal rights of a person; proprietary rights only; proprietary rights in rem only; and, narrowest, the ownership of material things. In Nepal, Section 251 of the Muluki Civil Code 2074 treats any cash, goods or work as property if it can be used or transacted, its title can be transferred, or some benefit can be derived from it.

    This answer first explains the ways of acquiring property and then examines the idea of property as a "bundle of rights".

    **Part A: Ways of acquiring property**

    Acquisition of property is either **original** or **derivative**. In original acquisition, a new right arises that does not come from any earlier owner, for example taking an ownerless thing. In derivative acquisition, an existing right passes from one person to another, for example by sale or inheritance. In derivative acquisition the rule is *nemo dat quod non habet*: no one can give a better title than he has.

    Salmond identifies four modes of acquisition: possession, prescription, agreement and inheritance.

    **1. Possession**

    Possession is the oldest mode of acquiring property. If a person takes possession of a thing that belongs to nobody (*res nullius*), such as a wild fish in the open sea, he becomes its owner. This was the basis of the occupation theory of Grotius and Pufendorf.

    If the thing already has an owner, possession still gives a title, but it is good against everyone except the true owner or a person with a better title. A finder of lost goods can keep them against everyone except the person who lost them.

    Possession also includes **creation** and **accession**. A person who makes a new thing, or who creates a book, song or invention, acquires property in it. The owner of a cow owns its calf, and the owner of land owns its crops, by accession.

    In Nepal, Section 268 of the Muluki Civil Code 2074 provides that a person who lawfully holds property with the intention to possess it has a possessory right over it.

    **2. Prescription**

    Prescription is the effect of lapse of time in creating or ending rights. It is of two kinds. **Positive or acquisitive prescription** gives a right to a person who has possessed or used a thing for a long time, such as adverse possession or an easement by long use. **Negative or extinctive prescription** destroys a right, or the remedy for it, if the owner does not enforce it within the time fixed by law, as with limitation periods.

    The law allows prescription because long possession is good evidence of title, because disputes must end, and because the law helps the vigilant and not those who sleep on their rights.

    In Nepal, Section 273 of the Civil Code provides for **adverse possessory right**. A person who has possessed another's movable property for more than three years, or land for more than thirty years, as if it were his own, gets an adverse possessory right and may obtain ownership in his own name. No such right arises over government, public, community or trust land. It does not apply if the possession was secret, forcible or without the owner's knowledge. The period is counted from the commencement of the Code.

    **3. Agreement**

    Agreement is the most common mode today. The owner transfers property to another during his lifetime by sale, exchange, gift, donation, mortgage or lease. Salmond separates **assignment**, which transfers an existing right, from **grant**, which creates a new right such as a lease or mortgage over one's own property.

    In Nepal, Section 413 of the Civil Code says property is transferred when a person sells, donates, gifts or otherwise transfers property in which he has right and ownership. Under Section 416(1), the transferor's ownership ends and the transferee becomes owner from the date of transfer. Section 406 defines donation and gift: granting property free of cost, either for a religious, social or public purpose or to another person, or as a reward or out of love and affection. Land must be transferred by a registered deed.

    **4. Inheritance**

    Inheritance is acquisition on the death of the owner. It may be intestate, according to an order of heirs fixed by law, or testamentary, according to a will. Proprietary rights generally pass to heirs, while purely personal rights end with death. The heirs receive both the assets and the liabilities.

    In Nepal, Section 237 of the Civil Code provides that succession opens on a person's death. Succession means the devolution of rights and liabilities relating to the debt, wealth and property of the deceased upon his heirs. Sections 238 to 243 fix the heirs and their order of preference, and Section 248 gives the property to the Local Level if there is no heir. Members of a family also acquire property as coparceners through partition (Sections 205 and 206).

    **Other modes**

    Property may also be acquired by government grant, by operation of law, or by court order, for example in bankruptcy.

    **Part B: Property as a "bundle of rights"**

    **Meaning**

    The "bundle of rights" theory says that property is not one single right but a collection of separate rights over a thing. The image is a bundle of sticks. Each stick is a right: to possess, to use, to sell, to rent, to mortgage, to exclude others, to leave by will. An owner holds the full bundle. He may give one or more sticks to others and still remain owner. When a landlord leases a house, the tenant takes the right to possess and use it. When the owner mortgages it, the bank takes a security right. When the lease ends, those sticks return to the owner.

    **Origin**

    Wesley Hohfeld, in articles in the *Yale Law Journal* (1913 and 1917), showed that a "right" can mean four different things: a claim, a privilege, a power or an immunity. Property is therefore a bundle of many such legal relations between the owner and other people. The American legal realists used the bundle image to argue that property is a set of rights defined and limited by the state.

    **Honoré's incidents of ownership**

    A.M. Honoré, in his essay "Ownership" (1961), listed eleven standard incidents of ownership: the right to possess, the right to use, the right to manage, the right to the income, the right to the capital, the right to security, transmissibility, absence of term, prohibition of harmful use, liability to execution, and residuary character. No single incident is essential. Ownership is the greatest possible interest in a thing that a mature legal system recognises.

    **Value of the concept**

    1. It explains how ownership can be split, as between landlord and tenant, mortgagor and mortgagee, or trustee and beneficiary.
    2. It explains incorporeal property such as leases and easements, which are separated sticks.
    3. It explains how the state can regulate property, for example by land ceilings, zoning or acquisition, without abolishing it.

    **Nepal's position**

    Nepal's law follows the bundle idea closely. Section 267 of the Muluki Civil Code 2074 lists the rights of an owner, subject to law: to use the property; to sell or transfer title; to mortgage or pledge it; to deal in it; to take benefits from it; to build structures, fences and boundaries and change its form; to use the land below the surface and the sky above it; to destroy or dispose of it; and to bring legal action to acquire or protect it. The Code also lets these rights be separated, through usufruct, servitude, mortgage, tenancy and trust.

    Article 25(1) of the Constitution of Nepal guarantees every citizen, subject to law, the right to acquire, enjoy, own, sell, have professional gains from, and otherwise deal with and dispose of property. Article 25(2) to (4) allow the state to acquire property in the public interest with compensation and to carry out land reform. Each of these provisions removes or limits one stick, which shows that the right is a bundle and not an absolute single right.

    **Critical view**

    The bundle theory is useful but has critics. Writers such as J.E. Penner argue that it loses sight of the thing itself and of the core right to exclude others. If property is any bundle of rights, it is hard to say when a person stops being an owner. Some also say it makes property look easy to take apart by regulation. On the modes of acquisition, prescription can seem unfair to a true owner, which is why Nepal's Section 273 excludes secret or forcible possession and public land.

    **Conclusion**

    Property may be acquired originally, by possession, creation, accession or prescription, or derivatively, by agreement or inheritance. Nepal's Muluki Civil Code 2074 regulates each mode. Modern law sees property as a bundle of rights rather than one absolute power. This view explains divided ownership and state regulation, and it is reflected in Section 267 of the Civil Code and Article 25 of the Constitution of Nepal.

??? example "Q2. Define property. Explain its kinds and importance. (likely question, 20 marks)"
    **Introduction**

    In everyday speech, property means a thing that belongs to someone. In law, property means the rights a person has over a thing, which the law protects against others. A house is called property because the owner has legal rights to use it, keep others out, sell it and leave it to heirs.

    Jurists have defined property in different ways. Blackstone called it "that sole and despotic dominion which one man claims and exercises over the external things of the world, in total exclusion of the right of any other individual in the universe". Bentham said, "property is nothing but a basis of expectation", the expectation of advantages from a thing, secured by law. Austin described ownership as a right "indefinite in point of user, unrestricted in point of disposition, and unlimited in point of duration".

    **Salmond's four senses**

    Salmond explained that property is used in four senses:

    1. In the widest sense, it means **all the legal rights** of a person, including life, liberty and reputation. Locke used it in this sense.
    2. In a narrower sense, it means only **proprietary rights**, that is, rights with money value (a person's estate), as opposed to personal rights (status).
    3. Narrower still, it means only **proprietary rights in rem**, good against the whole world, and excludes rights in personam such as debts.
    4. In the narrowest sense, it means only the **ownership of material things**, that is, corporeal property.

    **Definition in Nepal**

    Section 251 of the Muluki Civil Code 2074 treats any cash, goods or work as property if it can be used or transacted, its title can be transferred by sale or otherwise, or any benefit can be derived from it. The Explanation to Article 25 of the Constitution of Nepal says that property means all types of movable and immovable property and includes intellectual property.

    **Kinds of property**

    **1. Corporeal and incorporeal.** Corporeal property is the ownership of material things such as land, a car or gold. Incorporeal property, according to Salmond, is every other proprietary right in rem. It has two forms. *Jura in re aliena* are rights over a thing owned by another, such as a lease, easement or mortgage. *Jura in re propria* are rights over one's own immaterial things, such as patents, copyrights, trademarks and goodwill.

    **2. Movable and immovable.** Immovable property is land and anything permanently attached to it. Movable property is everything that can be moved without losing its identity. In Nepal, Section 252 of the Civil Code says every property, tangible or intangible, is either movable or immovable. Section 253 lists immovable property: land, buildings and structures fixed to land, goods permanently attached to land, mines and minerals, natural, surface and underground water, permanent floating structures, standing trees, fruits and crops, and movables attached to immovables. Anything detached becomes movable. Section 254 lists movable property: cash, gold and jewellery, other movable goods, negotiable instruments, intellectual property, security rights, goodwill and franchise, and all property that is not immovable.

    **3. Tangible and intangible.** Tangible property has a physical body, like a house. Intangible property has none, like shares, copyright or a debt.

    **4. Real and personal.** In English law, real property means land and interests in it, which could be recovered by a "real action". Personal property means chattels and other things for which only money compensation was available. Personal property is further divided into choses in possession, such as a watch, and choses in action, such as a debt or shares.

    **5. Private and public.** Private property belongs to an individual or legal person. Public property is held for the use of the public or by the state. Section 255 of Nepal's Civil Code classifies property by ownership and use into seven kinds: private property (Section 256), property in common of coparceners (Section 257), joint property (Section 258), community property (Section 301), public property (Section 300), government property (Section 299) and trust property. Public property includes old roads, wells, ponds, pasture land, cremation grounds, temples and *chautari*. Government property includes government buildings and land, roads, forests, rivers, mines and the Himalayas. Sections 304 to 306 prohibit registering such property in an individual's name or occupying it.

    **6. Other kinds.** Property may also be consumable or non-consumable, fungible or non-fungible, divisible or indivisible, and ancestral or self-acquired.

    **Importance of property**

    1. **Basic needs and security.** Food, shelter and land are property. Secure property lets people plan their lives.
    2. **Incentive to work.** People work and save when they can keep the fruits of their labour, as Locke argued.
    3. **Liberty and personality.** Property gives independence. Hegel saw it as the outward expression of a person's will and personality.
    4. **Economic development.** Trade, credit and investment depend on clear property rights that can be sold or mortgaged.
    5. **Social order.** Clear rules on "mine and thine" reduce conflict. Bentham said that property and law are born together and die together.
    6. **Family continuity.** Inheritance and partition keep wealth within families.
    7. **State revenue.** Property and income from it are a major tax base. Article 25(1) permits progressive taxation of property and income.
    8. **Basis of many branches of law.** Contract, tort, trust, succession, land and company law all deal with property.

    **Nepal's position**

    Article 25 of the Constitution of Nepal makes the right to property a fundamental right. Every citizen, subject to law, may acquire, enjoy, own, sell, have professional gains from, and otherwise deal with and dispose of property. The state may not acquire, requisition or encumber property except in the public interest, and the basis of compensation is set by Act. Land reform and land regulation are allowed under Article 25(4), and Article 51(e) commits the state to scientific land reform. Article 48(d) makes it a duty of citizens to protect public property. The Muluki Civil Code 2074, Part 4, gives detailed rules on the kinds, ownership, use, transfer and protection of property.

    **Critical view**

    Property is important but not absolute. Proudhon called property theft, and Marx saw private ownership of the means of production as the source of class exploitation. Duguit argued that property is a social function that must serve society. These criticisms explain why modern law, including Nepal's, protects property but also taxes, regulates and acquires it for public purposes. Unequal land ownership remains a social problem in Nepal, which is why land reform is a constitutional policy.

    **Conclusion**

    Property, in law, is a set of rights of economic value over things, protected against others. It can be corporeal or incorporeal, movable or immovable, tangible or intangible, real or personal, and private or public. Nepal's Civil Code adopts the movable and immovable division and a seven-fold classification by ownership. Property is important for individual freedom, economic growth and social order, but it must be balanced with the public interest. Nepal's Constitution strikes this balance in Article 25.

??? example "Q3. Discuss the theories of property. Explain the right to property under the Constitution of Nepal. (likely question, 20 marks)"
    **Introduction**

    Property means the rights a person has over a thing, protected by law against others. Blackstone described it as "that sole and despotic dominion which one man claims and exercises over the external things of the world, in total exclusion of the right of any other individual in the universe". Bentham called it "nothing but a basis of expectation". Salmond used the term in four senses, from all legal rights of a person to only the ownership of material things.

    Two questions have long been asked about property: how did it begin, and why should the law protect it? The answers given by jurists are called the theories of property.

    **1. Natural law theory (occupation theory)**

    According to this theory, all things were once common or owned by nobody. The first person to take possession of an ownerless thing became its owner. Grotius and Pufendorf held this view and added that people agreed, expressly or tacitly, to respect each other's holdings. It rests on the Roman rule that *res nullius* belongs to the first taker.

    *Criticism:* it explains only how the first owner got the thing, not why everyone else must respect it. Today, nearly everything is already owned.

    **2. Labour theory**

    John Locke, in his *Second Treatise of Government* (1689), argued that "every man has a property in his own person". When a person mixes his labour with something from nature, it becomes his. Locke set limits: a person may take only what he can use before it spoils, and must leave enough and as good for others. Adam Smith and Ricardo built the labour theory of value on this, and Marx later used it against capitalism.

    *Criticism:* much property is acquired without labour, through inheritance, gift or chance. In modern production many people work together, so it is unclear whose labour created the product.

    **3. Metaphysical (personality) theory**

    Kant and Hegel saw property as the expression of a person's free will. Hegel, in the *Philosophy of Right* (1821), argued that a person puts his will into a thing, and property is necessary for a person to exist as free.

    *Criticism:* it is abstract and cannot explain unequal holdings or ownership by companies.

    **4. Historical theory**

    The historical school, especially Sir Henry Maine in *Ancient Law* (1861), showed that property grew slowly over time. Early property was largely collective, held by the family, clan or village. Individual ownership developed later, and long possession gradually became ownership.

    *Criticism:* it describes the growth of property but does not justify it.

    **5. Psychological theory**

    This theory traces property to the human instinct to acquire and keep things. Bentham's view that property is "a basis of expectation" fits here: the law protects a person's expectation of benefit from a thing.

    *Criticism:* instinct alone cannot explain the very different property rules in different societies.

    **6. Positive (legal) theory**

    This theory says property exists only because the law creates it. Bentham wrote that "property and law are born together, and die together. Before laws were made there was no property; take away laws, and property ceases."

    *Criticism:* customary forms of property existed before formal state law.

    **7. Sociological theory**

    Leon Duguit argued that property is not an absolute individual right but a social function; the owner must use it for the good of society. Roscoe Pound treated property as one of the interests that law must balance through social engineering. Laski held that property is justified only as far as it serves social welfare. This theory supports land ceilings, land reform, taxation and compulsory acquisition.

    *Criticism:* "social function" is vague and can be used to justify excessive state control.

    **8. Socialist theory**

    Proudhon declared that "property is theft" (1840). Marx and Engels, in the *Communist Manifesto* (1848), summed up their theory in the phrase "abolition of private property", meaning private ownership of the means of production. They saw such property as the basis of class exploitation.

    *Criticism:* socialist states that abolished private property mostly failed to achieve prosperity, and many later reintroduced private property.

    **Assessment of the theories**

    No single theory fully explains property. Occupation and labour explain how property begins; the personality theory explains its link with freedom; the historical theory shows how it grew; the positive theory shows that its form depends on law; and the sociological and socialist theories show its social costs and limits. Modern legal systems combine these ideas: they protect private property but subject it to the public interest.

    **Right to property under the Constitution of Nepal**

    The Constitution of Nepal 2015 guarantees the right to property as a fundamental right in **Article 25**.

    1. **Clause (1):** every citizen shall, subject to law, have the right to acquire, enjoy, own, sell, have professional gains from, and otherwise deal with and dispose of property. The proviso allows the state to levy tax on a person's property and income according to the concept of progressive taxation.
    2. **Explanation:** "property" means all types of movable and immovable property, and the word also includes intellectual property.
    3. **Clause (2):** the state shall not, except in the public interest, acquire, requisition or create any encumbrance on the property of any person. This protection does not apply to property acquired illegally.
    4. **Clause (3):** the basis of compensation and the procedure for acquiring property are to be set by Act.
    5. **Clause (4):** clauses (2) and (3) do not prevent the state from carrying out land reform, management and regulation by law, for purposes such as increasing productivity, modernising agriculture, protecting the environment, and planned housing and urban development.
    6. **Clause (5):** property acquired for one public purpose may be used for another public purpose.

    Related provisions support this right. Article 40(5) provides land once to landless Dalits. Article 42(4) gives farmers access to land for agriculture according to law. Article 48(d) makes it a citizen's duty to protect public property. Article 51(e) directs the state to carry out scientific land reform by ending dual ownership of land and to discourage absentee land ownership.

    **Link between the theories and Article 25**

    Article 25(1) reflects the natural, labour and personality theories: it protects a citizen's freedom to acquire and use property. The words "subject to law" reflect the positive theory, since the right exists within the limits set by law. Clauses (2) to (5), and the land reform policy, reflect the sociological theory: property serves a social function and may be acquired or regulated in the public interest. Article 4, which describes Nepal as "socialism-oriented", shows the influence of socialist ideas, but Nepal does not abolish private property.

    The Muluki Civil Code 2074 gives effect to this. Section 266 recognises ownership acquired in accordance with law, and Section 267 lists the owner's rights subject to law.

    **Critical view**

    Article 25 gives the right only to **citizens**, not to all persons. The right is "subject to law", so its strength depends on ordinary legislation. Compensation is left to an Act and is not expressly required to be fair or market-based. On the other hand, these limits allow land reform, which is needed in a country with unequal landholding.

    **Conclusion**

    Theories of property explain its origin and justify its protection from different angles, from Locke's labour to Duguit's social function. None is complete alone. Nepal's Constitution combines them: it protects the citizen's right to property as a fundamental right while allowing taxation, acquisition in the public interest and land reform. Property in Nepal is therefore a protected but limited right.

## One-minute revision

- **Meaning:** property = rights of economic value over things, protected against others. Blackstone "sole and despotic dominion"; Bentham "basis of expectation"; Salmond's four senses. Nepal: Civil Code s. 251; Constitution Art. 25 Explanation includes intellectual property.
- **Importance:** security, incentive, liberty (Locke), personality (Hegel), economy, social order, inheritance, tax. Limits: Proudhon, Marx, Duguit.
- **Kinds:** corporeal / incorporeal (*jura in re aliena*, *jura in re propria*); movable / immovable (s. 252-254); tangible / intangible; real / personal; private / public (s. 255: seven kinds; s. 299-301 government, public, community).
- **Theories:** occupation (Grotius), labour (Locke), personality (Hegel), historical (Maine), psychological and positive (Bentham), sociological (Duguit, Pound), socialist (Proudhon, Marx).
- **Modes (Salmond):** possession, prescription, agreement, inheritance. Original vs derivative; *nemo dat*. Nepal: s. 268, 273 (3/30 years), 406, 413, 416, 237.
- **Bundle of rights:** Hohfeld, realists, Honoré's eleven incidents. Nepal s. 267 lists owner's rights; Art. 25(1).
- **Article 25:** (1) acquire, enjoy, own, sell, dispose; progressive tax. (2) acquisition only in public interest. (3) compensation by Act. (4) land reform. (5) reuse for another public purpose.
