# Unit 10: Possession

!!! abstract "In one line"
    Possession means control of a thing together with the intention to control it. The law protects it, uses it as evidence of ownership, and in some cases turns long possession into ownership.

## Why it matters for the exam

- **Asked as a full 20-mark question:** 2078 Q6 "Explain the various forms of possession."
- Possession is linked to Unit 11 (Ownership). 2080 Q6 asked about classification of ownership and Nepal's law on acquisition, continuation and termination of ownership. A "possession vs ownership" question can come from either unit.
- Likely short notes: animus possidendi, corpus possessionis, constructive possession, mediate possession, adverse possession, possessory remedies.
- Nepal's angle is easy to score: the Muluki Civil Code 2074 has a whole chapter on possession (sections 266 to 275).

## Start here: what does "possess" mean?

You lend your bicycle to a friend for a week. Who has it?

- Your friend **holds** it. He rides it and keeps it at his house.
- You still **own** it. You can ask for it back.
- Who **possesses** it? The law says your friend has immediate possession, and you keep a kind of possession through him (mediate possession).

Now suppose a thief takes the bicycle from your friend. The thief has no right at all. But he has the bicycle under his control and he means to keep it. In law, the thief is in possession. This shows the key point: **possession is a matter of fact, not of right**. Ownership is a matter of right.

Three questions run through this unit:

1. **What makes possession?** Physical control (corpus) plus intention (animus).
2. **What kinds are there?** In law and in fact, mediate and immediate, corporeal and incorporeal, constructive, concurrent, adverse.
3. **Why does the law protect it?** Because protecting possession keeps peace and usually protects owners too.

## 10.1 Meaning and importance of possession

### Meaning

In plain words, a person possesses a thing when he controls it and means to keep others away from it.

Possession is notoriously hard to define. Salmond wrote that in the whole range of legal theory there is no conception more difficult than that of possession. Different jurists stress different parts of it.

| Jurist | Definition or view |
|---|---|
| **Salmond** | Possession of a material object is "the continuing exercise of a claim to the exclusive use of it" |
| **Pollock** (with R.S. Wright, *An Essay on Possession in the Common Law*, 1888) | Possession is physical control. A person possesses a thing when he has apparent control over it and can exclude others from using it. Pollock separates possession in fact from possession in law |
| **Savigny** (*Das Recht des Besitzes*, 1803) | Possession = physical power over the thing (corpus) + intention to hold it as owner (animus domini) |
| **Ihering** | Possession is the visible, outward form of ownership. A person possesses when he deals with the thing as an owner normally would |
| **Holmes** (*The Common Law*, 1881) | Possession needs a certain physical relation to the object and an intention to exclude others from it |

**Possession is a fact with legal results.** Salmond calls it *de facto* (in fact), while ownership is *de jure* (in law). A thief has possession; he never has ownership.

### Importance

Possession matters in almost every branch of law.

1. **Evidence of ownership.** The law presumes that the possessor is the owner until someone proves otherwise. Hence the saying "possession is nine points of the law".
2. **Protection against others.** A possessor can sue anyone who disturbs him, except a person with a better title. In *Armory v Delamirie* (1722), a chimney sweep's boy found a jewel. A goldsmith kept the stones. The court held that the finder had a good title against everyone except the true owner.
3. **A way of acquiring ownership.** Taking a thing that belongs to no one (*res nullius*), such as a wild bird or fish, makes you its owner. Long adverse possession can also turn into ownership.
4. **Transfer of property.** Many transfers of movable goods are completed by delivery of possession. A sale or gift of goods is often completed by handing them over.
5. **Criminal law.** Theft is defined around possession: dishonestly taking a thing out of another's possession.
6. **Bailment and contracts.** A bailee (e.g. a repairer) holds possession of goods for a purpose and must return them.
7. **Limitation.** Time limits for recovering property often run from the date possession was lost.

!!! note "Nepal"
    Section 268 of the Muluki Civil Code 2074 says: if a person holds a property lawfully with the intention to possess it, the person is deemed to have the possessory right (भोगाधिकार) over it. This is the corpus-plus-animus idea written into Nepali law.

## 10.2 Constituent elements: animus possidendi and corpus possessionis

All major theories agree that possession has two elements:

| Element | Latin | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| Mental | **animus possidendi** | The intention to possess: to keep the thing and exclude others |
| Physical | **corpus possessionis** | Actual control over the thing |

Both must exist together. Intention alone is a wish. Control without intention is not possession. A sleeping man does not possess a coin slipped into his pocket until he becomes aware of it and means to keep it (a common textbook example).

### Animus possidendi (the mental element)

Salmond describes it as the intention to appropriate to oneself the exclusive use of the thing. Its features, as Salmond explains them:

1. **It need not be a claim of right.** A thief has animus possidendi, though he knows the thing is not his.
2. **It need not be an intention to own.** A tenant or borrower intends to possess, though he knows someone else owns the thing. (This is where Salmond differs from Savigny.)
3. **It need not be specific.** A person may intend to possess all the contents of his letterbox without knowing what they are. A man possesses the fish in his net before he counts them.
4. **It need not be on one's own behalf.** A servant or agent may hold for his master.
5. **It must be exclusive.** It is an intention to exclude others, including the owner in some cases (e.g. a pledgee who keeps goods until a loan is repaid).

### Corpus possessionis (the physical element)

Corpus does not mean holding the thing in your hand at every moment. It means a relationship of control. Salmond explains it as two relations:

1. **Relation to other people.** The possessor must have reasonable assurance that others will not interfere. This may come from physical power, the protection of the law, or the general respect people show for another's belongings. A farmer's crops in an open field are possessed though no one guards them.
2. **Relation to the thing.** The possessor must be able to use the thing when he wants. Things in your locked house are possessed even when you are away.

What counts as control depends on the nature of the thing. A purse is controlled by holding it. A field is controlled by fencing, cultivating or using it. A car left parked on the street remains in the owner's possession.

### The finder cases (corpus and animus in practice)

| Case | Facts | Held |
|---|---|---|
| *Armory v Delamirie* (1722) | A chimney sweep's boy found a jewel | Finder's title good against all but the true owner |
| *Bridges v Hawkesworth* (1851) | Banknotes found on the floor of the public part of a shop | Finder, not shopkeeper, entitled; the shopkeeper had no intention to control things he did not know of in the public area |
| *Elwes v Brigg Gas Co* (1886) | An ancient boat buried in leased land | Landowner possessed it, though he did not know of it |
| *South Staffordshire Water Co v Sharman* (1896) | Rings found at the bottom of a pool by a worker cleaning it | Landowner entitled; he controlled the pool and what was in it |
| *Hannah v Peel* (1945) | A brooch found in a house the owner had never lived in | Finder entitled; the owner had never occupied the house |
| *Parker v British Airways Board* (1982) | A bracelet found in an airport executive lounge | Finder entitled; the airline had not shown a clear intention to control lost items |

**Rule from these cases:** things in or attached to land are possessed by the occupier. Things lying loose on land open to the public go to the finder, unless the occupier clearly showed an intention to control such things.

### Theories of possession

This is the heart of most exam answers. Learn the four theories in this order.

#### 1. Savigny's theory (subjective theory)

Friedrich Carl von Savigny, the founder of the historical school, wrote *Das Recht des Besitzes* (The Law of Possession) in 1803. He based it on Roman law.

- **Corpus:** the physical power to deal with the thing and to exclude others. It need not be actual touch, but there must be a present physical power over it.
- **Animus domini:** the intention to hold the thing **as owner**. Not merely to hold it, but to hold it as one's own.
- So a tenant, borrower or agent has only **detention** (custody), not possession, because each knows another person is the owner.
- Roman law gave possession to some people who lacked animus domini: the pledgee, the sequester (a stakeholder holding disputed property), the holder at will (*precarium*) and the long-term lessee (*emphyteuta*). Savigny explained these as **derivative possession**: the owner's possession passed to them.
- **Why protect possession?** Savigny said disturbing possession is an act of violence against the person. The law protects possession to protect the person and keep peace.

**Criticism of Savigny:**

1. Animus domini is too narrow. Modern law (English, Indian, Nepali) treats tenants, bailees and pledgees as possessors.
2. The idea of "derivative possession" is an artificial patch for exceptions his theory cannot explain.
3. It is based on Roman law and does not fit modern systems.
4. Requiring present physical power is too strict. Timber left in a forest or crops in an unguarded field are still possessed.

#### 2. Ihering's theory (objective theory)

Rudolf von Ihering attacked Savigny's theory, especially in his work on the will to possess (*Der Besitzwille*, 1889).

- **Possession is the outward form of ownership.** A person is in possession when he stands to the thing as an owner usually does. Ihering called possession the visible realisation of ownership.
- **Corpus is not physical power.** It is the outward, economic relation a normal owner has with that kind of thing. Building materials left on a construction site are possessed by the builder, though no one guards them. A wallet dropped on the road is not, because owners do not keep wallets on roads.
- **Animus is not a separate element.** It is just the awareness that goes with corpus. If corpus exists, animus is presumed. The person who denies possession must prove that the law refuses it in that case.
- So a tenant or borrower **is** a possessor, unless the law expressly denies it.
- **Why protect possession?** Possession is the outwork, or first line of defence, of ownership. In most cases the possessor is the owner. Protecting possession is a quick and easy way to protect owners, without making them prove title every time.

**Criticism of Ihering:**

1. Making animus a mere shadow of corpus underrates intention. Intention matters in finder cases and in theft.
2. Protecting possession also protects thieves and wrongdoers, not only owners.
3. The test "as an owner would" is vague.

#### 3. Salmond's theory

Sir John Salmond (*Jurisprudence*, first published 1902) gave the theory most used in common-law countries, including Nepal's law schools.

- Possession is "the continuing exercise of a claim to the exclusive use" of a thing.
- It has two elements: **corpus possessionis** and **animus possidendi**.
- Animus is the intention to exclude others, **not** the intention to own. So tenants, bailees, pledgees and even thieves possess.
- Corpus means the relation to other people (security against interference) and the relation to the thing (power to use it).
- Salmond also explains **incorporeal possession**: the possession of a right, such as a right of way.

**Criticism:** Salmond's theory is practical but it is descriptive. It explains how courts decide cases rather than giving one principle. Some jurists say the word "possession" has no single meaning; it means different things in different branches of law.

#### 4. Pollock's theory

Sir Frederick Pollock, with R.S. Wright, wrote *An Essay on Possession in the Common Law* (1888).

- Possession is **physical control** (possession in fact, or *de facto* control).
- Pollock gives less weight to animus. The test is effective control and the power to exclude others.
- He separates **possession in fact** (physical control) from **possession in law** (what the law treats as possession). The two usually go together but not always.
- He also recognises **the right to possess**, which an owner may have even when someone else has the thing.

**Criticism:** physical control alone cannot explain constructive possession, mediate possession, or the finder cases, where intention decides the result.

#### Other views (mention in one line each)

- **Kant and Hegel:** possession expresses the will of the person over a thing. To disturb it is to disturb the person's freedom.
- **Holmes:** possession needs physical relation and an intent to exclude others; the law protects it because people instinctively resist losing what they hold.
- **Paton:** there is no single concept of possession. Each legal system decides, for practical reasons, who should be treated as a possessor.

### The four theories side by side

| Point | Savigny | Ihering | Salmond | Pollock |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Name | Subjective theory | Objective theory | Common-law (analytical) theory | Physical control theory |
| Corpus | Physical power to deal with the thing | Outward relation like an owner's | Relation to others and to the thing | Effective physical control |
| Animus | Animus domini (intention to own) | Not separate; follows corpus | Animus possidendi (intention to exclude) | Less important |
| Tenant / bailee | Not a possessor (only detention) | Possessor | Possessor | Possessor |
| Why protect | Protect the person, keep peace | Protect ownership | Practical: peace, evidence of title | Protect control |
| Main weakness | Too narrow; artificial "derivative possession" | Ignores intention | Descriptive, no single principle | Ignores intention |

## 10.3 Types of possession

### 10.3.1 Possession in law and possession in fact

- **Possession in fact** (*de facto* possession): actual physical control, whether or not the law recognises it.
- **Possession in law** (*de jure* possession): possession that the law recognises and protects.

Usually both go together. But there are three situations.

| Situation | Example |
|---|---|
| In fact and in law | I wear my own watch |
| In fact but not in law | A servant holding his master's goods. He has only **custody**; the master has possession in law. (In Roman law this was *possessio naturalis*, natural possession) |
| In law but not in fact | The master in the example above. Also an heir before taking over the deceased's property, or an owner of goods in a locked warehouse while abroad |

Roman law used *possessio civilis* for possession that could ripen into ownership and was legally protected, and *possessio naturalis* for mere physical holding.

!!! note "Nepal"
    Section 269(2) of the Civil Code says that only possessory right acquired in good faith, peacefully or openly is deemed to be lawfully acquired. So Nepal's law separates lawful possession from mere holding.

### 10.3.2 Mediate and immediate possession

- **Immediate (direct) possession:** the person holds the thing himself.
- **Mediate (indirect) possession:** the person possesses through another who holds it for him.

Salmond explains mediate possession as possession through another person. The common situations:

1. **Through an agent or servant.** My servant carries my bag. He holds it only for me and claims no interest. I am in possession.
2. **Through a bailee or tenant who holds for himself and for me.** I lend my book to a friend or rent my house to a tenant. The friend or tenant has immediate possession. I keep mediate possession, because he holds subject to my right to get it back.
3. **Through a holder who acknowledges my title.** A warehouse keeper stores my goods. He holds them, but he recognises that they are mine.

Both kinds can exist at the same time over the same thing. The tenant has immediate possession; the landlord has mediate possession.

| Point | Immediate | Mediate |
|---|---|---|
| Who holds | The possessor himself | Another person on his behalf |
| Example | I keep my book on my desk | My tenant lives in my house |
| Number of people | One | At least two (mediate + immediate) |

!!! note "Nepal"
    Section 270(1) of the Civil Code says a person may acquire possessory right in person or through an agent. Section 270(2) lets an incompetent or quasi-competent person (e.g. a minor or person of unsound mind) acquire it through a guardian or curator. This is mediate possession in Nepali law.

### 10.3.3 Corporeal and incorporeal possession

- **Corporeal possession:** possession of a material thing (land, a car, a book). Roman law called it *possessio corporis*.
- **Incorporeal possession:** possession of a **right**, not a thing. Examples: a right of way over a neighbour's land, a right to take water from a stream, a right to light. Roman law called it *juris quasi possessio*, "quasi-possession of a right".

Salmond says incorporeal possession is the continuing exercise and enjoyment of a right. You possess a right of way by actually using the path, openly and repeatedly, as of right.

| Point | Corporeal | Incorporeal |
|---|---|---|
| Object | A material thing | A right |
| How shown | Physical control | Continuous use or enjoyment |
| Example | Holding a watch | Using a path over another's land for years |
| Roman term | *possessio corporis* | *juris quasi possessio* |

Long incorporeal possession can create rights such as easements (rights over another's land). The Civil Code 2074 deals with easements (सुविधाभार) in a separate chapter, which is outside this unit.

### 10.3.4 Constructive possession

Constructive possession is possession that the law treats as existing, though the person has no physical control.

Examples:

- **Symbolic delivery.** Handing over the keys of a warehouse transfers possession of the goods inside.
- **Documents of title.** Handing over a bill of lading (a shipping document) transfers possession of the goods on the ship.
- **An absent owner.** A person on a long trip still possesses the furniture in his locked house.

Constructive possession overlaps with "possession in law but not in fact" and with mediate possession. The idea is the same: the law looks at the right to control, not at the hand.

### 10.3.5 Concurrent (joint) possession

Concurrent possession means two or more persons possess the same thing at the same time.

The general rule is that possession is **exclusive**: two people cannot each possess the whole thing **adversely to each other**. If A and B both claim the whole field against each other, only one is in possession.

But concurrent possession is possible when the claims are **not hostile**:

1. **Co-owners.** Brothers who jointly own family land possess it together. In Nepal, members of a joint family hold property in common (सगोलको सम्पत्ति, Civil Code s. 257) until partition.
2. **Mediate and immediate possession together.** Landlord and tenant, or bailor and bailee, both possess at the same time.
3. **Owner and holder of a limited right.** An owner possesses his land while his neighbour possesses a right of way over it.

### Adverse possession (not listed in the syllabus heading, but always expected)

Adverse possession is possession of another's property **without his consent, openly, and as if one were the owner**, for the period fixed by law. When that period ends, the owner's right to recover is barred and the possessor can become the owner.

Conditions (common to most systems):

1. Actual possession.
2. Open, not secret.
3. Peaceful, not by force.
4. Hostile to the owner (as of right, not by permission).
5. Continuous for the full period.

!!! note "Nepal: Section 273 of the Civil Code 2074"
    - If a person possesses another's **movable property for more than three years**, or **land for more than thirty years**, as if he owned it, he is deemed to have adverse possessory right (प्रतिकूल भोगाधिकार) (s. 273(1)).
    - **No adverse possession** over government, public, community or trust (guthi) land, however long the possession (proviso 1).
    - A contract or other law may provide otherwise for particular property (proviso 2).
    - The adverse possessor may obtain ownership in his own name (s. 273(2)).
    - It does **not** apply if the property was possessed without the owner's knowledge, secretly or by force (s. 273(3)).
    - The period is counted **from the commencement of the Code** (s. 273(4)). The Code came into force on 1 Bhadra 2075 (17 August 2018). So the thirty-year period for land cannot finish before 2105 B.S.

### 10.3.6 Acquisition, continuation and termination of possession

#### Acquisition

Salmond lists three modes.

**1. Taking (without the consent of the previous possessor)**

- **Lawful taking:** catching a wild animal, picking up a thing that belongs to no one (*res nullius*), finding a lost thing.
- **Unlawful taking:** theft, robbery, forcible seizure. The wrongdoer still gets possession in fact, though not a lawful possessory right.

**2. Delivery (with the consent of the previous possessor)**

| Kind | What happens | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Actual delivery** | The thing is physically handed over | I hand you a book I sold you |
| **Symbolic delivery** | A symbol is handed over | I give you the keys of the godown |
| ***traditio brevi manu*** ("delivery with a short hand") | The person already holding the thing as custodian or bailee becomes the possessor in his own right; no physical handing over | I lent you my book; now I sell it to you. You already have it |
| ***constitutum possessorium*** ("possessory agreement") | The possessor becomes a mere holder for the new possessor | I sell you my book but borrow it back. It stays with me, but now I hold it for you |
| **Attornment** | A third person holding the thing agrees to hold it for the new possessor | My goods are in a warehouse. I sell them to you; the warehouse keeper agrees to hold them for you |

**3. Operation of law**

Possession passes without taking or delivery: on death to heirs, on insolvency to a trustee, or by court order.

!!! note "Nepal: Section 269(1)"
    Possessory right may be acquired (a) by holding the property as its owner, (b) by acquiring property owned by another in accordance with law, or (c) by obtaining consent to have possessory right. Under s. 269(2), only possession acquired in good faith, peacefully or openly counts as lawful.

#### Continuation

- Possession, once acquired, continues as long as the corpus and animus continue in some form.
- Continuous physical holding is not needed. A person keeps possession of his house while on holiday, and of goods in a warehouse.
- Animus is presumed to continue unless the possessor shows he has given it up.
- A thing lost inside one's own house is still possessed. A thing lost on a public road usually is not.

!!! note "Nepal: Section 271"
    A possessor has the right (a) to possess the property uninterruptedly, subject to law or contract, and (b) to use its benefits. A bona fide possessor of another's property may recover essential expenses on its care from the owner (s. 271(2)) and may keep the property until paid (s. 271(3)). He may also remove goods he attached to it, without damaging the property (s. 271(4)).

#### Termination (loss of possession)

Possession ends when:

1. **Both corpus and animus are lost:** abandonment. I throw away an old shoe.
2. **Corpus alone is lost:** the thing is taken by someone else (theft, dispossession), or lost where I cannot recover it.
3. **Animus alone is lost:** I keep the thing but now hold it for another (*constitutum possessorium*).
4. **Delivery** to another person.
5. **Destruction** of the thing.
6. **Operation of law:** death, court order.

!!! note "Nepal: Section 272"
    Possessory right is extinguished if the person (a) abandons the property, (b) transfers it, or (c) the property is totally destroyed or becomes useless.

### 10.3.7 Possessory remedies

Possessory remedies are legal actions that protect a person **because he is in possession**, without asking him to prove ownership.

#### Why the law protects possession

| Theory | Reason |
|---|---|
| Savigny | Disturbing possession is violence against the person; the law keeps peace |
| Ihering | Possession is the outwork of ownership; protecting the possessor usually protects the owner, cheaply and quickly |
| Kant, Hegel | Possession expresses the person's will; to disturb it is to disturb his freedom |
| Holmes | People instinctively resist losing what they hold; law follows this instinct |
| Practical view | Prevents self-help and violence; possession is good evidence of title; proof of ownership is slow |

#### Roman law: the interdicts

The Roman praetor issued orders called **interdicts** to protect possession, without deciding ownership.

- *Uti possidetis*: to keep possession of land.
- *Utrubi*: to keep possession of movables.
- *Unde vi*: to restore a person thrown out of land by force.

#### English law

- **Trespass** to land and to goods: any direct interference with a person's possession.
- **Ejectment:** action to recover land.
- **Detinue** and **conversion:** actions to recover goods or their value. (In England the Torts (Interference with Goods) Act 1977 abolished detinue; conversion now covers its ground.)
- A possessor can sue a wrongdoer even if he is not the owner. The wrongdoer generally cannot defend himself by saying the property belongs to a third person. This defence is called *jus tertii* ("the right of a third party"), and it is generally not allowed against a possessor. (For goods in England, s. 8 of the 1977 Act now lets a defendant show a third party's better right; the classic common-law rule is as stated.)

#### Indian law (often cited in Nepali textbooks)

- Section 6 of the Specific Relief Act 1963: a person dispossessed of immovable property without his consent, otherwise than by due course of law, may sue to recover possession within six months, without proving title.

#### Nepal's possessory remedies (Muluki Civil Code 2074)

| Section | What it says (plain words) |
|---|---|
| 271(1) | The possessor has the right to possess uninterruptedly and to use the benefits |
| 274(1) | A person who possesses property forcibly, in bad faith or secretly must return it with the benefits he took, and pay reasonable compensation for loss caused by his negligence |
| 274(2) | If another's property is lost or damaged during someone's possession, that possessor must pay compensation |
| 275 | A person aggrieved under this chapter may sue within **three months** of knowing of the act |
| 276 | No one may use, damage or take another's property without consent, or enter another's house and compound without consent except in accordance with law |
| 277 | Property is inviolable. Taking possession of another's property by encroachment is deemed trespass |
| 287 | No one may cultivate or use land in which another has right, possession and ownership, without consent |
| 421(4)-(6) | Lost or stolen property: the owner may claim it from the finder within three years, with proof of ownership; the finder returns it on being paid the expenses of keeping it; a buyer at a public market or auction need not return it until paid its value |

Article 25(1) of the Constitution of Nepal (2015) guarantees every citizen the right, subject to law, to acquire, enjoy, own, sell, have professional gains from, and otherwise use or dispose of property. Possessory remedies give practical effect to this right.

## Possession and ownership

This comparison is asked often, in this unit and in Unit 11.

| Point | Possession | Ownership |
|---|---|---|
| Nature | A fact (*de facto*) | A right (*de jure*) |
| What it is | Control with intention to control | The fullest right over a thing |
| Can a thief have it? | Yes | No |
| Proof | Show control | Show title (documents, lawful transfer) |
| Relation | Possession is the outward sign of ownership (Ihering) | Ownership is the legal basis that possession usually rests on |
| Duration | Lost when control is lost | Continues even when the thing is with someone else |
| Transfer | By delivery | By lawful transfer (sale deed, registration for land) |
| Protection | Possessory remedies; quick, no proof of title | Proprietary remedies; owner must prove title |
| Nepal | Sections 268-275 (भोगाधिकार) | Sections 266-267 (स्वामित्व) |

How they connect:

1. **Usually together.** Most owners possess what they own.
2. **Possession without ownership:** thief, finder, tenant, bailee.
3. **Ownership without possession:** landlord, owner of stolen goods, bailor.
4. **Possession can become ownership:** by taking things that belong to no one, and by adverse possession (s. 273 in Nepal).
5. **Possession is evidence of ownership:** the possessor is presumed owner until the contrary is shown.

## Compare

| Pair | First | Second | Key difference |
|---|---|---|---|
| In fact vs in law | Physical control | Control recognised by law | A servant has the first only; the master has the second |
| Immediate vs mediate | Held by the possessor himself | Held through another | Tenant (immediate), landlord (mediate) |
| Corporeal vs incorporeal | Of a thing | Of a right | Watch vs right of way |
| Constructive vs actual | Treated by law as possession without physical control | Physical control | Keys of a godown vs goods in hand |
| Exclusive vs concurrent | One possessor | Two or more with non-hostile claims | Sole owner vs co-owners |
| Lawful vs adverse | With the owner's consent or right | Against the owner, openly, as of right | Tenant vs squatter |
| Animus domini vs animus possidendi | Intention to own (Savigny) | Intention to exclude others (Salmond) | Tenant lacks the first but has the second |

## Model answers

??? question "Likely short note: Animus possidendi and corpus possessionis"
    - **Meaning.** The two elements of possession. *Animus possidendi* is the intention to possess; *corpus possessionis* is actual control.
    - **Animus (Salmond):** the intention to appropriate the exclusive use of the thing. It need not be a claim of right (a thief has it), need not be an intention to own (a tenant has it), need not be specific (contents of a letterbox), and may be on behalf of another.
    - **Corpus (Salmond):** two relations: to other people (assurance they will not interfere) and to the thing (power to use it when wished). Depends on the nature of the thing.
    - **Savigny vs Ihering.** Savigny required animus domini, the intention to own. Ihering said animus is not separate; corpus is the owner-like relation to the thing.
    - **Case.** *Bridges v Hawkesworth* (1851): a shopkeeper lacked animus over notes on the public floor; the finder won.
    - **Nepal.** Section 268 of the Civil Code 2074: lawful holding plus intention to possess gives possessory right.

??? question "Likely short note: Adverse possession"
    - **Meaning.** Possession of another's property without consent, openly and as owner, for the period fixed by law. When the period ends, the owner loses the right to recover and the possessor may become owner.
    - **Conditions.** Actual, open, peaceful, hostile (as of right) and continuous.
    - **Basis.** The law favours the person who uses property over an owner who sleeps on his rights; it also ends old disputes.
    - **Nepal (Civil Code 2074, s. 273).** Three years for movables, thirty years for land. Not available over government, public, community or trust land. Not available where possession was secret, forcible or without the owner's knowledge. Period counted from the Code's commencement (17 August 2018). The possessor may get ownership registered in his name.
    - **Criticism.** It can reward wrongdoers; Nepal limits it heavily, especially for public land.

??? question "Likely short note: Mediate and immediate possession"
    - **Immediate:** the possessor holds the thing himself.
    - **Mediate:** he possesses through another, who holds it for him (Salmond).
    - **Three situations:** through an agent or servant; through a bailee or tenant who holds for himself and for the owner; through a holder (e.g. a warehouse keeper) who acknowledges the owner's title.
    - Both can exist at once: tenant (immediate) and landlord (mediate). This is a form of concurrent possession.
    - **Nepal.** Section 270 of the Civil Code: possessory right may be acquired in person or through an agent; a minor or incompetent person acquires it through a guardian or curator.

??? question "Likely short note: Possessory remedies"
    - **Meaning.** Remedies given to a person because he is in possession, without proof of ownership.
    - **Why.** Savigny: protect the person and keep peace. Ihering: possession is the outwork of ownership.
    - **Roman law:** interdicts *uti possidetis*, *utrubi*, *unde vi*.
    - **English law:** trespass, ejectment, detinue, conversion; *jus tertii* generally no defence. *Armory v Delamirie* (1722).
    - **India:** s. 6 of the Specific Relief Act 1963 (suit within six months, no proof of title).
    - **Nepal (Civil Code 2074):** s. 271 (right to uninterrupted possession), s. 274 (return and compensation for forcible, mala fide or secret possession), s. 275 (three-month limitation), ss. 276-277 (no use or encroachment of another's property).

## Full 20-mark answers

??? example "Q1. Explain the various forms of possession. (2078 Q6, 20 marks)"
    **Introduction**

    Possession is the relation of control between a person and a thing. In simple words, a person possesses a thing when he has control over it and means to keep others away from it. Salmond defines the possession of a material object as "the continuing exercise of a claim to the exclusive use of it". Pollock treats possession mainly as physical control and separates possession in fact from possession in law. Savigny held that possession needs physical power over the thing (corpus) and an intention to hold it as owner (animus domini). Ihering saw possession as the outward, visible form of ownership.

    Possession has two elements: *corpus possessionis*, the physical control, and *animus possidendi*, the intention to possess. Because these two elements can be present in different degrees and through different persons, possession takes many forms. The main forms are explained below.

    **1. Possession in fact and possession in law**

    Possession in fact means actual physical control. Possession in law means possession that the law recognises and protects. Usually they go together, as when I wear my own watch. But they can separate. A servant who holds his master's goods has possession in fact but only custody in law; the master has possession in law. An heir has possession in law of the deceased's property before he physically takes it. Roman law expressed this through *possessio naturalis* (mere physical holding) and *possessio civilis* (legally protected possession).

    **2. Immediate and mediate possession**

    Immediate or direct possession is held by the possessor himself. Mediate or indirect possession is held through another person. Salmond explains mediate possession in three situations: possession through an agent or servant, who claims no interest; possession through a bailee or tenant, who holds both for himself and for the owner; and possession through a holder such as a warehouse keeper who acknowledges the owner's title. A landlord has mediate possession and his tenant has immediate possession of the same house at the same time.

    **3. Corporeal and incorporeal possession**

    Corporeal possession is possession of a material thing, such as land, a car or a book. Incorporeal possession is possession of a right, such as a right of way over a neighbour's land or a right to take water. Roman law called it *juris quasi possessio*. A right is possessed by its continuous exercise and enjoyment. Long incorporeal possession may create rights such as easements.

    **4. Constructive possession**

    Constructive possession is possession that the law treats as existing, though there is no physical control. Handing over the keys of a godown transfers possession of the goods inside. Handing over a bill of lading transfers possession of goods on a ship. An owner on a long journey still possesses the furniture in his locked house. The law here looks at the right and power to control rather than at the hand.

    **5. Concurrent possession**

    As a rule, possession is exclusive: two people cannot each possess the whole of a thing against each other. But two or more persons can possess the same thing at the same time when their claims are not hostile. Co-owners, such as members of a joint family, possess jointly. A landlord and tenant, or a bailor and bailee, possess concurrently as mediate and immediate possessors. An owner of land and the holder of a right of way over it also possess at the same time.

    **6. Adverse possession**

    Adverse possession is possession of another's property without his consent, openly, peacefully and as if one were the owner, for the period fixed by law. When the period ends, the owner loses his remedy and the possessor may become owner. It is the main way possession turns into ownership.

    **7. Lawful (bona fide) and unlawful (mala fide) possession**

    Possession may be lawful, as with an owner or tenant, or unlawful, as with a thief. Even a thief has possession in fact, and the law protects him against everyone except the true owner or a person with a better right. But the law gives fuller rights to the bona fide possessor, such as the right to recover expenses.

    **Forms of possession by mode of acquisition**

    Possession can also be classified by how it begins. It may be acquired by **taking**, lawfully (catching a wild animal, finding a lost thing) or unlawfully (theft). It may be acquired by **delivery**: actual delivery, symbolic delivery, *traditio brevi manu* (the holder becomes possessor, as when I sell you the book you borrowed), *constitutum possessorium* (the possessor becomes a holder for the buyer, as when I sell you my book but borrow it back) and attornment (a warehouse keeper agrees to hold for the buyer). It may also pass by **operation of law**, as on death or insolvency.

    **Nepal's position**

    The Muluki Civil Code 2074 has a chapter on ownership and possession (Part 4, Chapter 2, sections 266 to 275). It uses the term भोगाधिकार (possessory right).

    - **Section 268:** a person who lawfully holds property with the intention to possess it has possessory right. This adopts corpus plus animus.
    - **Section 269:** possessory right is acquired by holding property as owner, by acquiring another's property lawfully, or by consent. Only possession acquired in good faith, peacefully or openly is lawful. This separates lawful from unlawful possession.
    - **Section 270:** possessory right may be acquired in person or through an agent; a minor or incompetent person acquires it through a guardian or curator. This is mediate possession.
    - **Section 271:** the possessor may possess without interruption and use the benefits. A bona fide possessor may recover essential expenses from the owner and keep the property until paid.
    - **Section 272:** possessory right ends on abandonment, transfer, or total destruction of the property.
    - **Section 273:** adverse possessory right after three years for movables and thirty years for land, counted from the Code's commencement. It is not available over government, public, community or trust land, or where possession was secret, forcible or without the owner's knowledge.
    - **Section 274:** a person who possesses forcibly, in bad faith or secretly must return the property and its benefits and pay compensation.

    Joint family property (सगोलको सम्पत्ति, s. 257) in Nepal is a good example of concurrent possession. The Code's chapter on bailment (sections 575 and 576) gives an example of mediate and immediate possession.

    **Critical view**

    The forms overlap. Constructive possession, possession in law without fact and mediate possession describe much the same idea from different angles. This shows that possession is not one fixed concept. As Paton suggests, each legal system decides who should be treated as possessor for practical reasons. Nepal's Code is practical. It gives clear rules on acquisition, rights and termination. But it does not define incorporeal, constructive or concurrent possession; these are left to courts and to general principles. Its rule on adverse possession is strict: by counting time only from 2075 B.S., no one can acquire land by adverse possession before 2105 B.S.

    **Conclusion**

    Possession is control plus intention. Its forms show the different ways the law recognises that control: directly or through others, over things or over rights, physically or constructively, alone or jointly, lawfully or adversely. Nepal's Civil Code 2074 accepts most of these forms and protects possession as a separate right, while guarding public land from adverse possession.

??? example "Q2. Define possession. Explain its elements with reference to Savigny's and Ihering's theories. (likely question, 20 marks)"
    **Introduction**

    Possession is one of the most basic ideas in law. In plain words, a person possesses a thing when he controls it and means to keep others away from it. Possession is a fact, while ownership is a right. A thief possesses stolen goods but never owns them.

    Salmond wrote that no conception in legal theory is more difficult than possession. He defined the possession of a material object as "the continuing exercise of a claim to the exclusive use of it". Pollock described possession as physical control, the apparent power to use a thing and exclude others. Holmes required a physical relation to the object and an intention to exclude others.

    **Elements of possession**

    All main theories accept two elements.

    1. **Corpus possessionis (physical element).** This is actual control. It does not mean constant physical holding. Salmond explains it as two relations. The first is the relation to other people: the possessor must have reasonable assurance that others will not interfere. The second is the relation to the thing: he must be able to use it when he wishes. What is enough depends on the nature of the thing. A purse is controlled by holding it; a field by fencing or cultivating it; goods in a locked house by the lock.
    2. **Animus possidendi (mental element).** This is the intention to possess. Salmond calls it the intention to appropriate to oneself the exclusive use of the thing. It need not be a claim of right, since a thief has it. It need not be an intention to own, since a tenant has it. It need not be specific, since a person possesses the unknown contents of his letterbox. It may be held on behalf of another, as by a servant.

    Both elements must be present together. *Bridges v Hawkesworth* (1851) shows that without animus there is no possession: a shopkeeper did not possess notes dropped in the public part of his shop because he did not know of them, so the finder took them.

    Savigny and Ihering disagreed sharply on how these two elements work.

    **Savigny's theory (subjective theory)**

    Savigny wrote *Das Recht des Besitzes* in 1803, based on Roman law.

    - **Corpus** for Savigny is the physical power to deal with the thing and exclude others. It need not be actual touch, but there must be a present physical power.
    - **Animus** for Savigny is *animus domini*, the intention to hold the thing as owner. This is the key point of his theory. It is called "subjective" because the mental element decides possession.
    - So a tenant, borrower, agent or bailee is not a possessor. He has only detention (custody), because he holds for another.
    - Roman law protected some holders who lacked animus domini, such as the pledgee, the sequester and the long-term lessee. Savigny explained these as cases of **derivative possession**, transferred from the owner.
    - Savigny said the law protects possession because disturbing it is violence against the person.

    **Ihering's theory (objective theory)**

    Ihering rejected Savigny's view.

    - **Possession is the outward form of ownership.** A person possesses when he stands to the thing in the visible relation in which an owner usually stands. Ihering called possession the objective realisation of ownership.
    - **Corpus** is not physical power but this outward, economic relation. Building materials left on a building site are possessed by the builder though unguarded. A wallet left on a road is not, because owners do not leave wallets on roads.
    - **Animus** is not a separate element. It is only the awareness that goes with corpus. If corpus is shown, animus is presumed. The theory is called "objective" because it looks at the outward facts.
    - So tenants and bailees are possessors unless the law expressly says otherwise.
    - The law protects possession because it is the outwork of ownership. Most possessors are owners, so protecting possession protects owners without making them prove title.

    **Comparison**

    | Point | Savigny | Ihering |
    |---|---|---|
    | Type | Subjective | Objective |
    | Corpus | Physical power to deal with the thing | Owner-like outward relation |
    | Animus | Animus domini, essential | Not separate; follows corpus |
    | Tenant, bailee | Not possessors (detention only) | Possessors |
    | Why protect | Protect the person; prevent violence | Protect ownership |

    **Criticism of both**

    Savigny's theory is too narrow. Modern law treats tenants, bailees and pledgees as possessors. His idea of derivative possession is an artificial patch for exceptions. His demand for present physical power fails for unguarded crops or timber.

    Ihering's theory corrects these faults but goes too far the other way. By reducing animus to a shadow of corpus, it cannot explain the finder cases, where intention decides the result. Its test, "as an owner would deal with it", is vague. And protecting possession to protect owners also protects thieves.

    **Salmond's middle path**

    Salmond kept both elements but changed animus domini to animus possidendi: an intention to exclude others, not to own. This accepts Ihering's result (tenants and bailees possess) while keeping Savigny's insistence on intention. Pollock went further towards physical control and separated possession in fact from possession in law. Salmond's view is the one followed in common-law countries and in Nepal's law teaching.

    **Nepal's position**

    The Muluki Civil Code 2074 adopts the corpus-plus-animus approach. Section 268 provides that a person who lawfully holds property with the intention to possess it is deemed to have possessory right (भोगाधिकार). "Holds" reflects corpus; "intention to possess" reflects animus possidendi, not animus domini. Section 269(1) allows possessory right to be acquired by holding property as owner, by lawful acquisition, or by consent. The third mode, by consent, covers tenants and bailees, which is closer to Ihering and Salmond than to Savigny. Section 270 allows possession through an agent, guardian or curator. Section 271 protects the possessor's right to uninterrupted possession and benefits.

    The intention to possess "as owner" still matters for one purpose in Nepal: adverse possession under s. 273 requires possession as if the property were one's own. Here the idea is close to Savigny's animus domini.

    **Critical view**

    No single theory explains every case. Savigny captured the importance of intention; Ihering captured the importance of outward facts and the link with ownership. Modern law takes something from each. Nepal's Code is a good example: it requires intention to possess, but not intention to own, except for adverse possession.

    **Conclusion**

    Possession is control with intention. Its two elements are corpus possessionis and animus possidendi. Savigny made the intention to own the heart of possession; Ihering made the outward owner-like relation the heart of it. Salmond's balanced view, followed in Nepal's Civil Code 2074, keeps both elements but requires only an intention to possess.

??? example "Q3. Distinguish possession and ownership. Discuss possessory remedies. (likely question, 20 marks)"
    **Introduction**

    Possession and ownership are the two basic relations between a person and a thing. In plain words, possession is having control of a thing; ownership is having the fullest right to it. Salmond describes possession as "the continuing exercise of a claim to the exclusive use" of a thing. Ownership, in Salmond's account, is the relation between a person and a right that is vested in him. Austin described ownership as a right over a determinate thing, indefinite in point of user, unrestricted in point of disposition and unlimited in duration.

    Salmond put the difference simply: possession is the de facto (factual) counterpart of ownership, which is de jure (legal). Ihering saw possession as the outward, visible form of ownership.

    **Elements of possession**

    Possession has two elements: *corpus possessionis*, actual control, and *animus possidendi*, the intention to possess. Savigny required an intention to hold as owner (animus domini). Salmond and modern law require only an intention to exclude others, so tenants and bailees are possessors.

    **Differences between possession and ownership**

    | Point | Possession | Ownership |
    |---|---|---|
    | Nature | Fact (de facto) | Right (de jure) |
    | Basis | Control and intention | Lawful title |
    | Thief | Has possession | Never has ownership |
    | Proof | Show control | Show title |
    | Duration | Ends when control is lost | Continues though the thing is lost or with another |
    | Transfer | By delivery | By lawful transfer, registration for land |
    | Protection | Possessory remedies, quick | Proprietary remedies, need proof of title |
    | Nepal | Civil Code ss. 268-275 | Civil Code ss. 266-267 |

    **Relationship between them**

    1. They usually go together: most owners possess what they own.
    2. Possession can exist without ownership: a thief, finder, tenant or bailee.
    3. Ownership can exist without possession: a landlord, a bailor, an owner of stolen goods.
    4. Possession is evidence of ownership: the possessor is presumed to be owner until the contrary is proved. Hence "possession is nine points of the law".
    5. Possession can become ownership: by taking things that belong to no one, and by adverse possession after the period fixed by law.

    **Possessory remedies**

    Possessory remedies protect a person because he is in possession, without asking him to prove ownership.

    *Why the law protects possession.* Savigny said disturbing possession is violence against the person, so the law protects it to keep peace. Ihering said possession is the outwork of ownership: protecting the possessor protects the owner in most cases, quickly and without proof of title. Kant and Hegel said possession expresses the person's will. Practical reasons also apply: it discourages self-help and keeps disputes out of the street.

    *Roman law.* The praetor protected possession by **interdicts**: *uti possidetis* (to keep possession of land), *utrubi* (to keep possession of movables) and *unde vi* (to restore a person forcibly thrown out of land). Ownership was not decided in these proceedings.

    *English law.* The possessor may sue in **trespass** for any direct interference, in **ejectment** to recover land, and in **detinue** or **conversion** to recover goods or their value. A wrongdoer generally cannot defend by showing that a third person owns the thing (*jus tertii*). In *Armory v Delamirie* (1722), a chimney sweep's boy who found a jewel recovered its value from a goldsmith who kept the stones. The finder's title was good against all but the true owner.

    *Indian law.* Section 6 of the Specific Relief Act 1963 allows a person dispossessed of immovable property without consent and otherwise than by due course of law to sue for recovery within six months, without proving title.

    **Nepal's position**

    The Muluki Civil Code 2074 separates ownership (स्वामित्व) and possessory right (भोगाधिकार) in Part 4, Chapter 2.

    - **Ownership.** Section 266: a person who acquires a right in property in accordance with law is deemed its owner. Section 267 lists the owner's rights: to use, sell or transfer, mortgage, deal in, take benefits, build on and protect, use the land below and the sky above, destroy or dispose of, and sue to secure the property.
    - **Possession.** Section 268: lawful holding with the intention to possess gives possessory right. Section 271: the possessor may possess uninterruptedly and use the benefits; a bona fide possessor may recover essential expenses and keep the property until paid.
    - **Remedies.** Section 274 requires a person who possesses forcibly, in bad faith or secretly to return the property and its benefits and to pay compensation for loss caused by negligence. If another's property is lost or damaged during someone's possession, that person must compensate. Section 275 sets a limitation of three months from knowledge of the act. Sections 276 and 277 forbid using, damaging, taking or encroaching on another's property without consent; taking possession by encroachment is deemed trespass. Section 287 forbids cultivating or using land in which another has right, possession and ownership.
    - **Possession into ownership.** Section 273 allows adverse possessory right after three years for movables and thirty years for land, counted from the Code's commencement, and lets the possessor obtain ownership. It does not apply to government, public, community or trust land, or to secret or forcible possession.
    - **Lost property.** Section 421(4)-(6): the owner may claim lost or stolen property from the finder within three years; the finder returns it on being paid his expenses.
    - **Constitution.** Article 25(1) guarantees every citizen the right, subject to law, to acquire, enjoy, own, sell, have professional gains from, and otherwise use or dispose of property.

    **Critical view**

    Possessory remedies sometimes protect wrongdoers, since even a thief is protected against strangers. The justification is that peace and order matter more than a quick decision on title, which can be decided later. Nepal's Code is protective of owners: it penalises forcible and secret possession and does not let such possession ripen into ownership. But the three-month limitation in s. 275 is short; a possessor who learns of an interference late, or is poor or far away, may lose his remedy. The Code also gives no special summary procedure, like India's s. 6, to restore a dispossessed person quickly without proof of title.

    **Conclusion**

    Possession is a fact; ownership is a right. They usually coincide, but each can exist without the other, and long possession can become ownership. The law protects possession as a separate interest to keep peace and to protect owners. Nepal's Civil Code 2074 clearly separates the two, gives the possessor rights and remedies, and allows adverse possession only within narrow limits.
