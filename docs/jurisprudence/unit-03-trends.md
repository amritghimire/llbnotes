## Start here: why "trends"?

In Unit 2 you met six schools. Each gives an answer to "what is law?". All six share one hidden belief: that law can be studied as something fairly neutral, rational and the same for everyone.

From the 1970s, three movements challenged that belief.

| Movement | Its main charge against older jurisprudence | One-line image |
|---|---|---|
| Critical legal studies | Law pretends to be neutral but serves the powerful. "Law is politics." | Law as a mask |
| Feminist jurisprudence | Law was written by men, from men's experience, and it hides women's harms | Law wearing male glasses |
| Postmodernism | There is no one true theory of law; every "truth" is one story among many | Law as a text with many readings |

They are called **trends** rather than schools because they are loose movements, not single theories. Members often disagree with each other. What they share is a **critical** attitude: they ask whose interests the law really serves.

**An everyday example.** Before 2002, Nepal's Muluki Ain gave a daughter a share in family property only on terms tied to her marriage, while a son's share did not depend on marriage. The rule was written in neutral legal language. A positivist would simply say "that is the law". A CLS writer would ask who gains from it (the male line that keeps land). A feminist would ask why a woman's property right depends on her marriage but a man's does not. A postmodernist would ask why one idea of "family" was treated as the only true one. Nepal later changed this rule, as you will see in 3.2.

## 3.1 Critical Legal Studies

### Meaning

Critical legal studies (CLS) is a movement of left-wing legal scholars, mostly in the United States, that began in the late 1970s. Its central claim is that **law is politics**. Legal rules, it says, are not neutral or objective. They are shaped by, and they protect, the interests of powerful groups. Legal reasoning only makes these political choices look natural and necessary.

### Origin

- The movement took shape at the **Conference on Critical Legal Studies**, held at the University of Wisconsin-Madison in **1977**.
- Leading figures: **Duncan Kennedy**, **Roberto Mangabeira Unger**, **Morton Horwitz**, **Mark Tushnet**, **David Trubek**, and later **Mark Kelman**, who wrote *A Guide to Critical Legal Studies* (1987).
- Unger's article and book *The Critical Legal Studies Movement* (1983/1986) is the best-known statement of its ideas.
- Harvard Law School became its main centre in the 1980s.

### Roots

| Root | What CLS took from it |
|---|---|
| American legal realism (Unit 2) | Judges do not just "find" law; their choices shape it. CLS pushed this further: the choices are political |
| Marxism (Unit 2) | Law serves the dominant class. CLS used this idea but did not tie it only to economics |
| Frankfurt School critical theory | The method of "critique": exposing how ideas hide power |
| Civil rights and anti-Vietnam War movements of the 1960s | Distrust of official claims that the system is fair |

### Main ideas

1. **Law is politics.** Legal decisions are political choices dressed up as logic. There is no neutral "legal reasoning" separate from politics and values.
2. **Indeterminacy.** Legal rules do not decide cases by themselves. For almost every rule there is a counter-rule or an exception (freedom of contract vs. protection of the weak party, for example). A judge can reach different results using equally "legal" arguments. So the outcome depends on the judge's values.
3. **Legitimation.** Law makes an unequal society look fair. Because the law speaks of equal rights for all, people come to believe that existing inequality is natural or deserved. This hides domination.
4. **Hierarchy.** Law and legal education reproduce social hierarchy. Duncan Kennedy's essay *Legal Education and the Reproduction of Hierarchy* (1982) argued that law schools train students to accept the existing order and their place in it.
5. **The "fundamental contradiction".** Kennedy argued that liberal law is built on a tension between individual freedom and the need for community. Law can never settle it, so it switches between the two as convenient.
6. **Critique of rights.** Some CLS writers, such as Mark Tushnet, argued that formal rights are unstable and can distract people from real political struggle.
7. **"Trashing".** A CLS technique of taking a respected legal text or doctrine and showing that it is inconsistent, biased or empty.

### Offshoots

CLS gave birth to, or strongly influenced, later movements:

- **Feminist jurisprudence** (3.2): applies the critique to gender.
- **Critical race theory:** applies it to race. Derrick Bell and Kimberlé Crenshaw are key names.
- **Postmodern legal theory** (3.3): shares the distrust of neutral, objective truth.

### Criticism of CLS

| Criticism | Explanation |
|---|---|
| Nihilism | It destroys faith in law but offers no alternative system. Critics say it leaves nothing to build on |
| Overstates indeterminacy | Most cases are easy and predictable. Hard cases are a small minority |
| Inconsistent | If all legal argument is just politics, then CLS's own arguments are just politics too |
| Attacks rights that the weak need | Critical race theorists, such as Patricia Williams, replied that rights matter greatly to minorities who never had them |
| Faded quickly | As an organised movement, CLS declined by the 1990s, though its ideas live on in its offshoots |

### Nepal angle

- The **Muluki Ain of 1910 B.S.** (1854 A.D.) wrote caste hierarchy into law. Punishments differed by caste. This is a clear example of what CLS means by law serving and legitimising a social hierarchy.
- Today the Constitution of Nepal (2015) commits to **proportional inclusion**. Article 42(1) gives economically, socially or educationally backward women, Dalits, indigenous nationalities, Madhesi and other listed groups the right to take part in state bodies on that basis. This accepts the CLS-type point that formal equality alone is not enough.
- **Public interest litigation** (PIL) has let NGOs and lawyers challenge laws that hide discrimination behind neutral words. Many of the gender cases in 3.2 came this way.

## 3.2 Feminist Jurisprudence

### Meaning

Feminist jurisprudence is the philosophy of law based on the political, economic and social **equality of the sexes**. It studies how law has shaped and kept women's lower position, and how law can be used to end it.

It asks three things:

1. Does the law treat women unequally, openly or in hidden ways?
2. Why: whose experience was the law built on?
3. What changes would give women real (substantive) equality?

The term "feminist jurisprudence" came into wide use in the United States in the late 1970s and 1980s. It grew partly out of critical legal studies, but it focused on gender rather than class.

### The core idea: law is not gender-neutral

Most law looks neutral. It says "a person" or "the citizen". Feminists say this neutrality is false. The "person" the law has in mind is usually a man: a wage-earner, head of household, property owner. Women's experiences (pregnancy, unpaid housework, violence at home) were left out or treated as private.

Catharine MacKinnon put it sharply: "The state is male in the feminist sense: the law sees and treats women the way men see and treat women." (*Toward a Feminist Theory of the State*, 1989.)

### Historical background: the "waves"

| Wave | Period | Main demand | Names to cite |
|---|---|---|---|
| First wave | Late 1700s to early 1900s | Legal personhood: the right to vote, own property, enter professions | Mary Wollstonecraft, *A Vindication of the Rights of Woman* (1792); J.S. Mill, *The Subjection of Women* (1869) |
| Second wave | 1960s to 1980s | Equality at work and in the family; sexual and reproductive freedom; violence against women | Simone de Beauvoir, *The Second Sex* (1949): "One is not born, but rather becomes, a woman"; Catharine MacKinnon; Carol Gilligan |
| Third wave | 1990s onward | Differences among women (race, class, caste, sexuality) | Kimberlé Crenshaw (intersectionality, 1989) |

De Beauvoir's line is the source of the **sex/gender distinction**: sex is biological, but gender roles are made by society. If gender roles are made, law can unmake them.

### Main strands of feminist legal thought

Learn these four; examiners like a table.

| Strand | Key idea | What it wants from law | Names |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Liberal feminism** | Women are as rational as men. Treat them the **same** | Remove legal barriers; equal rights, equal pay, equal property | Wollstonecraft, Mill; Ruth Bader Ginsburg as a litigator |
| **Cultural (difference) feminism** | Women and men differ, and women's values are equally valid | Law should value care and relationships, not only rights and rules | Carol Gilligan, *In a Different Voice* (1982): the "ethic of care" |
| **Radical (dominance) feminism** | The real issue is not difference but **male domination**, especially through sexuality | Attack sexual harassment, rape, pornography as tools of domination | Catharine MacKinnon, Andrea Dworkin |
| **Socialist / Marxist feminism** | Women's oppression comes from both capitalism and patriarchy | Value unpaid domestic work; economic independence for women | — |

Two later strands:

- **Postmodern feminism** rejects the idea of one "woman's experience". Women differ by class, race and culture.
- **Intersectional feminism** (Crenshaw) shows that a person can face several forms of discrimination at once. In Nepal, a Dalit woman may face both caste and gender discrimination.

### Key concepts

1. **Patriarchy.** A system in which men hold power in the family and in society. Feminists say much law grew out of patriarchy: for example, rules on inheritance through the male line.
2. **Public/private divide.** Old law left the family alone as "private". This meant violence and rape inside marriage went unpunished. Feminists say "the personal is political": what happens at home is a matter for the law.
3. **Formal vs. substantive equality.** Formal equality means the same rule for all. Substantive equality looks at results. Because women start from a weaker position, equal treatment may not be enough. This justifies **positive discrimination**, such as reserved seats.
4. **The "reasonable man" standard.** Many legal tests ask what a reasonable man would do. Feminists ask whether this standard ignores women's experience, for example in self-defence by abused women.

### Feminist legal method

Katharine Bartlett, in "Feminist Legal Methods" (1990), described three methods:

1. **Asking the woman question.** Does this rule leave out or harm women's experience? If so, how, and why?
2. **Feminist practical reasoning.** Decide cases by looking closely at the real situation of the people involved, not only by abstract rules.
3. **Consciousness-raising.** Learn from women's shared personal experiences and treat them as evidence.

### International law on women

| Instrument | Year | Point |
|---|---|---|
| Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Discrimination against Women (CEDAW) | 1979 | The "bill of rights for women". Nepal ratified it in 1991 |
| Beijing Declaration and Platform for Action | 1995 | Gender mainstreaming in all policies |

The Supreme Court of Nepal often relies on CEDAW. Section 9(1) of the Nepal Treaty Act, 2047 (1990) says that where a treaty Nepal has joined conflicts with a domestic law, the treaty prevails to that extent. The Court cited this in the 2002 marital rape case.

| Section | Provision |
|---|---|

#### (d) Landmark Supreme Court cases

Most were public interest petitions filed by women lawyers, many through the Forum for Women, Law and Development (FWLD).

| Case | Year | What the Court did |
|---|---|---|
| Property-rights petition (filed 1993) | 1995 | Directed the government to draft a bill on women's right to inherit property. This led to the Muluki Ain (Eleventh Amendment), passed in 2002, which first gave unmarried daughters a share |
| **Meera Dhungana (for FWLD) v. HMG, Ministry of Law, Justice and Parliamentary Affairs** (Writ No. 55 of 2058) | Decided 19 Baisakh 2059 (2002) | Held that a husband who rapes his wife is not immune: marital rape is an offence. Did not strike the law down, but issued a **directive order** to bring a bill to fill the legal gaps on marital rape |
| **Meera Dhungana and Others v. Office of the Prime Minister and Others** | 2004 | Struck down the rule that let a man take a second wife if there was no child after 10 years of marriage. It assumed infertility was the woman's fault. Also held that positive discrimination for women is constitutional |
| **Punyabati Pathak and Others v. Ministry of Foreign Affairs** | 2005 | Struck down the Passport Act rule that women under 35 needed a guardian's consent for a passport |
| **Sapana Pradhan Malla v. | **Sunil Babu Pant and Others v. Nepal Government** | 21 December 2007 | Separated sex from gender and recognised a "third gender"; directed recognition in official documents |
| **Jit Kumari Pangeni (Neupane) and Others v. Prime Minister and Council of Ministers** | 2008 | Held that punishing marital rape more lightly (3-6 months) than other rape violated equality; directed Parliament to revise the punishment |
| **Lakshmi Dhikta v. Government of Nepal** | 2009 | Held that a poor woman's inability to pay for a legal abortion violated her rights; directed a comprehensive abortion law and a government fund for abortion costs |

!!! tip "How to use these cases in the exam"
    You do not need all eight. Pick four that show a pattern: property (1995 directive), violence (marital rape 2002, Jit Kumari Pangeni 2008), autonomy (Punyabati Pathak 2005), reproductive rights (Lakshmi Dhikta 2009). Then say: the Court used Article 11 equality and CEDAW to read feminist ideas into Nepali law.

#### (e) What is still unequal

- **Citizenship for children of Nepali mothers.** Under Article 11(7), a child born to a Nepali woman married to a foreign man may get only **naturalised** citizenship (if domiciled in Nepal and not holding another citizenship). Feminists argue this still treats mothers differently from fathers.
- **Weak marital rape punishment.** Section 219(4) sets a maximum of five years with no minimum.
- **Harmful practices continue** despite bans: dowry, child marriage below 20, chhaupadi (isolating women during menstruation), and witchcraft accusations.
- **Gap between law and life.** Few women actually hold land; women in Parliament are mostly on proportional lists, not directly elected; enforcement in rural areas is weak.

### Criticism of feminist jurisprudence

| Criticism | Reply |
|---|---|
| It speaks for all women, but women differ (class, caste, race) | Third-wave and intersectional feminism accept this and study the differences |
| Positive discrimination is reverse discrimination against men | Substantive equality needs unequal treatment of unequals; the Nepal Supreme Court accepted this in 2004 |
| Its strands contradict each other (sameness vs. difference) | They are tools for different problems, not one theory |
| Too political to be "jurisprudence" | CLS and realism showed that all law involves value choices |

## 3.3 Postmodernism

### Meaning

Postmodernism is a broad movement in philosophy, art and literature from the late 20th century. It rejects the "modern" belief, inherited from the Enlightenment, that human reason can find universal, objective truths.

**Postmodern jurisprudence** applies this to law. It denies that there is one true theory of law (natural, positivist or any other). Law is seen as a text with many possible meanings, shaped by language and power.

Jean-François Lyotard gave the classic definition in *The Postmodern Condition* (1979): "I define postmodern as incredulity toward metanarratives." In plain words: postmodernism does not believe in grand stories that claim to explain everything.

### "Modern" vs. "postmodern" view of law

| Point | Modern view (older schools) | Postmodern view |
|---|---|---|
| Truth | Reason can find objective truth about law | No single objective truth; many truths from many standpoints |
| Grand theories | Natural law, positivism, Marxism each explain law as a whole | All grand theories are "metanarratives" and should be doubted |
| Meaning of legal texts | Fixed; the judge finds it | Unstable; every reading is an interpretation |
| Law and power | Law limits power | Law is itself a form of power |
| The individual | A free, rational, unified subject | Shaped by language, culture and power |
| Values | Universal | Local, plural, culture-bound |

### Main thinkers

| Thinker | Key work | Idea for law |
|---|---|---|
| **Jean-François Lyotard** | *The Postmodern Condition* (1979) | Distrust of metanarratives; prefer small, local stories |
| **Jacques Derrida** | *Of Grammatology* (1967); "Force of Law" (1990) | **Deconstruction**: every text contains hidden oppositions (man/woman, reason/emotion) where one side is favoured. Read legal texts to expose them. Law and justice are not the same thing |
| **Michel Foucault** | *Discipline and Punish* (1975) | Power and knowledge go together. Power works not only through the sovereign's commands but through institutions (prisons, schools, hospitals) that "discipline" people |
| **Richard Rorty** | *Philosophy and the Mirror of Nature* (1979) | Knowledge does not mirror reality; it is a matter of what a community agrees on |
| **Stanley Fish** | *Is There a Text in This Class?* (1980) | "Interpretive communities": the meaning of a legal text depends on the community of readers, such as judges and lawyers |

Derrida's line "there is nothing outside the text" is often quoted to mean that we can only reach reality through language and interpretation.

### Main features of postmodern jurisprudence

1. **Rejection of grand theory.** No single school can explain law. Each is one partial story.
2. **Anti-foundationalism.** Law has no firm base, such as God, reason, a Grundnorm or a sovereign, that stands outside human interpretation.
3. **Law as text and language.** Legal meaning is made through language, and language is unstable.
4. **Deconstruction.** Legal concepts rest on paired opposites (public/private, reasonable/unreasonable). Exposing them shows whose view the law favours.
5. **Law as power.** Following Foucault, law is one way society controls and shapes people.
6. **Pluralism and difference.** Respect for many cultures, local norms and minority voices. Distrust of one uniform law imposed from the centre.

### How it differs from CLS

| Point | Critical legal studies | Postmodernism |
|---|---|---|
| Origin | US law schools, 1977 | European (mainly French) philosophy, 1960s-70s, later applied to law |
| Focus | Law serves powerful classes | No single truth; meaning and power in language |
| Politics | Clearly left-wing, aims at social change | Often avoids any fixed political programme |
| Key method | Showing indeterminacy, "trashing" | Deconstruction, genealogy of power |

### Criticism of postmodernism

| Criticism | Explanation |
|---|---|
| Relativism | If no view is truer than another, we cannot say slavery or caste discrimination is wrong |
| Self-refuting | "There is no universal truth" is itself a claim to universal truth |
| No positive programme | It criticises but offers no workable theory of law or justice |
| Obscure language | Its writing is hard to follow, even for lawyers |
| Undermines rights | Human rights rely on universal claims that postmodernism doubts |

Supporters reply that it has made law more humble and more open to minority voices.

### Nepal angle

- Article 3 of the Constitution describes the Nepali nation as multi-ethnic, multi-lingual, multi-religious and multi-cultural. This accepts plurality rather than one uniform culture.
- Section 70(2) of the Muluki Civil Code 2074 allows marriage between relatives where the practices of the couple's ethnic community or clan allow it. This is legal pluralism: local norms are respected inside national law.
- In **Sunil Babu Pant v. Nepal Government** (2007), the Supreme Court separated sex from gender and recognised a third gender. This fits the postmodern idea that categories such as "man" and "woman" are not fixed by nature alone.

These are written the way you would write them in the exam. Each takes about 35 minutes by hand. Learn the structure and the key sentences; you do not need to memorise every word.

??? example "Q1. Assess the impact of feminist jurisprudence in Nepalese legal system. (2080 Q2, 20 marks)"
    **Introduction**

    Feminist jurisprudence is the philosophy of law based on the political, economic and social equality of the sexes. It studies how law has kept women in a lower position and how law can be used to end that inequality. Its central claim is that law, though it looks neutral, was built on men's experience. Catharine MacKinnon wrote that "the state is male in the feminist sense: the law sees and treats women the way men see and treat women." Simone de Beauvoir's statement that "one is not born, but rather becomes, a woman" shows that gender roles are made by society, and so can be changed by law.

    **Development and main strands**

    The *first wave* (late 1700s to early 1900s) demanded the vote and property rights for women. Mary Wollstonecraft's *A Vindication of the Rights of Woman* (1792) and J.S. Mill's *The Subjection of Women* (1869) are its key texts. The *second wave* (1960s-80s) attacked discrimination at work, in the family and in sexual life. The *third wave* (from the 1990s) stressed differences among women; Kimberlé Crenshaw called this intersectionality.

    Feminist legal thought has several strands:

    1. **Liberal feminism:** women and men are equally rational and should be treated the same.
    2. **Cultural feminism:** Carol Gilligan's *In a Different Voice* (1982) argued that women's "ethic of care" is as valid as men's ethic of rights.
    3. **Radical feminism:** MacKinnon argued that the core problem is male domination, seen in rape, sexual harassment and pornography.
    4. **Socialist feminism:** women's oppression comes from both capitalism and patriarchy.

    **Key ideas**

    Feminists attack the **public/private divide**, which kept violence inside the family out of the law. They distinguish **formal equality** (the same rule for all) from **substantive equality** (equal results), and so support **positive discrimination**. Katharine Bartlett's method of "asking the woman question" asks how a rule affects women's lives. At the international level, the Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Discrimination against Women (CEDAW), 1979, is the main instrument. Nepal ratified it in 1991.

    **Impact on Nepal's legal system**

    *1. Constitution of Nepal (2015).*

    The Constitution reflects feminist ideas more fully than any earlier Nepali constitution.

    - Article 18(3) bans discrimination on grounds including sex, but allows special laws for the protection, empowerment or advancement of women. This is positive discrimination.
    - Article 18(4) bans gender discrimination in pay for the same work and in social security.
    - Article 18(5) gives all family members an equal right to parental property without gender discrimination.
    - Article 38 is a separate fundamental right for women. It guarantees equal right to lineage, safe motherhood and reproductive health, and freedom from physical, mental, sexual and psychological violence. It also gives women a right to take part in all state bodies on the basis of proportional inclusion, special opportunity in education, health, employment and social security, and equal rights of spouses in property and family affairs.
    - Article 11(2)(b) allows citizenship by descent through the father **or** the mother.
    - Representation is guaranteed. At least one third of each party's members in the Federal Parliament must be women (Article 84(8)). At least three of the eight National Assembly members from each province must be women (Article 86(2)(a)). Either the Speaker or the Deputy Speaker must be a woman (Article 91(2)). The President and Vice-President must be of different gender or communities (Article 70).
    - Article 252 establishes the National Women Commission.

    *2. Muluki Civil Code 2074.*

    - The minimum marriage age is 20 for both men and women (section 70(1)(d)), and marriage must rest on free consent (section 68).
    - Section 205 makes the husband, wife, father, mother, son and daughter coparceners. Section 206 gives each coparcener an equal share. A daughter's right to parental property no longer depends on her marriage.
    - A married woman may keep her father's or mother's surname (section 81).
    - A wife may divorce her husband without his consent if he marries again, expels her, harms her, or rapes her (section 95).

    *3. Criminal law and other Acts.*

    Section 219(4) of the Muluki Criminal Code 2074 makes marital rape an offence. Other laws include the Gender Equality Act 2063 (2006), the Domestic Violence (Crime and Punishment) Act 2066 (2009), the Human Trafficking and Transportation (Control) Act 2064 (2007), the Sexual Harassment at Workplace (Prevention) Act 2071 (2015) and the Safe Motherhood and Reproductive Health Rights Act 2075 (2018).

    *4. Role of the Supreme Court.*

    Women lawyers used public interest litigation to bring feminist arguments to the Supreme Court. The Court relied on the right to equality and on CEDAW.

    - In 1995, on a petition against discriminatory inheritance rules, the Court directed the government to draft a bill on women's property rights. The Muluki Ain (Eleventh Amendment), passed in 2002, followed.
    - In *Meera Dhungana (FWLD) v. HMG* (2059 B.S./2002), the Court held that a husband who rapes his wife cannot be immune from criminal law. It issued a directive order to bring a law on marital rape.
    - In *Meera Dhungana and Others v. Office of the Prime Minister* (2004), it struck down the rule that allowed a man to take a second wife if there was no child within ten years, and held that positive discrimination for women is constitutional.
    - In *Punyabati Pathak v. Ministry of Foreign Affairs* (2005), it struck down the rule that women under 35 needed a guardian's consent to get a passport.
    - In *Sapana Pradhan Malla v. Office of the Prime Minister* (2007), it issued guidelines to keep secret the identity of women victims in rape, trafficking and similar cases.
    - In *Jit Kumari Pangeni v. Prime Minister* (2008), it held that a lighter punishment for marital rape than for other rape was discriminatory.
    - In *Lakshmi Dhikta v. Government of Nepal* (2009), it directed the government to make abortion services affordable and to enact a comprehensive law.

    **Critical view**

    The impact is real but incomplete.

    - Under Article 11(7), a child of a Nepali mother and a foreign father can generally get only naturalised citizenship, while the child of a Nepali father gets citizenship by descent.
    - Marital rape carries a maximum of five years with no minimum, lower than other rape.
    - Dowry, child marriage, chhaupadi and witchcraft accusations continue despite legal bans.
    - Women still own little land, and most women in Parliament come through proportional lists rather than direct election.
    - The Supreme Court has often issued directive orders rather than striking laws down, and the government has been slow to act on them.
    - From the critics' side, some see positive discrimination as unfair to men. Intersectional feminists add that benefits have gone mostly to elite women, not to Dalit or rural women.

    **Conclusion**

    Feminist jurisprudence has changed Nepal's law from a patriarchal code to one that formally promises gender equality. Its influence is clear in Articles 18 and 38 of the Constitution, in the equal coparcenary and marriage rules of the Civil Code, in the criminalisation of marital rape, and in a series of Supreme Court decisions. The remaining task is substantive equality: closing the gap between equal words in the law and the unequal lives of many Nepali women. Better enforcement, removal of the remaining discriminatory provisions and attention to the most marginalised women are the next steps.

??? example "Q2. What is critical legal studies? Discuss its origin, main ideas and criticism. (likely question, 20 marks)"
    **Introduction**

    Critical legal studies (CLS) is a movement in legal thought that began in the United States in the late 1970s. Its central claim is that **law is politics**. Law is not a neutral, objective system of rules applied by logic. It is the product of political choices, and it serves and legitimises the interests of the powerful. CLS writers try to expose these hidden choices.

    Mark Kelman, in *A Guide to Critical Legal Studies* (1987), described the movement as an attack on the claim that liberal law is coherent and neutral. Roberto Unger's *The Critical Legal Studies Movement* (1983) is its best-known statement.

    **Origin**

    The movement took shape at the Conference on Critical Legal Studies at the University of Wisconsin-Madison in 1977. Its leading figures were Duncan Kennedy, Roberto Mangabeira Unger, Morton Horwitz, Mark Tushnet and David Trubek. Harvard Law School became its main centre in the 1980s.

    CLS had four roots:

    1. **American legal realism.** Holmes and Frank showed that judges do not simply find the law. CLS went further and said the judge's choices are political.
    2. **Marxism.** Law serves the dominant class. CLS used this idea but did not reduce everything to economics.
    3. **The Frankfurt School.** Its "critical theory" showed how ideas can hide power.
    4. **The 1960s.** The civil rights and anti-war movements made young lawyers distrust official claims that the system was fair.

    **Main ideas**

    *1. Law is politics.* Legal decisions are value choices. The language of legal reasoning only makes them look necessary.

    *2. Indeterminacy.* Legal rules do not fix one right answer. For almost every rule there is a counter-rule: freedom of contract against protection of the weaker party, or the right to property against the public interest. A judge can reach opposite results with equally "legal" arguments, so the result depends on the judge's values.

    For example, suppose a landlord and a poor tenant sign a harsh lease. One line of legal argument says the court must enforce what free adults agreed. Another says the court must protect a weaker party from unfair terms. Both are accepted legal principles. Whichever the judge picks, the judgment will read as if the law itself demanded it. CLS says the real choice was political.

    *3. Legitimation.* Law makes an unequal society look fair. Because the law speaks of equal rights, people accept existing inequality as natural or deserved. Law's real work is to make domination acceptable.

    *4. Hierarchy.* Law and legal education reproduce social hierarchy. In *Legal Education and the Reproduction of Hierarchy* (1982), Duncan Kennedy argued that law schools train students to accept the existing order and their own place in it.

    *5. The fundamental contradiction.* Kennedy argued that liberal law rests on a conflict between individual freedom and the need for community. Law cannot resolve it, and moves between the two as it suits.

    *6. Critique of rights.* Writers such as Mark Tushnet argued that formal rights are unstable and can distract people from real political struggle.

    *7. Trashing and deconstruction.* CLS writers take respected doctrines and show that they are inconsistent or biased. They borrowed deconstruction from postmodern philosophy.

    **Offshoots**

    CLS influenced feminist jurisprudence, which applied its critique to gender, and critical race theory, which applied it to race (Derrick Bell, Kimberlé Crenshaw). It also shares much with postmodern legal theory.

    **CLS compared with realism and Marxism**

    | Point | Legal realism | Marxism | CLS |
    |---|---|---|---|
    | What decides cases | The judge's personal reactions to facts | Class interest | Political and ideological choices |
    | Source of bias | Individual judges | Economic base | The whole liberal legal order |
    | Aim | Describe how courts work | Overthrow capitalism | Expose and change hidden hierarchy |

    So CLS is broader than realism, because it attacks the whole structure of liberal law and not just the judge. It is looser than Marxism, because it accepts that law has some independence from economics.

    **Contribution**

    CLS made lasting contributions. It forced lawyers to admit that legal reasoning involves value choices. It brought social inequality into the centre of legal theory. It made legal education more self-critical. And it opened the way for feminist and race-based critiques that later changed real law.

    **Criticism**

    1. **Nihilism.** CLS destroys faith in law but offers no alternative. Critics said it left nothing to build on.
    2. **Overstated indeterminacy.** Most cases are easy and predictable. Uncertainty arises only in a small number of hard cases.
    3. **Self-defeating.** If all legal argument is politics, CLS's own arguments are politics too, and have no special claim to truth.
    4. **Attack on rights.** Critical race theorists such as Patricia Williams replied that rights matter greatly to people who have never had them. Dropping rights talk would hurt the weak.
    5. **Short life.** As an organised movement, CLS faded by the 1990s, though its ideas survive in its offshoots.

    **Relevance to Nepal**

    Nepal's history shows the CLS point clearly. The Muluki Ain of 1910 B.S. wrote caste hierarchy into law; punishments differed by caste. That law looked like a neutral code but protected the dominant groups. Modern Nepal has responded in ways CLS would recognise. The Constitution of 2015 adopts proportional inclusion: Article 42(1) gives backward women, Dalits, indigenous nationalities, Madhesi and other listed groups the right to take part in state bodies on that basis. The Supreme Court has opened its doors through public interest litigation, which has allowed hidden discrimination in "neutral" laws to be challenged, as in the gender equality cases.

    **Conclusion**

    Critical legal studies showed that law is never fully neutral and that legal reasoning can hide political choices. It deepened the realist insight and gave rise to feminist jurisprudence and critical race theory. But it went too far in claiming that law is wholly indeterminate, and it offered little to replace what it criticised. Its lasting value lies in the habit it teaches: always ask whose interests a law really serves.

??? example "Q3. Explain the main trends in modern jurisprudence. (likely question, 20 marks)"
    **Introduction**

    The classical schools of jurisprudence (natural, historical, analytical, sociological, realist and socialist) each tried to answer "what is law?". Most of them assumed that law could be studied as a fairly rational, neutral and universal system. From the 1970s, new trends challenged this assumption. The three main trends are critical legal studies, feminist jurisprudence and postmodernism. They are called "trends" rather than schools because they are loose movements whose members often disagree. What they share is a critical attitude: they ask whose interests the law really serves and whose voices it leaves out.

    **Why new trends arose**

    Several changes after the Second World War pushed jurisprudence in this direction:

    1. Legal realism had already shown that judges do more than apply rules mechanically.
    2. The civil rights, anti-war and women's movements of the 1960s made people doubt that the legal system was fair to everyone.
    3. Women, racial minorities and other groups entered law schools and the legal profession in larger numbers. They brought experiences that the older theories had ignored.
    4. In philosophy, French thinkers questioned whether reason could ever reach neutral, universal truth.

    The result was a move from asking "what is law?" to asking "whom does law serve?".

    **1. Critical legal studies**

    Critical legal studies (CLS) began at a conference at the University of Wisconsin-Madison in 1977. Its leading writers were Duncan Kennedy, Roberto Unger, Morton Horwitz and Mark Tushnet. Its slogan is **"law is politics"**.

    Its main ideas are:

    - **Indeterminacy:** legal rules do not fix one answer, so judges decide on the basis of values.
    - **Legitimation:** law makes an unequal society look fair.
    - **Hierarchy:** law and legal education reproduce social hierarchy.
    - **Critique of rights:** formal rights can hide real inequality.

    It drew on legal realism and Marxism.

    *Criticism:* it is nihilistic, overstates uncertainty, and offers no alternative.

    **2. Feminist jurisprudence**

    Feminist jurisprudence studies how law treats women and seeks equality of the sexes. It argues that law is not gender-neutral but was built on men's experience. Catharine MacKinnon wrote that "the state is male in the feminist sense".

    Its strands are:

    - **Liberal feminism:** treat women the same as men.
    - **Cultural feminism:** Carol Gilligan's "ethic of care".
    - **Radical feminism:** MacKinnon's focus on male domination.
    - **Socialist feminism:** oppression comes from both capitalism and patriarchy.

    Its key ideas are patriarchy, the public/private divide, substantive equality and positive discrimination. Its method, in Katharine Bartlett's words, is "asking the woman question". At the international level it is reflected in CEDAW (1979).

    *Criticism:* it assumes one "women's experience" when women differ by class, race and caste. Intersectional feminism (Crenshaw) answers this.

    **3. Postmodernism**

    Postmodernism rejects the modern belief that reason can find universal, objective truth. Lyotard defined it as "incredulity toward metanarratives".

    Its key thinkers are Derrida (deconstruction), Foucault (power and knowledge), Rorty and Stanley Fish. In law it means:

    - No grand theory, whether natural law or positivism, can explain law fully.
    - Law is a text whose meaning is unstable.
    - Legal categories hide power.
    - Plural, local norms deserve respect.

    *Criticism:* it leads to relativism, it is self-refuting, and it offers no positive programme.

    **Common features of the three trends**

    1. **Anti-formalism.** All three reject the idea that law is a closed, logical system of rules.
    2. **Focus on power.** All three see law as tied to power: of a class, of men, or of dominant ideas.
    3. **Voice for the excluded.** All three speak for groups the older theories ignored: the poor, women, minorities.
    4. **Doubt about neutrality.** All three deny that law is neutral between groups.
    5. **Link with practice.** Especially in feminist jurisprudence, theory is tied to concrete law reform.

    **Other modern trends**

    Examiners may also credit brief mention of related movements:

    - **Critical race theory**, an offshoot of CLS that studies race.
    - **Law and economics**, which judges legal rules by their efficiency.
    - The **revival of natural law** by Lon Fuller and John Finnis, and the rise of **human rights** after 1948.

    **Comparison**

    | Point | CLS | Feminist | Postmodern |
    |---|---|---|---|
    | Main claim | Law is politics | Law reflects a male view | No single truth |
    | Who is hidden | The weak and poor | Women | Minorities, "the other" |
    | Method | Indeterminacy | Woman question | Deconstruction |
    | Thinkers | Kennedy, Unger | MacKinnon, Gilligan | Lyotard, Derrida, Foucault |

    **Nepal's position**

    All three trends can be seen in Nepal's law.

    - **CLS:** the old Muluki Ain wrote caste hierarchy into law. The 2015 Constitution answers this with proportional inclusion (Article 42) and public interest litigation.
    - **Feminist jurisprudence:** this trend has had the strongest effect. Article 18(5) gives equal rights to parental property. Article 38 sets out women's rights, including freedom from violence and proportional inclusion. Article 84(8) requires one-third women in the Federal Parliament. Sections 205 and 206 of the Muluki Civil Code 2074 make sons and daughters equal coparceners, and section 219(4) of the Muluki Criminal Code 2074 punishes marital rape. The Supreme Court recognised marital rape as an offence in the *Meera Dhungana (FWLD)* case of 2002 and struck down discriminatory passport and second-marriage rules in 2005 and 2004.
    - **Postmodernism:** Article 3 describes Nepal as multi-ethnic, multi-lingual, multi-religious and multi-cultural. Section 70(2) of the Civil Code respects ethnic marriage customs. In *Sunil Babu Pant v. Nepal Government* (2007), the Supreme Court recognised a third gender, treating gender as more than a fixed biological fact.

    **Critical view**

    The new trends are better at criticism than construction. They show the hidden biases of law, but they do not offer a complete theory to replace the older schools. CLS and postmodernism, pushed too far, can weaken the rule of law and universal human rights. Feminist jurisprudence has been more practical because it pursued concrete legal reforms.

    **Conclusion**

    Modern trends in jurisprudence have moved the subject from asking "what is law?" to asking "whom does law serve?". Critical legal studies exposed the politics in law, feminist jurisprudence exposed its gender bias, and postmodernism exposed its claim to one truth. Nepal's Constitution of 2015, with its commitment to inclusion, gender equality and diversity, shows the practical influence of these ideas. A balanced jurisprudence keeps the insights of these trends while holding on to the stability and fairness that the classical schools sought.
