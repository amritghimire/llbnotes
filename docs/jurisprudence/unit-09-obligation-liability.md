# Unit 9: Obligation and Liability

!!! abstract "In one line"
    An **obligation** is a legal bond that ties one person to another to do something, usually pay or perform. **Liability** is what the law makes you suffer when you break a duty: either you repair the harm (civil, remedial) or you are punished (criminal, penal).

## Why it matters for the exam

- **Asked as a full 20-mark question:** 2080 Q5 "Differentiate between obligation and liability. Explain kinds of liability." It is written out in full below.
- **Asked as a short note:** 2078 Q7(a) "obligation".
- Likely long questions: mens rea (intention and negligence), strict and absolute liability, vicarious liability. Two are written out in full below.
- Examiners expect Nepal's law here. The Muluki Civil Code 2074 (National Civil Code) has a whole Part on obligations, contracts and torts. The Muluki Criminal Code 2074 (National Penal Code) grades crimes by intention, recklessness and negligence.

## Start here: a borrowed motorbike

Ram borrows Sita's motorbike and promises to return it on Sunday.

- From the moment he promises, Ram is under an **obligation** to Sita. A legal bond ties the two of them. Sita has a right; Ram has a matching duty.
- Ram does not return it. Now he has broken his duty. The law makes him **liable**: Sita can sue and the court will make him return the bike or pay its value. That is **civil** (remedial) liability.
- Suppose Ram sells the bike and keeps the money. Now the State can also prosecute him for a crime. That is **criminal** (penal) liability.
- Suppose Ram rides carelessly and knocks down a pedestrian. He had no contract with her, but he still owed her a duty of care. He is liable in **tort**. Whether he hit her on purpose (intention) or through carelessness (negligence) decides how serious his criminal liability is.

So: **obligation comes first, liability follows breach.** The rest of this unit fills in the details.

## 9.1 Obligation: meaning and kinds

### Meaning in plain words

In everyday speech "obligation" just means "duty". In law it has a narrower meaning. An obligation is a **bond between two particular persons**. One (the creditor or obligee) can demand something. The other (the debtor or obligor) must give or do it.

### Legal definitions

- **Justinian's Institutes (Roman law):** *obligatio est juris vinculum, quo necessitate adstringimur alicujus solvendae rei secundum nostrae civitatis jura*. In English: an obligation is a legal bond by which we are bound by the necessity of performing something according to the laws of our state. The key words are **vinculum juris**, "a chain of law".
- **Salmond:** an obligation, in its legal sense, is the *vinculum juris*, the bond of legal necessity that binds two or more determinate persons. It is a **proprietary right in personam**, or the duty that matches such a right. "Proprietary" means it has money value and forms part of the creditor's estate (property). "In personam" means it is good against a particular person, not against the whole world.
- **Muluki Civil Code 2074, section 493:** if a person is legally bound to do or not to do an act, an obligation is created when he does or omits that act.

### Features of an obligation (Salmond)

1. **Two determinate persons.** There is always a creditor (who holds the right) and a debtor (who owes the duty). They are known persons.
2. **Right in personam.** The right is enforceable only against the debtor, not against everyone. Ownership of land, by contrast, is a right *in rem*, good against the world.
3. **Proprietary.** It has money value. A debt owed to you is part of your property and can be transferred.
4. **A bond of legal necessity.** The debtor cannot escape it at will; the law will enforce it.
5. **A positive act is usually owed.** The debtor must pay, deliver or perform.

!!! tip "Every obligation is a duty, but not every duty is an obligation"
    Your duty not to hit anyone is owed to the whole world. That is a duty, not an obligation. Your duty to repay Rs 10,000 to Hari is owed to one known person and has money value. That is an obligation.

### Kinds of obligation by source (Salmond)

Salmond classifies obligations by **how they arise** into four kinds.

| Kind | How it arises | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Contractual** | From an agreement between the parties | Buyer must pay the price; seller must deliver the goods |
| **2. Delictal (tortious)** | From a civil wrong (tort, or *delict* in Roman law) | Driver who negligently injures a pedestrian must pay compensation |
| **3. Quasi-contractual** | Imposed by law, as if there were a contract, to prevent unjust gain. There is no real agreement | Money paid to you by mistake must be returned |
| **4. Innominate** | "Unnamed". A residual class that fits none of the three. Salmond's main example is the obligation of a trustee to the beneficiary | A trustee must manage trust property for the beneficiary |

**1. Contractual obligations.** These come from the free will of the parties. They agree to bind themselves. The law enforces the agreement because people must be able to rely on promises.

**2. Delictal obligations.** These come from a wrong. If you injure another person's body, property or reputation, the law creates an obligation to pay damages. The parties had no prior agreement; the wrong itself creates the bond.

**3. Quasi-contractual obligations.** "Quasi" means "as if". There is no agreement, but the law treats the situation as if there were one. The basis is the principle against **unjust enrichment**: no one should keep a benefit at another's cost without a legal reason. Examples: money paid by mistake; goods delivered to the wrong person; necessaries supplied to a person who cannot contract (a minor or a person of unsound mind); a finder of lost goods who spends money to keep them safe.

**4. Innominate obligations.** Salmond uses this as a catch-all for obligations that have no special name. The obligations of trustees are the leading example. Some writers also put here obligations created directly by statute, such as the obligation to pay maintenance.

**Roman law background.** Justinian divided obligations into four by source: *ex contractu* (from contract), *quasi ex contractu* (as if from contract), *ex delicto* (from a wrong) and *quasi ex delicto* (as if from a wrong). Salmond's scheme grows from this.

### Solidary obligations

Salmond also discusses **solidary obligations**: several debtors owe the same single thing to one creditor. The creditor can demand the whole from any one of them, but can get it only once. Three types:

| Type | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Several | Each debtor is bound separately, for the same thing |
| Joint | All debtors are bound together, as one obligation |
| Joint and several | Each is bound for the whole, and all are bound together; the creditor may sue one or all |

Joint wrongdoers (joint tortfeasors) are the common example. The Muluki Civil Code 2074, section 681 ("To bear liability jointly"), makes joint tortfeasors jointly and severally liable, in proportion to each one's share of fault. If the shares cannot be worked out, they bear it equally.

### Obligation in Nepal's law

The **Muluki Civil Code 2074, Part 5** ("Provisions Relating to Contract and Other Obligations") is built around obligation. This is the best Nepal angle for this unit.

- **Section 493:** where a person is legally bound to do or not do an act, doing or omitting it creates an obligation.
- **Section 494(1)** lists seven sources of obligation:
    1. a law,
    2. a contract,
    3. an indirect or quasi-contract (Chapter 15),
    4. unjust enrichment (Chapter 16),
    5. a unilateral commitment by a person to assume an obligation,
    6. an act treated as a tort (Chapter 17),
    7. an act treated as a quasi-tort, including liability for defective products (Chapter 18).
- **Section 500:** a person who fails or delays to fulfil an obligation must pay compensation for the actual loss caused. If the failure is by fraud, or done knowingly or recklessly, he must also compensate the loss so caused.
- **Section 504:** a contract is an agreement enforceable by law between two or more persons to do or not to do an act. Once concluded it creates a binding legal relationship.
- **Section 505(1):** a contract is enforceable if there is consent, capacity, a certain subject matter for the obligation, and a lawful obligation.
- **Section 664:** a person who gets a benefit from another without a lawful reason has an unjust enrichment, and bears an obligation under Chapter 16 to return it.

Notice how closely section 494 follows Salmond and Roman law: contract, quasi-contract, tort and quasi-tort are all there. Nepal adds unjust enrichment and unilateral commitment as separate heads.

## 9.2 Liability: meaning and kinds

### Meaning in plain words

Liability is the position of a person who has broken a duty and must now **answer for it** before the law. The law either makes him repair the harm or punishes him.

### Legal definitions

- **Salmond:** "Liability or responsibility is the bond of necessity that exists between the wrongdoer and the remedy of the wrong."
- **Markby:** liability describes the condition of a person who has a duty to perform. (Wider: it covers the duty itself, not only the result of breaking it.)
- In plain terms: *duty* tells you what you must do; *liability* tells you what happens to you if you do not.

### Obligation and liability: the difference

| Point | Obligation | Liability |
|---|---|---|
| Meaning | A bond of legal necessity between two determinate persons to do something | The bond between a wrongdoer and the remedy or punishment for the wrong |
| When it arises | Before any breach, from contract, law, quasi-contract, etc. (a delictal obligation arises from the wrong itself) | After a breach of duty or commission of a wrong |
| Nature | Primary: it says what must be done | Secondary: it says what follows if it is not done |
| Branch of law | Civil law only | Civil and criminal law |
| Parties | Always two known persons: creditor and debtor | Wrongdoer on one side; the injured person (civil) or the State (criminal) on the other |
| Kind of right | Always a right *in personam* | May follow breach of a right *in rem* (e.g. trespass, theft) as well as *in personam* |
| Money value | Always proprietary | Criminal liability (imprisonment) has no money value for the victim |
| Aim | Performance | Remedy (compensation, specific performance) or punishment |
| Scope | Narrower | Wider: every breached obligation leads to liability, but not every liability comes from an obligation |
| Nepal example | Civil Code ss. 493-494 (creation of obligation) | Civil Code ss. 500, 537, 682 (compensation); Criminal Code (punishment) |

!!! note "Where they overlap"
    Once a tort is committed, the wrongdoer's liability to pay damages is itself a **delictal obligation** to the victim. So liability can turn into an obligation. Salmond's point is that the two words describe different stages: the bond to perform, and the bond to answer for breach.

### Kinds of liability

The main division is **civil** and **criminal**. Salmond restates it as **remedial** and **penal**, because the real difference is the purpose of the proceedings.

#### 1. Civil and criminal liability

| Point | Civil liability | Criminal liability |
|---|---|---|
| Arises from | A civil wrong: breach of contract, tort, breach of trust | A crime |
| Proceedings | Civil proceedings, started by the injured person | Criminal proceedings, usually started by the State |
| Purpose | To enforce the plaintiff's right: compensation, return of property, specific performance, injunction | To punish the wrongdoer |
| Mens rea | Usually not needed; carelessness or even no fault may be enough | Usually needed: intention, recklessness or negligence |
| Can it be settled? | Yes, the parties may compromise | Generally no, it is the State's case (some minor offences can be compromised) |
| Nepal | Muluki Civil Code 2074 | Muluki Criminal Code 2074 |

The same act can create both. A driver who negligently kills a pedestrian faces a criminal charge (Criminal Code s. 182) and a civil claim for compensation (Civil Code s. 672).

#### 2. Remedial and penal liability (Salmond)

- **Remedial liability** aims to **enforce the right** of the injured person. The wrongdoer must do what he should have done, or pay its money value.
- **Penal liability** aims to **punish** the wrongdoer, to deter him and others.

They do not match civil and criminal exactly. Civil proceedings are mostly remedial, but they can be penal: exemplary (punitive) damages, or a fine recoverable in a civil action. Criminal proceedings are penal, but the court may also order compensation to the victim.

**Theory of remedial liability.** Salmond's general rule: wherever the law creates a duty, it should enforce that duty specifically. But there are three exceptions where a duty is not specifically enforced:

1. **Duties of imperfect obligation.** The law recognises the duty but gives no remedy, e.g. a debt barred by limitation.
2. **Duties that cannot be specifically enforced after breach.** Once a person has published a defamatory statement, you cannot "undo" it. The law gives damages instead.
3. **Duties where specific enforcement is possible but not expedient.** A court will not force a person to marry, or to perform a contract of personal service. Damages are given instead.

**Theory of penal liability.** Penal liability needs two things: an act (*actus reus*) and a guilty mind (*mens rea*). This is the maxim *actus non facit reum nisi mens sit rea*, "the act does not make a person guilty unless the mind is also guilty". The details follow below.

#### 3. Vicarious liability

**Meaning.** Normally a person is liable only for his own acts. Vicarious liability is liability for **another person's** wrong because of a relationship between the two. "Vicarious" means "in place of another".

**Bases (maxims).**

- *Qui facit per alium facit per se*: he who acts through another acts himself.
- *Respondeat superior*: let the superior (master) answer.

**Main cases of vicarious liability.**

| Relationship | Rule |
|---|---|
| Master and servant (employer and employee) | Employer is liable for wrongs done by the employee **in the course of employment** |
| Principal and agent | Principal is liable for the agent's wrongs within the scope of authority |
| Partners | Each partner is liable for wrongs of the others in the business of the firm |
| Parents and children (in Nepal by statute) | Parents liable for torts of young children (Civil Code s. 673) |

**Why the law allows it.** The employer gets the benefit of the work and so should bear its risks. He chooses and controls the employee. He is usually better able to pay, or to insure. And it pushes employers to take care in choosing and supervising staff.

**Leading case.** *Lloyd v Grace, Smith & Co* [1912] AC 716: a solicitors' firm was liable for the fraud of its clerk, done in the course of his work, even though the fraud was for the clerk's own benefit.

**In criminal law** vicarious liability is the exception, because punishment needs a guilty mind. It is found mainly in statutory offences, e.g. a licensee liable for a sale made by his servant.

**Nepal.**

- Civil Code **s. 673:** the father and mother living with a minor below fourteen (or whoever exercises parental authority) are liable for a tort committed by that minor.
- Civil Code **s. 674:** the guardian or curator of a person of unsound mind is liable for loss or damage caused by that person.
- Civil Code **s. 675(1):** a person, firm, company or organisation that employs a domestic helper, worker or employee is liable for loss caused by an act the employee does **in good faith in the course of the work**. Under **s. 675(2)** the employee is personally liable for acts done negligently or dishonestly.
- Criminal Code **s. 30:** when a firm, company or body corporate commits an offence, the person who did or caused the act is liable. If that person cannot be identified, liability falls on the owners or partners (firm), or the director, managing director or general manager who did or caused the act (company). If even they cannot be identified, the chief executive bears the criminal liability.

#### 4. Strict and absolute liability

**Meaning.** In some cases the law makes a person liable **without proof of fault**: he did not intend the harm and was not careless, yet he must pay or is punished. Salmond calls these wrongs of **absolute liability**. Modern writers split them into two:

- **Strict liability:** no fault needed, but some defences are allowed.
- **Absolute liability:** no fault needed and the usual defences are not allowed.

**Strict liability: *Rylands v Fletcher* (1868) LR 3 HL 330.** The defendant built a reservoir on his land. The water burst through old mine shafts and flooded the plaintiff's mine. The defendant had not been negligent, yet he was held liable. Blackburn J laid down the rule: "the person who for his own purposes brings on his lands and collects and keeps there anything likely to do mischief if it escapes, must keep it in at his peril, and, if he does not do so, is prima facie answerable for all the damage which is the natural consequence of its escape." The House of Lords added that the use of land must be **non-natural**.

Essentials:

1. A dangerous thing brought onto the land,
2. Non-natural use of land,
3. Escape of the thing,
4. Damage caused by the escape.

Defences (exceptions): act of God; plaintiff's own default; plaintiff's consent; act of a stranger (third party); statutory authority.

**Absolute liability: *M.C. Mehta v Union of India* (1987, Oleum gas leak case).** After the Bhopal gas disaster (1984) and an oleum gas leak from a Delhi factory, the Supreme Court of India held that an enterprise engaged in a **hazardous or inherently dangerous activity** is absolutely liable for harm caused by an accident in that activity. None of the *Rylands* exceptions apply. The amount of compensation should relate to the size and capacity of the enterprise, so that it deters.

| Point | Strict liability | Absolute liability |
|---|---|---|
| Leading case | *Rylands v Fletcher* (1868), England | *M.C. Mehta v Union of India* (1987), India |
| Fault needed? | No | No |
| Exceptions allowed? | Yes: act of God, plaintiff's fault, consent, act of stranger, statutory authority | No |
| Escape needed? | Yes, from the defendant's land | No; harm from the hazardous activity is enough |
| Non-natural use | Required | Replaced by "hazardous or inherently dangerous activity" |
| Damages | Compensatory | Compensatory, and large enough to deter, linked to the enterprise's capacity |

**Strict liability in criminal law.** Some statutory offences need no *mens rea*: food adulteration, selling liquor to a minor, traffic and pollution offences. The State's interest in public safety outweighs the ordinary rule. In *R v Prince* (1875) the accused was convicted of taking an unmarried girl under sixteen out of her father's possession, although he honestly believed she was eighteen.

**Why the law imposes it.** Some activities are so dangerous that whoever profits from them should bear the risk. The victim often cannot prove fault. And it pushes people to take maximum care.

**Nepal.**

- Criminal Code **s. 29:** in a strict liability offence under this Act or any other law, the court shall not examine whether the offence was committed intentionally.
- Civil Code **s. 676:** the owner of an animal kept by or under his control is liable for loss or damage it causes. He escapes if the animal was properly controlled with adequate safety measures, or if the loss came from the injured person's own act, default or negligence. This is strict liability with defences.
- Civil Code **ss. 685-687 (defective products):** a producer of a defective product is liable for loss to body, life or property caused by its consumption (s. 685). The claimant must prove the link between the defective product and the injury, and that the injury came solely from using it (s. 686). The producer escapes only on listed grounds, such as the product not being defective when sold, or the consumer's misuse (s. 687). This is no-fault liability with defences: strict, not absolute.

#### 5. Measure of liability

**Measure of civil liability.** Civil liability is measured by the **loss to the plaintiff**, not by the wrongdoer's guilt. A careless driver and a deliberate one pay the same damages for the same broken car. The aim is to put the injured person, as far as money can, back in the position he was in before.

- Civil Code **s. 537:** damages for breach of contract cover the actual loss, or loss the parties knew at the time of contract was likely to result. No compensation for indirect or remote loss.
- Civil Code **s. 682:** compensation for a tort is limited to actual loss; remote loss is not recoverable. If the injured person was also at fault (omission, recklessness or negligence), compensation is reduced. It may be paid in cash, kind or service.

**Measure of criminal liability (Salmond).** Punishment is measured by the purpose of deterrence. Salmond names three factors:

1. **Motive of the offence.** The stronger the temptation, the heavier the punishment needed to deter.
2. **Magnitude of the offence.** The greater the harm, the greater the punishment.
3. **Character of the offender.** A habitual offender deserves more than a first offender.

Modern law also weighs the mental state. Nepal's Criminal Code shows this clearly for causing death:

| Mental state | Section | Punishment |
|---|---|---|
| Intention (homicide) | s. 177 | Life imprisonment |
| Recklessness | s. 181 | 3 to 10 years and a fine of Rs 30,000 to 100,000 |
| Negligence | s. 182(1)-(2) | Up to 3 years and a fine up to Rs 30,000 |
| Pure accident, from an act less likely to cause death | s. 182(3) | 6 months to 2 years, or a fine up to Rs 20,000, or both |

### Mens rea: the conditions of penal liability

#### Meaning

*Mens rea* means "guilty mind". It is the **mental element** that the law requires before it punishes. *Actus reus* is the **physical element**: the act or omission and its result. Both must be present together.

Salmond identifies two forms of *mens rea*: **intention** and **negligence**. Modern law adds **recklessness**, which lies between them. Penal liability, in Salmond's words, rests on wrongful intent or culpable negligence.

#### Intention

**Salmond:** "Intention is the purpose or design with which an act is done. It is the foreknowledge of the act, coupled with the desire of it."

So intention has two parts: **knowing** what will happen, and **wanting** it to happen. A person is also taken to intend the natural and likely results of what he deliberately does.

**Intention and motive.**

- **Immediate intent** is the wrongful act itself (taking the money).
- **Ulterior intent**, or **motive**, is the further purpose behind it (to feed his family).
- In general **motive is irrelevant** to liability. A bad act with a good motive is still a wrong. A lawful act does not become wrong because the motive is bad: in *Bradford Corporation v Pickles* [1895] AC 587, a landowner who drained water from under his land to force the town to buy it was not liable, despite his bad motive.
- Motive does matter in some places: in measuring punishment, in defamation (malice), and in malicious prosecution.

**Malice.** In law "malice" often just means intention, "wrongful intention" without lawful excuse. In a narrower sense it means an improper motive such as spite.

**Nepal.** The Criminal Code defines homicide as **intentionally** killing (s. 177). The Explanation to s. 177 adds that if a person intentionally causes a hurt likely to cause death, and the victim dies later from it, he is treated as having killed.

#### Recklessness

The person **foresees the risk** of harm but does not want it, and goes ahead anyway, not caring. It is more blameworthy than negligence but less than intention. Nepal treats it as a separate grade: death by reckless act (Criminal Code s. 181) and hurt by reckless act (s. 195).

#### Negligence

**Salmond:** "Negligence is culpable carelessness." It is the failure to take the care that the law requires in the circumstances.

**Alderson B in *Blyth v Birmingham Waterworks Co* (1856):** "Negligence is the omission to do something which a reasonable man, guided upon those considerations which ordinarily regulate the conduct of human affairs, would do, or doing something which a prudent and reasonable man would not do."

**Two theories of negligence.**

| | Subjective theory | Objective theory |
|---|---|---|
| Supporters | Salmond, Austin | Pollock, and most modern tort law |
| Negligence is | A state of mind: indifference or carelessness about consequences | A kind of conduct: falling below the standard of a reasonable person |
| Test | What was in the defendant's mind | What a reasonable person would have done |
| Criticism | Hard to prove what was in someone's mind | Ignores the actual person's ability |

**Negligence as a tort.** In *Donoghue v Stevenson* [1932] AC 562 a woman fell ill after drinking ginger beer with a decomposed snail in it, bought by her friend. She had no contract with the manufacturer, but he was held to owe her a duty of care. Lord Atkin's "neighbour principle": "You must take reasonable care to avoid acts or omissions which you can reasonably foresee would be likely to injure your neighbour."

Elements of the tort of negligence: (1) a duty of care owed to the plaintiff, (2) breach of that duty, (3) damage caused by the breach.

**Nepal.** Civil Code **s. 672(1)** forbids causing loss to another's body, life, property or legally protected right or interest by "default, negligence or recklessness". Under s. 672(2), if there is no prior contract between the parties, such loss is a tort. Criminal Code **s. 182** punishes causing death by negligence and **s. 195** hurt by negligence.

#### Intention vs negligence

| Point | Intention | Negligence |
|---|---|---|
| Mind | Foresees and desires the result | Does not desire the result; fails to take care |
| Blame | Highest | Lower |
| Criminal law | Basis of most serious crimes (e.g. homicide, s. 177) | Liable only where the law says so (e.g. s. 182) |
| Civil law | Needed for some torts (deceit, malicious prosecution) | Enough for most torts |
| Salmond | Wrongful intent | Culpable carelessness |

#### When mens rea is not needed, or not a defence

Salmond lists cases where liability is imposed regardless of the actor's mind:

1. **Mistake of law.** *Ignorantia juris non excusat*: ignorance of the law is no excuse. Everyone is presumed to know the law. Nepal: proviso to Criminal Code **s. 8**, "no act done in ignorance of law shall be excused".
2. **Mistake of fact.** In civil law it is generally no defence: a person who takes another's goods believing them his own is still liable for conversion. In criminal law, an honest mistake of fact is usually a defence. Nepal: Criminal Code **s. 8** says an act done by a person who, by mistake of fact, in good faith believes he is bound or justified by law is not an offence.
3. **Inevitable accident.** An accident that could not have been avoided by reasonable care is generally a defence, except in cases of strict liability.
4. **Strict liability offences** (above).

**Incapacity removes mens rea.** Nepal's Criminal Code recognises that some people cannot form a guilty mind:

- **s. 13:** an act by a child below ten years of age is not an offence.
- **s. 14:** an act by a person who, because of unsoundness of mind, cannot know the nature, fault or consequence of the act is not an offence.

## Compare

| | Obligation | Civil liability | Criminal liability |
|---|---|---|---|
| Arises | From contract, law, quasi-contract, tort, etc. | From breach of a civil duty | From a crime |
| Owed to | A determinate creditor | The injured person | The State (society) |
| Aim | Performance | Remedy (compensation, specific performance) | Punishment |
| Mental element | Not relevant | Usually not needed | Usually needed (*mens rea*) |
| Nepal | Civil Code ss. 493-494 | Civil Code ss. 500, 537, 672, 682 | Criminal Code ss. 29, 177, 181, 182 |

| | Fault-based liability | Strict liability | Absolute liability | Vicarious liability |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Defendant's fault needed? | Yes (intention or negligence) | No | No | No fault of defendant; the other person's wrong is needed |
| Defences | Many | Limited (act of God, stranger, consent, etc.) | Practically none | Act outside course of employment |
| Example | Murder; negligent driving | *Rylands v Fletcher*; animal owner (s. 676) | *M.C. Mehta* (oleum gas) | Employer for employee (s. 675) |

    - **Nepal:** Civil Code s. 673 (parents for child under 14), s. 675 (employer for employee's good-faith acts in the course of work; employee personally liable for negligent or dishonest acts); Criminal Code s. 30 (body corporate).

??? question "Likely short note: Mens rea"
    - **Meaning:** "guilty mind", the mental element of a crime. With *actus reus* (guilty act), it makes up penal liability.
    - **Maxim:** *actus non facit reum nisi mens sit rea*.
    - **Forms (Salmond):** intention ("foreknowledge of the act, coupled with the desire of it") and negligence ("culpable carelessness"). Recklessness lies between.
    - **Exceptions:** strict liability offences; mistake of law no excuse.
    - **Nepal:** Criminal Code grades death by intention (s. 177, life imprisonment), recklessness (s. 181), negligence (s. 182). s. 29: intention not examined in strict liability offences. Children below 10 (s. 13) and persons of unsound mind (s. 14) cannot commit an offence.

??? question "Likely short note: Quasi-contract"
    - **Meaning:** an obligation imposed by law as if there were a contract, though the parties never agreed. Based on the principle against unjust enrichment.
    - **Salmond:** one of the four kinds of obligation (with contractual, delictal, innominate).
    - **Examples:** money paid by mistake; necessaries supplied to a person without capacity; finder of goods; payment by an interested person of another's debt.
    - **Nepal:** Civil Code s. 494(1) lists quasi-contract and unjust enrichment as sources of obligation. Chapter 15 (ss. 648-663) deals with indirect or quasi-contracts, Chapter 16 (ss. 664-671) with unjust enrichment. Under s. 664 a person who gets a benefit without lawful reason must return it.

## Full 20-mark answers

These are written the way you would write them in the exam. Each takes about 35 minutes by hand. Learn the structure and the key sentences; you do not need to memorise every word.

??? example "Q1. Differentiate between obligation and liability. Explain kinds of liability. (2080 Q5, 20 marks)"
    **Introduction**

    Obligation and liability are two basic legal concepts. Both describe a person's legal burden, so they are often confused. But they belong to different stages. An obligation tells a person what he must do. Liability tells him what will follow if he does not do it, or if he commits a wrong.

    **Meaning of obligation**

    In ordinary speech, obligation means duty. In law it has a narrower meaning. Justinian's Institutes define it as *juris vinculum*, a legal bond by which we are bound by the necessity of performing something according to the laws of our state. Salmond describes obligation as the *vinculum juris*, a bond of legal necessity between two or more determinate persons. It is a proprietary right *in personam*, or the duty corresponding to it.

    An obligation therefore has these features: it binds two known persons, a creditor and a debtor; the right is good only against the debtor (*in personam*); it has money value and forms part of the creditor's estate; and the law will enforce it.

    Salmond classifies obligations by source into four kinds: contractual (arising from agreement), delictal (arising from a tort), quasi-contractual (imposed by law to prevent unjust enrichment) and innominate (a residual class, such as the obligations of trustees).

    **Meaning of liability**

    Salmond says: "Liability or responsibility is the bond of necessity that exists between the wrongdoer and the remedy of the wrong." Liability arises when a person breaks a duty. The law then either forces him to repair the harm or punishes him. Markby uses the word more widely, for the condition of a person who has a duty to perform.

    **Difference between obligation and liability**

    | Basis | Obligation | Liability |
    |---|---|---|
    | Meaning | Bond between two determinate persons to perform something | Bond between a wrongdoer and the remedy for the wrong |
    | Stage | Primary; exists before any breach | Secondary; arises after a breach or wrong |
    | Field | Civil law only | Both civil and criminal law |
    | Parties | Creditor and debtor | Wrongdoer and the injured person or the State |
    | Right involved | Always *in personam* | Can follow breach of rights *in rem* as well |
    | Money value | Always proprietary | Criminal liability is not proprietary |
    | Object | Performance | Compensation, specific relief or punishment |
    | Scope | Narrower | Wider |

    The two can meet. When a tort is committed, the wrongdoer's liability to pay damages is itself a delictal obligation owed to the victim. But the concepts remain distinct: one is the bond to perform, the other is the bond to answer for breach.

    **Kinds of liability**

    *1. Civil and criminal liability.* Civil liability arises from a civil wrong: breach of contract, tort or breach of trust. It is enforced by civil proceedings, started by the injured person, to secure compensation, return of property or specific performance. Criminal liability arises from a crime. The State prosecutes, and the aim is punishment. One act may create both: a driver who negligently kills a pedestrian may be punished under the Criminal Code and also made to pay compensation under the Civil Code.

    *2. Remedial and penal liability.* Salmond states the division by purpose. Remedial liability enforces the right of the injured person. Penal liability punishes the wrongdoer. Civil proceedings are mostly remedial but may be penal, as with exemplary damages. Under Salmond's theory of remedial liability, every duty should be specifically enforced, except: duties of imperfect obligation (such as time-barred debts); duties that cannot be enforced after breach (such as not to defame); and duties whose specific enforcement is not expedient (such as a contract of personal service). Under the theory of penal liability, punishment needs both *actus reus* and *mens rea*: *actus non facit reum nisi mens sit rea*. *Mens rea* takes two forms, intention and negligence.

    *3. Vicarious liability.* This is liability for another's wrong because of a relationship, such as master and servant or principal and agent. It rests on the maxims *qui facit per alium facit per se* and *respondeat superior*. The employer is liable for wrongs done by the employee in the course of employment, as in *Lloyd v Grace, Smith & Co* (1912). In criminal law it is an exception.

    *4. Strict and absolute liability.* Here a person is liable without fault. In *Rylands v Fletcher* (1868), a person who brings a dangerous thing onto his land for non-natural use is liable if it escapes and causes damage, subject to defences such as act of God and act of a stranger. In *M.C. Mehta v Union of India* (1987), the Supreme Court of India went further. An enterprise carrying on a hazardous activity is absolutely liable, with none of those defences.

    *5. Measure of liability.* Civil liability is measured by the loss caused to the plaintiff, not by the defendant's guilt. Criminal liability, according to Salmond, depends on the motive, the magnitude of the offence and the character of the offender. It also depends on mental state: intentional wrongs are punished more heavily than negligent ones.

    **Nepal's position**

    - The Muluki Civil Code 2074, Part 5, deals with contract and other obligations. Section 493 provides that an obligation is created when a person legally bound to do or not do an act does or omits it. Section 494 lists the sources of obligation: law, contract, quasi-contract, unjust enrichment, unilateral commitment, tort and quasi-tort.
    - Civil liability: section 500 requires compensation for failure to fulfil an obligation; section 537 gives damages for breach of contract; section 672 defines a tort and section 682 limits compensation to actual loss, reduced for the victim's own fault.
    - Vicarious liability: section 673 (parents for a minor below fourteen) and section 675 (employer for employees acting in good faith in the course of work).
    - Strict liability: section 676 (owner of animals) and sections 685-687 (defective products).
    - Criminal liability: the Muluki Criminal Code 2074 grades causing death by intention (s. 177, life imprisonment), recklessness (s. 181) and negligence (s. 182). Section 29 removes the inquiry into intention for strict liability offences. Section 30 fixes criminal liability for offences by companies.

    **Critical view**

    The distinction is useful but not watertight. Liability to pay damages is itself an obligation, so the line between the two is one of stage rather than kind. The civil-criminal division is also blurred: civil courts can award punitive damages and criminal courts can order compensation. Strict and absolute liability depart from the principle that liability should follow fault. They are justified by the needs of an industrial society, but they can be harsh on careful defendants.

    **Conclusion**

    Obligation is the primary bond between two persons to perform something. Liability is the secondary bond between a wrongdoer and the remedy or punishment. Liability is wider: it is civil or criminal, remedial or penal, personal or vicarious, fault-based or strict. Nepal's Civil Code 2074 adopts this structure closely, especially in sections 493-494, while the Criminal Code 2074 grades criminal liability by mental state. Together they show that the concepts of Salmond and Roman law still shape Nepal's modern codes.

??? example "Q2. What is mens rea? Discuss intention and negligence as forms of mens rea. (likely question, 20 marks)"
    **Introduction**

    A person is not punished merely for causing harm. The law also asks with what state of mind he acted. This mental element is called *mens rea*, "guilty mind". The physical element, the act or omission and its result, is called *actus reus*. The basic rule of criminal law is the maxim *actus non facit reum nisi mens sit rea*: the act does not make a person guilty unless the mind is also guilty.

    Salmond explains that penal liability requires two conditions: the doing of an act, and *mens rea*. *Mens rea* takes two forms: wrongful intention and culpable negligence. Modern law adds recklessness, which lies between the two.

    **Meaning and importance of mens rea**

    *Mens rea* separates a crime from an accident. A doctor whose patient dies despite proper care and a poisoner both "cause death", but only the poisoner has a guilty mind. Requiring *mens rea* ensures that punishment falls on the blameworthy. It also allows the law to grade punishment: the more guilty the mind, the heavier the sentence.

    **Intention**

    *Meaning.* Salmond defines it: "Intention is the purpose or design with which an act is done. It is the foreknowledge of the act, coupled with the desire of it." Intention therefore has two parts: knowledge of what will happen and desire that it should happen. A person is also taken to intend the natural and probable results of his deliberate act.

    *Immediate and ulterior intent.* Salmond distinguishes the immediate intent, which is the wrongful act itself, from the ulterior intent, which is the further purpose. A thief's immediate intent is to take the money; his ulterior intent may be to buy food. The ulterior intent is called motive.

    *Motive is generally irrelevant.* A wrongful act is not excused by a good motive, and a lawful act is not made wrongful by a bad motive. In *Bradford Corporation v Pickles* (1895), a landowner drained underground water to force the town to buy his land. His motive was bad, but his act was lawful, so he was not liable. Motive matters only in some areas, such as malicious prosecution and the measure of punishment.

    *Malice.* In law, malice usually means wrongful intention without lawful excuse. In a narrower sense, it means an improper motive such as spite.

    **Recklessness**

    A reckless person foresees that his act may cause harm, does not desire it, but goes ahead anyway. He takes an unjustified risk. It is more blameworthy than negligence because he knew of the danger.

    **Negligence**

    *Meaning.* Salmond calls negligence "culpable carelessness". Alderson B in *Blyth v Birmingham Waterworks Co* (1856) gave the classic definition: "Negligence is the omission to do something which a reasonable man, guided upon those considerations which ordinarily regulate the conduct of human affairs, would do, or doing something which a prudent and reasonable man would not do."

    *Two theories.*

    - The **subjective theory** (Salmond, Austin) treats negligence as a state of mind: indifference to consequences, a failure to think about them.
    - The **objective theory** (Pollock and modern tort law) treats negligence as conduct: falling below the standard of a reasonable person. The court asks what a reasonable person would have done, not what was in the defendant's mind.

    Modern law follows the objective theory, because a state of mind is hard to prove.

    *Negligence as a tort.* In *Donoghue v Stevenson* (1932), Lord Atkin laid down the neighbour principle: "You must take reasonable care to avoid acts or omissions which you can reasonably foresee would be likely to injure your neighbour." The tort of negligence needs a duty of care, a breach of that duty and resulting damage.

    **Intention and negligence compared**

    | Point | Intention | Negligence |
    |---|---|---|
    | Mind | Foresees and desires the result | Does not desire it; fails to take due care |
    | Blame | Highest | Lower |
    | Criminal law | Basis of serious crimes | Punished only where law provides |
    | Civil law | Needed for some torts (deceit) | Enough for most torts |
    | Salmond | Wrongful intent | Culpable carelessness |

    **Exceptions to mens rea**

    - **Strict liability offences.** Some statutory offences, such as food adulteration, need no *mens rea*. In *R v Prince* (1875), the accused was convicted although he honestly believed the girl was over sixteen.
    - **Mistake of law.** *Ignorantia juris non excusat*: ignorance of law is no excuse.
    - **Mistake of fact.** In criminal law an honest mistake of fact is usually a defence, but in civil law it generally is not.

    **Nepal's position**

    The Muluki Criminal Code 2074 does not give a general definition of intention or negligence. It builds them into each offence, and grades punishment by mental state:

    - Section 177: intentionally causing death (homicide) is punishable with life imprisonment. If a person intentionally causes a hurt likely to cause death and the victim dies later from it, he is treated as having killed.
    - Section 181: causing death by a reckless act, three to ten years' imprisonment and a fine.
    - Section 182: causing death by a negligent act, up to three years and a fine. Accidental death from an act less likely to cause death is punished more lightly (s. 182(3)).
    - Section 195: hurt by a reckless or negligent act, with lighter punishment for negligence.
    - Section 29: in a strict liability offence, the court does not examine intention.
    - Section 8: an act done by mistake of fact in good faith is not an offence, but ignorance of law is no excuse.
    - Sections 13 and 14: a child below ten, and a person who cannot understand his act because of unsoundness of mind, commit no offence. They cannot form *mens rea*.

    In civil law, the Muluki Civil Code 2074, section 672, makes a person liable in tort for loss caused by "default, negligence or recklessness". Section 500 requires extra compensation where an obligation is broken by fraud, knowingly or recklessly.

    **Critical view**

    The requirement of *mens rea* protects the innocent, but it is hard to prove what was in a person's mind. Courts therefore infer intention from conduct, which brings the subjective test close to the objective one. Strict liability offences protect the public but may punish the morally innocent. The objective test of negligence may be unfair to a person who, through no fault of his own, lacks ordinary skill.

    **Conclusion**

    *Mens rea* is the mental element that turns a harmful act into a crime. Its two main forms, as Salmond explains, are intention (foreknowledge plus desire) and negligence (culpable carelessness), with recklessness between them. Nepal's Criminal Code 2074 follows this scheme closely. It punishes intentional killing with life imprisonment, reckless killing with three to ten years, and negligent killing with up to three years. It also recognises strict liability offences where intention is not examined.

??? example "Q3. Explain the theory of strict and absolute liability. How far does the law of Nepal recognise it? (likely question, 20 marks)"
    **Introduction**

    The general rule of law is "no liability without fault". A person is liable only if he acted intentionally or negligently. But in some cases the law makes a person liable even when he took all possible care. This is **liability without fault**. Salmond calls such wrongs wrongs of absolute liability. Modern law divides them into strict liability, where some defences are allowed, and absolute liability, where they are not.

    **Why the law imposes liability without fault**

    1. Some activities are so dangerous that harm can occur even with every care.
    2. The person who carries on the activity for profit should bear its risks.
    3. The victim often cannot prove fault, because the evidence lies with the defendant.
    4. It pushes people to take the highest care.
    5. In criminal law, public welfare offences (food, drugs, pollution, traffic) protect the public and would be unenforceable if intention had to be proved every time.

    **The rule of strict liability: *Rylands v Fletcher* (1868)**

    *Facts.* The defendant employed contractors to build a reservoir on his land. Beneath it were old mine shafts. When the reservoir was filled, water burst through the shafts and flooded the plaintiff's mine. The defendant himself was not negligent.

    *Rule.* Blackburn J held: "the person who for his own purposes brings on his lands and collects and keeps there anything likely to do mischief if it escapes, must keep it in at his peril, and, if he does not do so, is prima facie answerable for all the damage which is the natural consequence of its escape." The House of Lords affirmed this, adding the requirement of non-natural use of land.

    *Essentials.* (1) a dangerous thing, (2) brought onto the land for non-natural use, (3) which escapes, (4) causing damage.

    *Exceptions.*

    - Act of God: a natural event no human foresight could guard against.
    - Plaintiff's own default.
    - Plaintiff's consent.
    - Act of a stranger, over whom the defendant had no control.
    - Statutory authority.

    Because of these exceptions, the liability is called "strict", not "absolute".

    **The rule of absolute liability: *M.C. Mehta v Union of India* (1987)**

    After the Bhopal gas disaster of 1984, oleum gas leaked from a factory in Delhi in 1985. In public interest litigation, the Supreme Court of India held that the rule in *Rylands v Fletcher*, made in the nineteenth century, was not enough for hazardous industries. It laid down that:

    - An enterprise engaged in a hazardous or inherently dangerous activity is absolutely liable for harm caused by an accident in that activity.
    - None of the *Rylands* exceptions apply.
    - Escape from land and non-natural use are not required.
    - Compensation must relate to the size and capacity of the enterprise, so that it deters.

    **Strict and absolute liability compared**

    | Point | Strict liability | Absolute liability |
    |---|---|---|
    | Source | *Rylands v Fletcher* (1868) | *M.C. Mehta v Union of India* (1987) |
    | Fault | Not required | Not required |
    | Defences | Allowed | Not allowed |
    | Escape | Required | Not required |
    | Activity | Non-natural use of land | Hazardous or inherently dangerous activity |
    | Damages | Compensatory | Compensatory and deterrent |

    **Strict liability in criminal law**

    Criminal law normally needs *mens rea*. But many statutory offences are of strict liability: selling adulterated food, selling liquor to minors, many traffic and environmental offences. In *R v Prince* (1875), a man was convicted of taking a girl under sixteen from her father, though he honestly believed she was eighteen. Such offences usually carry light penalties and protect public welfare.

    **Nepal's position**

    *Criminal law.* The Muluki Criminal Code 2074, section 29, provides that in a strict liability offence under this Act or any other law, the court shall not examine whether the offence was committed intentionally. This expressly recognises strict criminal liability. Which offences are of strict liability is left to the particular law.

    *Civil law.* The Muluki Civil Code 2074 recognises liability without fault in several places:

    - **Animals (s. 676).** The owner of an animal kept by or under his control is liable for loss or damage it causes. He is not liable if the animal was properly controlled with adequate safety measures, or if the loss came from the injured person's own act, default or negligence. This is strict liability with limited defences.
    - **Defective products (ss. 685-687).** A producer who makes, sells or distributes a defective product is liable for loss to body, life or property caused by its use (s. 685). The claimant need not prove the producer's negligence, only the link between the defective product and the injury, and that the injury came solely from using it (s. 686). The producer is discharged only on grounds listed in section 687, such as the product not being defective at sale, misuse by the consumer, or lack of scientific knowledge of the defect at the time. This is strict, not absolute, liability.
    - **Quasi-tort (s. 494(1)(g)).** The Code treats liability for defective products and other acts "deemed as a quasi-tort" as a separate source of obligation. This shows that Nepal recognises liability without proof of fault as a category of its own.
    - The same Chapter also has separate sections fixing liability on a house-owner for loss caused by the collapse of the house (s. 677) and on a property owner for loss from listed hazards (s. 678): explosion of a machine or engine, fire or explosion of inflammable or explosive substances (both where safety measures were not properly taken), excess smoke or noise from an industry, a fallen tree on a road, and discharge of toxic or infected substances.

    Nepal has no statute that adopts the *M.C. Mehta* rule of absolute liability for hazardous industries in the same terms. Nepali courts may draw on it as a persuasive Indian precedent.

    **Critical view**

    Supporters say liability without fault is fair in an industrial society. The person who creates the risk and earns the profit should bear the loss, not the innocent victim. Critics say it punishes people who did nothing morally wrong, and in criminal law it conflicts with the principle of *mens rea*. Absolute liability may also discourage useful but risky industries. A balance is found by keeping strict criminal liability for minor regulatory offences with light penalties, and by allowing narrow defences in civil cases.

    **Conclusion**

    Strict liability, from *Rylands v Fletcher*, makes a person liable without fault for the escape of dangerous things, subject to defences. Absolute liability, from *M.C. Mehta*, removes those defences for hazardous enterprises. Nepal recognises strict liability expressly in criminal law (Criminal Code s. 29) and in civil law for animals and defective products (Civil Code ss. 676, 685-687). These show that Nepal's law, like modern law elsewhere, accepts that fault is not the only basis of liability.

## One-minute revision

- **Obligation:** *vinculum juris* (Justinian); Salmond: proprietary right *in personam*. Two determinate persons, money value. Kinds: contractual, delictal, quasi-contractual, innominate. Solidary: several, joint, joint and several.
- **Nepal obligation:** Civil Code s. 493 (creation), s. 494 (seven sources), s. 500 (compensation for failure), s. 504-505 (contract), s. 664 (unjust enrichment).
- **Liability:** Salmond: "bond of necessity between the wrongdoer and the remedy of the wrong". Obligation is primary; liability is secondary, after breach, and wider.
- **Kinds:** civil/criminal; remedial/penal (Salmond's three exceptions to specific enforcement); vicarious (*respondeat superior*; Civil Code ss. 673, 675; Criminal Code s. 30); strict (*Rylands*) and absolute (*M.C. Mehta*).
- **Measure:** civil = actual loss (Civil Code ss. 537, 682); criminal = motive, magnitude, character (Salmond) plus mental state.
- **Mens rea:** *actus non facit reum nisi mens sit rea*. Intention = foreknowledge plus desire; negligence = culpable carelessness (*Blyth*; *Donoghue*). Motive generally irrelevant (*Pickles*).
- **Nepal criminal:** s. 177 intention (life), s. 181 recklessness (3-10 yrs), s. 182 negligence (up to 3 yrs); s. 29 strict liability; s. 8 mistake of fact, ignorance of law no excuse; s. 13 child under 10; s. 14 unsound mind.
- **Nepal strict civil:** s. 676 animals; ss. 685-687 defective products.
