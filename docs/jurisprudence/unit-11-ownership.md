# Unit 11: Ownership

!!! abstract "In one line"
    Ownership is the fullest right a person can have over a thing: to possess it, use it, enjoy its income, give it away and leave it to heirs, for an unlimited time. This unit covers its meaning, its kinds, how it is gained, kept and lost, and how it differs from possession.

## Why it matters for the exam

- **Asked as a full 20-mark question:** 2080 Q6 "Explain classification of ownership. Discuss Nepalese legal provision on acquisition, continuation and termination of ownership."
- The question asks for **Nepal's law**, not only theory. Learn the section numbers of the Muluki Civil Code 2074 given below (Sections 251-275 and 413-421), and Article 25 of the Constitution.
- Likely questions: "Define ownership and distinguish it from possession"; "Explain the incidents of ownership". Likely short notes: trust and beneficial ownership, vested and contingent ownership, co-ownership, adverse possession.

## Start here: what does "I own it" mean?

Ram buys a motorbike. He rides it (use). He keeps it in his yard (possession). He rents it to a friend for a week and takes the rent (income). He sells it next year (disposal). If he dies, it goes to his son (inheritance). No end date is fixed on any of this.

That bundle of powers is **ownership**.

Now Ram lends the bike to Shyam for a month. Shyam has the bike and rides it. Shyam has **possession**. Ram is still the **owner**. Ownership is a legal relation; possession is a fact on the ground.

Hold this picture. The whole unit grows from it.

## 11.1 Meaning and subject matter of ownership

### Meaning in plain words

To own a thing is to have the **best and most complete right** over it that the law allows. Others may have smaller rights over the same thing: a tenant, a borrower, a lender who took it as security. The owner's right is the one left over when all the smaller rights end.

### Definitions by jurists

**Austin.** Ownership is "a right — indefinite in point of user — unrestricted in point of disposition — and unlimited in point of duration — over a determinate thing".

This gives three marks:

| Mark | Plain meaning |
|---|---|
| Indefinite in user | The owner may use the thing in any lawful way. The uses are not listed in advance |
| Unrestricted in disposition | The owner may sell, give away, mortgage or destroy it |
| Unlimited in duration | The right has no end date. It passes to heirs |
| Over a determinate thing | It is over a specific, identified thing |

*Criticism:* no owner today has unlimited use or disposition. Planning laws, tax, land ceilings and the rights of neighbours all limit the owner. Austin also limits ownership to "things", but people own debts, shares and copyrights too.

**Salmond.** "Ownership, in its most comprehensive signification, denotes the relation between a person and any right that is vested in him." For Salmond, what a person owns is always a **right**, not a thing. When we say "Ram owns the land", we really mean Ram owns the **right of ownership over the land**. This lets Salmond fit debts, shares and patents into ownership.

**Holland.** Ownership is "plenary control over an object". Plenary means full.

**Hibbert.** Ownership includes four kinds of rights: the right to use the thing, the right to exclude others from it, the right to dispose of it, and the right to destroy it.

**Dias and Paton** treat ownership as the **residuary** right: the owner has whatever is left after the smaller rights of others are taken out.

### Incidents of ownership (what an owner can do)

"Incidents" means the rights, powers and duties that come with ownership.

**Salmond's incidents** (as summarised in most textbooks):

1. The owner has a right to **possess** the thing.
2. The owner has a right to **use and enjoy** it.
3. The owner has a right to **consume, destroy or alienate** (transfer) it.
4. Ownership is **indeterminate in duration**: it has no fixed end.
5. Ownership has a **residuary** character: when lesser rights such as a lease end, the full right returns to the owner.

**Honoré's eleven incidents.** A.M. Honoré, in his essay "Ownership" (1961), listed the standard incidents of "full liberal ownership". No single one is essential, but together they make up ownership:

| No. | Incident | Plain meaning |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Right to possess | To have exclusive physical control |
| 2 | Right to use | To use it personally |
| 3 | Right to manage | To decide how and by whom it is used |
| 4 | Right to the income | To take rent, crops, profits |
| 5 | Right to the capital | To sell, give away, consume or destroy it |
| 6 | Right to security | Protection from being taken away without consent or compensation |
| 7 | Transmissibility | It can pass to heirs or buyers |
| 8 | Absence of term | No fixed end date |
| 9 | Prohibition of harmful use | Duty not to use it to harm others |
| 10 | Liability to execution | It can be taken to pay the owner's debts |
| 11 | Residuary character | Rights come back to the owner when lesser rights end |

Incidents 9 and 10 are **duties and liabilities**, not rights. Honoré's point is that ownership is a bundle, not a single absolute power.

### Subject matter of ownership

What can be owned?

- **Material things:** land, houses, vehicles, gold. This is corporeal ownership.
- **Rights that are not things:** a debt owed to you, shares in a company, a copyright, a patent, a trademark, a right of way. This is incorporeal ownership.
- **Not persons.** A free human being cannot be owned. Slavery is abolished. Article 29 of the Constitution of Nepal (right against exploitation) prohibits slavery, trafficking and forced labour.
- **Not things outside commerce**, such as the open sea or air, which no one can own privately.

Austin says ownership is over "things". Salmond says it is always over **rights**. Most textbooks follow Salmond: the true subject matter of ownership is a right, and in corporeal ownership that right relates to a physical thing.

### Nepal: meaning of ownership in the Civil Code

The Muluki Civil Code 2074 (in force from 1 Bhadra 2075) deals with property in Part 4.

- **Section 251:** cash, goods or work is property if it can be used, transacted, transferred, or yield a benefit.
- **Section 252:** property is movable or immovable, whether physical or non-physical, tangible or intangible. This accepts incorporeal property.
- **Section 266:** a person who acquires a right in property in accordance with law is deemed to be its owner.
- **Section 267:** lists the rights of an owner, "subject to laws":

| Section 267 clause | Right | Matching Honoré incident |
|---|---|---|
| (a) | Use the property | Use |
| (b) | Sell or transfer title | Capital, transmissibility |
| (c) | Mortgage or pledge it | Capital |
| (d) | Deal in it | Management |
| (e) | Take benefits in any manner | Income |
| (f) | Build structures, fence, mark boundaries, change its form or protect it | Management, security |
| (g) | Use the land below the surface and the sky above it | Use |
| (h) | Destroy or dispose of it | Capital |
| (i) | Bring legal action to acquire or secure it | Security |

The words "subject to laws" show that Nepal does not accept Austin's "unrestricted" ownership.

**Constitution of Nepal, Article 25 (right to property).** Every citizen has the right, subject to law, to acquire, own, enjoy, sell, gain from and otherwise deal with property. The Explanation says property includes movable and immovable property and intellectual property. The State may tax property and income. The State may acquire, requisition or encumber private property only in the public interest, and compensation is governed by law. These limits do not apply to property acquired illegally. Land reform, management of land use and similar State policies are allowed.

## 11.2 Classification of ownership

Salmond classifies ownership into pairs. Indian and Nepali texts add absolute and limited ownership.

### 11.2.1 Sole and co-ownership

**Sole ownership:** one person owns the thing alone. Ram alone owns his motorbike.

**Co-ownership:** two or more persons own the same thing at the same time. Each has a share in the whole thing, not a separate piece of it, until they divide it.

Co-ownership in English law has two forms:

| Point | Joint ownership (joint tenancy) | Ownership in common (tenancy in common) |
|---|---|---|
| On the death of one owner | His share passes to the **surviving co-owners** (right of survivorship) | His share passes to **his own heirs** |
| Shares | Treated as one undivided whole | Each has a definite share, equal or unequal |
| Example | Trustees holding property jointly | Two friends who buy land in shares 60:40 |

**Nepal.** Section 255 of the Civil Code classifies property by ownership into seven kinds: private, property in common, joint, community, public, government and trust property.

- **Section 256, private property:** sole ownership, such as property earned by one's own skill or labour, gifts, inheritance, salary, pension, royalties, and a woman's property from her parents' side. Section 256(2) lets the owner deal with it exclusively.
- **Section 257, property in common:** family property of coparceners, such as ancestral property and property earned by the joint family. It is divided by partition (Part 3, Chapter 10; Section 206 gives each coparcener an equal share).
- **Section 258, joint property:** property owned by two or more persons who are not coparceners in a joint family. Their shares are as stated in the deed; if the deed is silent, the shares are equal.
- **Section 263:** any joint owner may separate his share at any time. If division would cause a big loss, the property is sold and the price shared; or one owner may buy out the others, and the highest bidder among them gets it.
- **Section 416(3)-(4):** where two or more persons acquire the same property, each is entitled in proportion to his portion; if the deed does not say, they share equally.

!!! warning "Watch the words"
    Nepal's "property in common" (Section 257) means **family coparcenary property**. It is not the same as English "ownership in common". Nepal's "joint property" (Section 258) works more like English ownership in common: shares are fixed and pass to each owner's heirs.

### 11.2.2 Corporeal and incorporeal ownership

| Point | Corporeal ownership | Incorporeal ownership |
|---|---|---|
| Object | A material thing that can be touched | A right that cannot be touched |
| Examples | Land, house, car, book | Copyright, patent, trademark, debt, share, easement |
| Possession | Physical possession is possible | Only "possession" in the sense of enjoying the right |
| Salmond's view | Ownership of the right of ownership over a thing | Ownership of any other right |

Salmond explains that corporeal ownership is really ownership of a **right over a material thing**. Because we identify that right with the thing itself, we speak of "owning the land".

**Nepal.** Section 252 of the Civil Code says property may be "physical or non-physical, tangible or intangible". Section 256(1)(e) treats property from intellectual property or royalty as private property. The Explanation to Article 25 of the Constitution includes intellectual property. So Nepal recognises both kinds.

### 11.2.3 Trust and beneficial ownership

A **trust** exists when one person holds property for the benefit of another.

- The **trustee** is the trust owner. He holds the title and manages the property, but must not use it for himself.
- The **beneficiary** is the beneficial owner. He gets the benefit: the income, the use.

Example: a father leaves land to his brother to manage for his minor son until the son turns 18. The uncle is trustee; the son is beneficiary.

Salmond calls the trustee's ownership **nominal**: it is held for another. The beneficiary's ownership is the **real** one. The trustee's ownership is a burden; the beneficiary's is a benefit.

| Point | Trust ownership | Beneficial ownership |
|---|---|---|
| Who | Trustee | Beneficiary |
| Holds | Title and management | Benefit and enjoyment |
| For whom | For someone else | For himself |
| Duty | Must manage honestly and keep accounts | No duty to manage |

**Nepal.** Part 4, Chapter 6 of the Civil Code (Sections 314-351) deals with trusts.

- **Section 314:** a trust exists where a person arranges for another to operate and manage property in which he has right, ownership and possession, for the benefit of a beneficiary.
- **Section 315:** a trust may be public (for schools, hospitals, temples, welfare and similar purposes) or private (for a particular person or group).
- **Section 345:** trust property is held in the name of the person named in the memorandum of incorporation, or else in the beneficiary's name.
- **Section 344:** in a private trust, a competent beneficiary may give up his benefit by written notice. If all beneficiaries give it up, the trust is dissolved.

### 11.2.4 Absolute and limited ownership

**Absolute ownership:** the owner has all the incidents: use, disposal and unlimited duration. This is what Austin described.

**Limited ownership:** one or more incidents are cut down. The limit may be on:

- **use:** for example, a house that can be used only as a residence;
- **disposal:** for example, a person who may enjoy property for life but may not sell it;
- **duration:** for example, ownership for life only, which then passes to someone else.

| Point | Absolute ownership | Limited ownership |
|---|---|---|
| Use | Any lawful use | Restricted use |
| Disposal | Free | Restricted or barred |
| Duration | Unlimited, passes to heirs | May end at death or on a fixed date |
| Example | A person's private house bought with his own money | A life interest; a usufruct |

In practice no ownership is fully absolute today. The law always imposes some limits: tax, zoning, environmental rules, State acquisition.

**Nepal.** Section 267 grants the owner's rights "subject to laws". Section 432 bars transfer of immovable property to a foreigner without prior permission of the Government of Nepal (non-resident Nepali citizens excepted). Part 4, Chapter 7 deals with **usufruct**: under Section 352 an owner gives another person the right to enjoy property and its fruits, while the owner keeps title. The owner's ownership is then limited for that period.

### 11.2.5 Vested and contingent ownership

**Vested ownership:** the owner's title is already complete. Nothing more needs to happen.

**Contingent ownership:** the title depends on a condition. Salmond explains two forms:

- **Condition precedent:** ownership will begin only if a future event happens. Example: "my land to Sita if she becomes a doctor." Until then her ownership is contingent.
- **Condition subsequent:** ownership exists now but will end if an event happens. Example: "my land to Hari, but if he leaves Nepal it goes to Gita." Hari's ownership is vested but defeasible (can be ended), and Gita's is contingent.

| Point | Vested ownership | Contingent ownership |
|---|---|---|
| Title | Complete now | Depends on an uncertain event |
| Transfer | Can be transferred and inherited | Only a chance or expectation, though the law may protect it |
| Example | Land bought and registered | Land promised on passing an exam |

Contingent ownership is still a real present interest. It is more than a mere hope, because the law protects it.

**Nepal.** Section 406(3)-(4) of the Civil Code lets a donation or gift take effect immediately, after a period, or after the donor's death (a testamentary gift). Section 416(2) says that where a transfer is to take effect on death, the transferee becomes owner only after the transferor dies. Until then the transferee's interest is contingent. Section 408 makes a gift void if, for example, the gift is to an unborn child who is not born alive.

### Legal and equitable ownership (added by textbooks)

This pair comes from English history, where two sets of courts existed: common law courts and the Court of Chancery (equity).

| Point | Legal ownership | Equitable ownership |
|---|---|---|
| Recognised by | Common law courts | Courts of equity |
| Example | The trustee's title | The beneficiary's interest under a trust |
| Against a buyer | Good against everyone | Lost against a bona fide buyer for value without notice of it |

Nepal has a single civil code and never had separate equity courts. So this pair has little direct use in Nepal. Its idea survives in Nepal's trust provisions, where the trustee holds title for the beneficiary.

## 11.3 Acquisition, continuation and termination of ownership

### Acquisition: how ownership begins

Salmond divides modes of acquisition into **original** and **derivative**.

**Original acquisition:** a new title arises, not taken from any earlier owner.

| Kind | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Absolute | Taking a thing that belongs to no one (*res nullius*), or creating a new thing | Catching a wild fish in a river; writing a book; inventing a machine |
| Extinctive | Acquiring by ending another's ownership | Adverse possession or prescription: the old owner's title is extinguished |
| Accessory | Acquiring something that grows from or attaches to one's own property | Fruits of a tree; calf born to one's cow; land added by a river's deposit |

**Derivative acquisition:** title passes from an existing owner to a new one.

- **By act of the parties:** sale, gift, exchange, will, mortgage foreclosure.
- **By operation of law:** inheritance on death, partition, court order, auction in execution of a judgment.

Textbooks often sum up the modes as **possession, prescription, agreement and inheritance**.

### Continuation: how ownership is kept

Ownership is **indeterminate in duration**. Once acquired, it continues until something ends it.

- It does **not** end by mere non-use. An owner who leaves land empty for years still owns it (subject to adverse possession).
- It does **not** end when the owner loses possession. If a thing is stolen, the thief gets possession, not ownership.
- It continues past the owner's death, through heirs.
- It is protected by law against interference.

### Termination: how ownership ends

| Mode | Plain meaning |
|---|---|
| Transfer | Sale, gift or exchange; the new owner takes over |
| Death | Passes to heirs by succession (for the deceased, it ends) |
| Abandonment | The owner gives up the thing on purpose |
| Destruction | The thing ceases to exist |
| Prescription / adverse possession | Another person gains title by long possession |
| State acquisition | The State takes the property for a public purpose with compensation |
| Forfeiture / confiscation | The law takes the property as a penalty |
| Execution of a judgment | Property is sold to pay the owner's debts |

### Nepal's law on acquisition, continuation and termination

**Acquisition**

- **Section 266:** whoever acquires a right in property in accordance with law is deemed its owner. Ownership in Nepal must have a lawful source.
- **Section 256(1):** lists lawful ways to acquire private property: by one's own knowledge, skill or effort; donation, bequest or succession; lottery or gift; salary, gratuity, pension, provident fund, insurance; intellectual property or royalty; and a woman's property from before marriage or from her parents' side.
- **Sections 413-414:** property is transferred when an owner sells, donates, gifts or otherwise transfers it. Any person competent to contract may transfer property, with immediate or testamentary effect. A deed is required, except for a donation or gift of movable property or cash up to one hundred thousand rupees.
- **Section 416(1):** on transfer, the transferor's ownership ends on the date of transfer and the transferee becomes owner.
- **Sections 406-408:** donation and gift. A gift is void if not accepted, or if the gifted property is destroyed before the gift takes effect.
- **Sections 237-238 (Part 3, Chapter 11):** on a person's death, succession opens and the nearest heir takes the property and debts.
- **Partition (Part 3, Chapter 10):** each coparcener gets an equal share of the property in common (Section 206). After partition, the share becomes his private property.
- **Section 273, adverse possession:** a person who possesses another's movable property for more than three years, or land for more than thirty years, as if it were his own, is deemed to have an adverse possessory right. Under the proviso he may then obtain ownership in his own name. Limits: it does not apply to government, public, community or trust land; it does not apply if possession was secret, forcible or without the owner's knowledge; and the period counts only from the commencement of the Code.
- **Section 421(6):** a person who buys a lost or stolen thing in a public market, auction or open bidding need not return it to the real owner until paid its real value and his costs.

**Continuation**

- **Section 267:** the owner keeps the rights to use, transfer, mortgage, take benefits, build, use the ground below and sky above, destroy, and sue to protect the property.
- **Sections 276-277:** no one may use, tamper with or acquire another's property without consent, or encroach on it. Property is inviolable.
- **Section 296:** no one may register another's land in his own name.
- **Section 421(1)-(4):** no one may transfer another's property. Such a deed is void, and the real owner can recover the property. An owner of lost or stolen property may claim it within three years.
- **Section 297:** on the death of a registered landholder, the heir must apply to the Land Revenue Office for transfer of registration within thirty-five days. Late applicants still get it registered on a fee of one hundred rupees.
- **Constitution Article 25:** guarantees the right to own and deal with property, and limits State acquisition to the public interest.

**Termination**

- **By transfer:** Section 416(1). The transferor's ownership ceases from the date of transfer.
- **By death:** Section 237. Succession opens; ownership passes to heirs.
- **By having no heir:** Section 248. If there is no heir, or the heir declines, the property left after funeral costs and debts passes to the Local Level.
- **By adverse possession:** Section 273. The old owner loses title to the long-term possessor.
- **By State acquisition:** Article 25(2)-(3) of the Constitution and the proviso to Section 276(3): the Government may acquire property for public interest in accordance with law. The procedure is in the Land Acquisition Act 2034.
- **By illegal transfer to a foreigner:** Section 432(2). The deed is void and the property goes to the Government of Nepal.
- **By destruction or abandonment:** Section 272 ends a **possessory** right on abandonment, transfer or destruction of the property. Ownership itself ends when the thing is destroyed. The Code does not have one express section on abandonment of ownership.
- **Within a trust:** Section 344. If all beneficiaries of a private trust give up their benefit, the trust is dissolved.

## 11.4 Ownership and possession

### The difference in plain words

Ownership is having the **right**. Possession is having the **thing**. They usually go together, but not always.

- A tenant possesses a flat that the landlord owns.
- A thief possesses a phone he does not own.
- An owner whose land is let out owns it but does not possess it.

Salmond describes ownership as the **de jure** relation (by law) between a person and a right, and possession as the **de facto** relation (in fact) between a person and a thing. Ihering called possession the **outwork** (outer defence) of ownership: protecting possession is a quick way to protect the owner, because in most cases the possessor is the owner.

Possession has two elements:

- *corpus possessionis*: actual physical control;
- *animus possidendi*: the intention to possess.

### Comparison

| Point | Ownership | Possession |
|---|---|---|
| Nature | A legal right (de jure) | A fact of control (de facto) |
| Elements | Title recognised by law | *Corpus* and *animus* |
| Duration | Indefinite, passes to heirs | Lasts while control lasts |
| Proof | Title deed, registration | Physical control |
| Who can have it | Only the lawful title holder | Owner, tenant, borrower, even a thief |
| Loss | Not lost by losing control | Lost when control ends |
| Protection | Suit to recover title | Protected even against the owner who uses force |
| Relation | The "end" | The "evidence" and outer defence of it |

### How they connect

1. **Possession is evidence of ownership.** The person in possession is presumed to be the owner until shown otherwise. The saying "possession is nine points of the law" captures this.
2. **Possession can ripen into ownership.** Long, open, peaceful possession becomes ownership by prescription or adverse possession.
3. **Ownership includes the right to possess.** This is Honoré's first incident.
4. **They can be separated.** Leases, bailments, pledges and trusts split the two.

### Nepal

Part 4, Chapter 2 of the Civil Code is titled "Provisions Relating to Ownership and Possession". It treats them side by side.

| Topic | Ownership | Possession |
|---|---|---|
| Meaning | s. 266: right acquired in accordance with law | s. 268: holding property lawfully with intention to possess |
| Rights | s. 267: nine rights including sale, mortgage, destruction | s. 271: to possess uninterruptedly and take benefits |
| How acquired | Transfer, gift, succession (ss. 256, 413-416, 237) | s. 269: as owner, by lawful acquisition from another, or with consent; must be in good faith, peaceful or open |
| Who | Any person | s. 270: in person or by agent; through a guardian or curator for an incompetent person |
| How it ends | Transfer, death, State acquisition (ss. 416, 237; Art. 25) | s. 272: abandonment, transfer, destruction |
| Link | s. 273: long adverse possession lets the possessor obtain ownership | s. 274: a forcible, mala fide or secret possessor must return the property and benefits and pay compensation |

Section 268 contains both elements of possession: **holding** the property (*corpus*) **with the intention to possess** it (*animus*). Section 271(2)-(3) protects a bona fide possessor: he can claim from the owner the essential expenses of caring for the property and keep it until they are paid. Section 275 sets a limitation of three months from knowledge for suits under this Chapter.

??? question "Likely Q7: Co-ownership. (short note)"
    **Meaning.** Two or more persons own the same thing at the same time. Each has a share in the whole until division.

    **Two forms (English law).**

    - **Joint ownership:** on one owner's death, his share goes to the surviving co-owners (survivorship).
    - **Ownership in common:** each has a definite share, which goes to his own heirs.

    **Nepal.** Muluki Civil Code 2074:

    - Section 255 lists seven classes of property by ownership.
    - Section 257: property in common = family property of coparceners, divided by partition.
    - Section 258: joint property = property of two or more persons who are not coparceners; shares per deed, else equal.
    - Sections 260-262: management by consent of the owners (else by majority); no alteration without everyone's consent; if not all owners are available, any one owner may sue or defend for all.
    - Section 263: any joint owner may separate his share; if division causes heavy loss, sale or buy-out by the highest bidder among the owners.

    **Conclusion.** Co-ownership allows shared investment but needs rules for management and exit, which Sections 258-263 provide.

    **Meaning.** Holding another person's property openly, as if one owned it, for a period fixed by law, until the possessor can claim ownership. It is an extinctive mode of acquisition: it ends the old owner's title.

    **Nepal: Section 273, Muluki Civil Code 2074.**

    - Movable property: possession for more than three years. Land: more than thirty years.
    - The possessor is deemed to have an adverse possessory right and may obtain ownership in his own name.
    - Not available over government, public, community or trust land.
    - Not available if possession was secret, forcible or without the owner's knowledge.
    - A contract or other law may provide otherwise.
    - The period counts from the commencement of the Code (1 Bhadra 2075).

    **Comment.** Because the period runs only from 2075, no one can gain land this way until 2105 B.S. The rule rewards productive use but bars land grabbing of public land.

??? example "Q1. Explain classification of ownership. Discuss Nepalese legal provision on acquisition, continuation and termination of ownership. (2080 Q6, 20 marks)"
    **Introduction**

    Ownership is the most complete right a person can have over property. It includes the rights to possess, use, enjoy, transfer and destroy the property, for an unlimited time.

    Austin defined ownership as "a right — indefinite in point of user — unrestricted in point of disposition — and unlimited in point of duration — over a determinate thing". Salmond defined it as "the relation between a person and any right that is vested in him". Holland called it "plenary control over an object". Honoré described it as a bundle of eleven incidents, including the rights to possess, use, manage, take income, transfer, and the absence of any fixed term.

    In Nepal, Section 266 of the Muluki Civil Code 2074 says a person who acquires a right in property in accordance with law is deemed its owner. Section 267 lists the owner's rights.

    **Classification of ownership**

    Salmond classifies ownership into the following pairs. Indian and Nepali texts add absolute and limited ownership.

    *1. Sole and co-ownership.* Sole ownership is ownership by one person. Co-ownership is ownership of the same thing by two or more persons at once. In English law it takes two forms. In joint ownership, a deceased owner's share passes to the survivors. In ownership in common, it passes to his own heirs. In Nepal, Section 258 of the Civil Code makes property owned by two or more non-family persons "joint property", with shares as stated in the deed or else equal. Section 263 lets any joint owner separate his share. Section 257 deals with family "property in common" of coparceners.

    *2. Corporeal and incorporeal ownership.* Corporeal ownership is ownership of a material thing, such as land or a car. Incorporeal ownership is ownership of a right with no physical body, such as a copyright, patent, share or debt. Salmond explains that even corporeal ownership is really ownership of a right over a thing. Section 252 of the Civil Code accepts property that is "physical or non-physical, tangible or intangible".

    *3. Trust and beneficial ownership.* In a trust, the trustee holds title and manages the property for the benefit of another, the beneficiary. Salmond calls the trustee's ownership nominal and the beneficiary's real. Sections 314-351 of the Civil Code govern trusts. Section 315 divides them into public and private.

    *4. Legal and equitable ownership.* In English law, legal ownership was recognised by common law courts and equitable ownership by the courts of equity. A beneficiary's interest is equitable. Nepal has no separate equity courts, so the distinction appears only through the trust provisions.

    *5. Absolute and limited ownership.* Absolute ownership has all the incidents of ownership. Limited ownership is restricted as to use, disposal or duration, as with a life interest or usufruct. In Nepal, every owner's rights are "subject to laws" (Section 267). A usufruct (Section 352) limits the owner's enjoyment for a period.

    *6. Vested and contingent ownership.* Vested ownership is complete now. Contingent ownership depends on a condition precedent, which must happen before title begins, or a condition subsequent, which ends a present title. Section 416(2) of the Civil Code says that in a transfer effective on death, the transferee becomes owner only after the transferor dies.

    **Acquisition of ownership in Nepal**

    Salmond divides acquisition into original and derivative. Original acquisition creates a new title: by taking ownerless things, by creating something, by accession, or by prescription. Derivative acquisition passes an existing title, by act of the parties or by operation of law. Nepal's law covers both.

    - *Lawful source:* Section 266 recognises ownership only where a right is acquired in accordance with law.
    - *Own effort and other sources:* Section 256(1) treats as private property what a person earns by knowledge, skill or effort, and property from donation, bequest, succession, lottery, gift, salary, pension, insurance, and intellectual property or royalty.
    - *Transfer:* Sections 413-414 allow an owner competent to contract to sell, donate, gift or otherwise transfer property, by deed, with immediate or testamentary effect. Section 416(1) says the transferee becomes owner from the date of transfer.
    - *Gift and donation:* Sections 406-408.
    - *Succession:* under Sections 237-238, succession opens on death and the nearest heir takes the property.
    - *Partition:* coparceners each get an equal share of family property (Section 206).
    - *Adverse possession:* under Section 273, possession of another's movable property for over three years, or land for over thirty years, as one's own, gives an adverse possessory right, and the possessor may obtain ownership. It does not apply to government, public, community or trust land, or to secret or forcible possession, and the period runs from the Code's commencement.

    **Continuation of ownership in Nepal**

    Ownership has no fixed term. It is not lost by non-use or by loss of possession.

    - Section 267 keeps with the owner the rights to use, transfer, mortgage, take benefits, build, use the ground below and sky above, destroy, and sue to protect the property.
    - Sections 276-277 forbid anyone from using, tampering with, acquiring or encroaching on another's property without consent.
    - Section 296 forbids registering another's land in one's own name.
    - Section 421 makes a transfer of another's property void. The real owner can recover it, and an owner of lost or stolen property may claim it within three years.
    - Section 297 requires heirs to register transmission of land with the Land Revenue Office within thirty-five days, so that the record follows the owner.
    - Article 25 of the Constitution guarantees every citizen the right to acquire, own, enjoy and dispose of property, subject to law.

    **Termination of ownership in Nepal**

    - *Transfer:* under Section 416(1), the transferor's ownership ends on the date of transfer.
    - *Death:* succession opens under Section 237. If there is no heir, Section 248 sends the remaining property to the Local Level.
    - *Adverse possession:* Section 273 lets a long-term possessor obtain ownership.
    - *State acquisition:* Article 25(2)-(3) of the Constitution allows the State to acquire property only in the public interest, with compensation as provided by law. The proviso to Section 276(3) of the Civil Code says the same. The Land Acquisition Act 2034 sets the procedure.
    - *Illegal transfer to a foreigner:* Section 432(2) makes the deed void, and the property passes to the Government of Nepal.
    - *Destruction or abandonment:* ownership ends when the thing ceases to exist. Section 272 ends a possessory right on abandonment, transfer or destruction.

    **Critical view**

    Nepal's Code has clear rules on acquisition and transfer. It accepts Honoré's view of ownership as a bundle of rights subject to law, rather than Austin's unlimited right. Three weaknesses remain. First, the Code has no single section on abandonment of ownership. Second, because adverse possession counts only from 2075, it will have no practical effect on land for decades. Third, State acquisition under the public-interest test has often been disputed over the amount and timing of compensation.

    **Conclusion**

    Ownership is the fullest right over property, but never an unlimited one. Salmond's classification shows its many forms: sole and joint, corporeal and incorporeal, trust and beneficial, legal and equitable, absolute and limited, vested and contingent. Nepal's Muluki Civil Code 2074 lays down how ownership is acquired (Sections 256, 266, 413-416, 237, 273), kept (Sections 267, 276-277, 421) and ended (Sections 416, 237, 248, 273, 432), within the guarantee of Article 25 of the Constitution.

??? example "Q2. Define ownership. Distinguish between ownership and possession. (likely question, 20 marks)"
    **Introduction**

    Ownership and possession are two basic concepts of property law. In daily life they usually go together: a person owns and holds his own phone. But they can be separated. A tenant possesses a house the landlord owns; a thief possesses a watch he does not own. Jurisprudence explains how the two differ and how they are linked.

    **Meaning and definitions of ownership**

    Ownership is the fullest right a person can have over a thing. Austin defined it as "a right — indefinite in point of user — unrestricted in point of disposition — and unlimited in point of duration — over a determinate thing". Salmond defined it as "the relation between a person and any right that is vested in him", so that what one owns is always a right. Holland called it "plenary control over an object". Hibbert said it includes the rights to use, to exclude others, to dispose and to destroy.

    Honoré listed eleven incidents of ownership: the rights to possess, use, manage, take income and dispose of the capital; the right to security; transmissibility; absence of term; the prohibition of harmful use; liability to execution; and the residuary character.

    **Meaning of possession**

    Possession is the actual control of a thing with the intention to hold it. It has two elements: *corpus possessionis*, physical control, and *animus possidendi*, the intention to possess. Salmond describes ownership as the de jure relation between a person and a right, and possession as the de facto relation between a person and a thing. Savigny stressed the intention to hold as owner. Ihering called possession the outwork of ownership.

    **Distinction between ownership and possession**

    *1. Nature.* Ownership is a right recognised by law. Possession is a fact of control, which the law then protects.

    *2. Elements.* Ownership depends on lawful title. Possession depends on *corpus* and *animus*.

    *3. Holder.* Only the lawful title holder is owner. Possession may be held by an owner, a tenant, a borrower, a pledgee or even a thief.

    *4. Duration.* Ownership is indefinite and passes to heirs. Possession lasts only while control lasts.

    *5. Effect of losing control.* An owner who loses the thing remains owner. A possessor who loses control ceases to be possessor.

    *6. Proof.* Ownership is shown by a title deed or registration. Possession is shown by physical control.

    *7. Remedies.* The owner sues to recover title. A possessor is protected even against the true owner who uses force, so that people do not take the law into their own hands.

    *8. Content.* Ownership includes the right to possess. Possession does not include the right to own.

    | Point | Ownership | Possession |
    |---|---|---|
    | Nature | De jure right | De facto control |
    | Holder | Title holder only | Owner, tenant, borrower, thief |
    | Duration | Indefinite | While control lasts |
    | Proof | Deed, registration | Physical control |
    | Loss of thing | Ownership remains | Possession ends |

    **Possession without ownership and ownership without possession**

    The two can exist apart in four common situations:

    - *Lease:* the tenant possesses; the landlord owns.
    - *Bailment:* a person who leaves a watch for repair still owns it; the repairer possesses it.
    - *Pledge:* a borrower pledges gold to a lender; the lender possesses it as security, the borrower still owns it.
    - *Theft or loss:* the thief or finder possesses; the owner keeps ownership. In Nepal, Section 421(4) lets the owner of lost or stolen property claim it within three years.

    **Why the law protects possession**

    The law protects even a possessor who is not the owner. There are three reasons. First, it keeps the peace: no one, not even an owner, may seize a thing by force. Second, most possessors are owners, so protecting possession protects owners cheaply. Third, a possessor who has cared for property in good faith deserves some protection. Nepal reflects this: Section 271 lets a bona fide possessor recover essential expenses, and Section 274 punishes forcible or secret possession.

    **Relation between the two**

    The two are closely linked. Possession is evidence of ownership: the possessor is presumed to be owner until the contrary is shown. Long possession can become ownership through prescription or adverse possession. Ownership includes the right to possess. Ihering argued that protecting possession is the easiest way to protect owners, since most possessors are owners.

    **Nepal's position**

    The Muluki Civil Code 2074, Part 4, Chapter 2 is titled "Provisions Relating to Ownership and Possession".

    - Section 266 deems a person owner who acquires a right in property in accordance with law.
    - Section 267 lists nine rights of the owner, including use, sale, mortgage, taking benefits, building, destruction and legal action.
    - Section 268 deems a person to have a possessory right who holds property lawfully with the intention to possess. This contains both *corpus* and *animus*.
    - Section 269 lets possession be acquired by holding as owner, by acquiring from another lawfully, or by consent. Only possession acquired in good faith, peacefully or openly is lawful.
    - Section 270 allows possession through an agent, or through a guardian or curator for an incompetent person.
    - Section 271 gives the possessor the right to possess uninterruptedly and take benefits. A bona fide possessor can recover essential expenses from the owner and keep the property until paid.
    - Section 272 ends possession on abandonment, transfer or destruction.
    - Section 273 lets a person who possesses another's movable property for over three years, or land for over thirty years, as his own, obtain ownership. This is not allowed over government, public, community or trust land, or after secret or forcible possession.
    - Section 274 makes a forcible, mala fide or secret possessor return the property and its benefits and pay compensation.

    Article 25 of the Constitution protects the right to own property, subject to law.

    **Critical view**

    Austin's definition overstates ownership: modern owners face tax, planning and environmental limits. Nepal's Section 267 rightly makes the owner's rights "subject to laws". The line between ownership and possession can also blur, since possession is protected and can ripen into ownership. Nepal limits this by excluding public land and secret or forcible possession.

    **Conclusion**

    Ownership is the right; possession is the fact. Ownership is the end, and possession is often the means and the evidence. Nepal's Civil Code places them side by side in one chapter, defines each, gives each its own rights and remedies, and links them through adverse possession under Section 273.

??? example "Q3. What is ownership? Explain its characteristics (incidents) with reference to the law of Nepal. (likely question, 20 marks)"
    **Introduction**

    Ownership is the most complete legal right over property. It answers the question "whose is this?". Austin defined ownership as "a right — indefinite in point of user — unrestricted in point of disposition — and unlimited in point of duration — over a determinate thing". Salmond defined it as "the relation between a person and any right that is vested in him". Holland called it "plenary control over an object".

    In Nepal, Section 266 of the Muluki Civil Code 2074 deems a person owner who acquires a right in property in accordance with law, and Article 25 of the Constitution guarantees the right to property.

    **Subject matter of ownership**

    Ownership may be of material things, such as land and houses (corporeal), or of rights with no physical body, such as copyrights, shares and debts (incorporeal). Salmond holds that ownership is always of rights. Austin limited it to determinate things. Human beings cannot be owned. Section 252 of the Civil Code accepts tangible and intangible property, and the Explanation to Article 25 includes intellectual property.

    **Characteristics (incidents) of ownership**

    *Salmond's incidents.*

    1. *Right to possess.* The owner is entitled to possess the thing, even if someone else holds it for the time being.
    2. *Right to use and enjoy.* The owner may use the thing and take its fruits and income.
    3. *Right to consume, destroy or alienate.* The owner may sell, gift, mortgage, consume or destroy it.
    4. *Indeterminate duration.* Ownership has no fixed end and passes to heirs.
    5. *Residuary character.* When lesser rights such as a lease or mortgage end, the full right returns to the owner.

    *Honoré's incidents.* Honoré, in "Ownership" (1961), listed eleven standard incidents of full liberal ownership:

    1. right to possess;
    2. right to use;
    3. right to manage;
    4. right to the income;
    5. right to the capital (to consume, waste or transfer);
    6. right to security (against expropriation);
    7. transmissibility (to heirs and buyers);
    8. absence of term;
    9. prohibition of harmful use;
    10. liability to execution (for the owner's debts);
    11. residuary character.

    Honoré's point is that ownership is a bundle. A person may lack one or two incidents and still be owner. The last two incidents are duties and liabilities, which shows that ownership is never unlimited.

    *Hibbert's four rights.* The rights to use, to exclude others, to dispose and to destroy.

    **Limits on ownership**

    Every incident of ownership is subject to limits:

    - *Use* is limited by the duty not to harm neighbours, by building and planning rules, and by environmental law.
    - *Disposal* is limited where the law requires consent. Under the Civil Code, property in common of a family cannot be transferred without the consent of the coparceners (Section 419), while private property can be transferred without anyone's consent (Section 417).
    - *Security* gives way to the public interest: the State may acquire property with compensation.
    - *Duration* is unlimited, but ownership can be lost by adverse possession or by liability to execution.

    These limits show that modern ownership is a social as well as a private institution.

    **Kinds of ownership (brief)**

    Salmond's classification shows how the incidents can be split: sole and co-ownership; corporeal and incorporeal; trust and beneficial; legal and equitable; vested and contingent. Textbooks add absolute and limited ownership.

    **Nepal's position**

    Section 267 of the Civil Code gives the owner, "subject to laws", the rights:

    - (a) to use the property;
    - (b) to sell or otherwise transfer the title;
    - (c) to mortgage or pledge it;
    - (d) to deal in it;
    - (e) to take benefits from it in any manner;
    - (f) to build structures, walls or fences, mark boundaries, change its form or protect it;
    - (g) to use the land below the surface, goods in it, and the sky above;
    - (h) to destroy or dispose of it;
    - (i) to bring legal action to acquire or secure it.

    These match Honoré's incidents closely. Clauses (a) and (g) cover use; (e) income; (b), (c) and (h) capital and transmissibility; (d) and (f) management; (i) security.

    Other provisions supply the remaining incidents:

    - *Security:* Sections 276-277 forbid use, tampering, acquisition or encroachment without consent. Article 25(2)-(3) of the Constitution allows State acquisition only in the public interest, with compensation under law.
    - *Transmissibility:* Sections 413-416 (transfer) and Sections 237-238 (succession).
    - *Absence of term:* no section fixes an end to ownership; it passes by succession.
    - *Prohibition of harmful use:* Sections 278-284 require safety measures and restrict building on another's land, windows, roof drains, septic tanks, wells and trees that harm neighbours.
    - *Liability to execution:* property can be sold in execution of a court decree for the owner's debts; Section 247 lets creditors of a deceased recover debts from the heir, up to the value of the property inherited.
    - *Limits on disposal:* Section 432 bars transfer of immovable property to a foreigner without Government permission.

    **Critical view**

    Austin's definition describes an ideal that no legal system allows. Planning law, tax, land reform and environmental rules limit every owner. Honoré's bundle approach fits modern law better, and Nepal's Section 267 follows it by listing rights "subject to laws". Article 25 also allows land reform and State land management, which shows that in Nepal ownership serves social goals as well as private ones.

    **Conclusion**

    Ownership is the fullest but not an unlimited right. Its incidents are the rights to possess, use, manage, take income, transfer and destroy, with security and no fixed term, balanced by the duty not to harm others and liability for debts. The Muluki Civil Code 2074 sets these out in Section 267 and related sections, and Article 25 of the Constitution protects them as a fundamental right.
