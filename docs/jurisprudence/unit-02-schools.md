# Unit 2: Schools of Jurisprudence

!!! abstract "In one line"
    A "school" is a group of jurists who share one answer to the question "what is law?". This unit covers six of them: natural, historical, sociological, analytical (positivist), realist and socialist.

## Why it matters for the exam

- **Asked as a full 20-mark question in both papers we have:** 2080 Q1 "Elaborate different types of school of jurisprudence" and 2078 Q1 "Examine the basic features of Natural School of legal thought".
- This is the most important unit in the paper. Learn the comparison table and the three full answers below.
- Short notes can come on any one thinker or idea: Volksgeist, command theory, Grundnorm, social engineering, legal realism.

## Start here: why so many schools?

Imagine six people looking at the same mountain from six sides. Each describes a different mountain, and each is partly right.

Jurists look at law the same way. Ask "what is law?" and you get six answers:

| School | Its answer to "what is law?" | One-line image |
|---|---|---|
| Natural | Law is what is **right and just**, by reason or morality | Law as a moral compass |
| Historical | Law is what grows from a **people's customs and history** | Law as a tree with roots |
| Analytical (positivist) | Law is what the **state commands**, good or bad | Law as an order from above |
| Sociological | Law is a **tool to balance interests in society** | Law as an engineer's tool |
| Realist | Law is what **courts actually do** | Law as a prediction |
| Socialist (Marxist) | Law is a **tool of the economically powerful class** | Law as a weapon |

Keep this table in your head. Every answer in this unit builds on it.

When each school took shape (roughly):

| Period | School |
|---|---|
| Ancient Greece to today | Natural |
| Early 1800s | Historical; Analytical (Bentham, Austin) |
| Mid to late 1800s | Socialist (Marx, Engels) |
| Late 1800s to 1900s | Sociological (Ihering, Ehrlich, Pound) |
| 1900s | Realist (Holmes, Frank, Llewellyn) |

The historical and sociological schools grew partly as reactions against natural law and against Austin's narrow positivism.

## 2.1 Natural School of Law

### Core idea

There is a **higher law** above the laws made by kings and parliaments. It comes from nature, human reason, morality or God. Man-made law is valid only if it agrees with this higher law. An unjust law is not truly law: *lex iniusta non est lex*, a phrase associated with St Augustine and Thomas Aquinas.

### How it developed

| Period | Main thinkers | Key idea |
|---|---|---|
| Ancient Greece | Sophocles (*Antigone*), Aristotle, the Stoics | Some laws are "natural" and the same everywhere; others are only local convention |
| Rome | Cicero | "True law is right reason in agreement with nature." Roman law separated *jus civile* (law of the city), *jus gentium* (law common to peoples) and *jus naturale* (natural law) |
| Middle Ages | Thomas Aquinas | Four kinds of law: eternal (God's plan), divine (scripture), natural (God's law known by reason), human (man-made). Human law must follow natural law |
| 1600s-1700s: social contract | Grotius, Hobbes, Locke, Rousseau | Natural law based on reason, not religion. The state is created by a contract among people |
| 1800s | — | Decline. Positivism and the historical school attacked it as vague and unscientific |
| 1900s revival | Stammler, Fuller, Finnis; Nuremberg trials; Universal Declaration of Human Rights (1948) | After the crimes of Nazi Germany, people again asked whether an evil law is really law |

### The social contract thinkers

These three appear in almost every exam answer on the natural school.

| | Hobbes | Locke | Rousseau |
|---|---|---|---|
| Book | *Leviathan* (1651) | *Two Treatises of Government* (1689) | *The Social Contract* (1762) |
| Life before the state | "War of every man against every man"; life "solitary, poor, nasty, brutish and short" | Peaceful but insecure; people have natural rights to life, liberty and property | Free and equal, but corrupted by property and society |
| Why people made the state | For safety | To protect their natural rights | To live by the "general will" |
| Power of the ruler | Absolute; people cannot rebel | Limited; people may resist a ruler who breaks their trust | The people as a whole are sovereign |
| Legacy | Strong state, order | Limited government, rights, constitutionalism | Popular sovereignty, democracy |

**Grotius** (1583-1645) is called the father of international law. He argued that natural law would hold even if God did not exist, so it rests on reason alone.

### The modern revival

- **Rudolf Stammler**: "natural law with a variable content". The ideal of justice is fixed, but the rules that serve it change with time and place.
- **Lon Fuller**, *The Morality of Law* (1964): law has an "inner morality". A system fails to be law if its rules are, for example, secret, retroactive, contradictory or impossible to obey.
- **John Finnis**, *Natural Law and Natural Rights* (1980): law exists to help people achieve basic goods. His list of seven: life, knowledge, play, aesthetic experience, sociability (friendship), practical reasonableness and religion.
- **Human rights.** The Universal Declaration of Human Rights (1948) and modern bills of rights put natural-law ideas into written form.

### Basic features of the natural school

1. There is a law higher than man-made law.
2. It comes from reason, nature, morality or God.
3. It is universal and unchanging (classical view), or has a fixed aim with changing content (Stammler).
4. Man-made law must agree with it; an unjust law lacks true authority.
5. Law and morality are closely linked.
6. It gives a standard to judge and reform existing law.
7. It is the root of the ideas of natural rights, human rights and the rule of law.

### Criticism

- **Vague.** "Nature" and "reason" mean different things to different people.
- **Used for opposite ends.** Hobbes used it to support absolute rulers; Locke used it to support revolution.
- **Mixes "is" and "ought".** Positivists say this confuses what the law is with what it should be.
- **Not scientific.** Its claims cannot be tested.

### Natural law in Nepal

- **Dharma.** In the Hindu tradition, *dharma* was a higher order that even the king was expected to follow. This is close to the natural-law idea.
- **Fundamental rights.** Part 3 of the Constitution of Nepal (2015) guarantees rights such as the right to live with dignity and the right to equality. These grew from natural-rights thinking.
- **Natural justice.** Nepali courts apply the principles of natural justice: no one should be a judge in their own cause, and both sides must be heard. These are covered in the Procedural and Administrative Law papers.
- **Judicial review.** Under Article 133, the Supreme Court can declare a law void if it conflicts with the Constitution. This tests man-made law against a higher standard.

## 2.2 Historical School of Law

### Core idea

Law is not made by a lawgiver all at once. It **grows slowly** from the customs, beliefs and way of life of a people, like its language. The legislator should only record what already lives in the people.

### Main thinkers

- **Friedrich Carl von Savigny** (German, 1779-1861) is the founder. In 1814 a German jurist, Thibaut, proposed a single civil code for all of Germany. Savigny opposed it in his essay *On the Vocation of Our Age for Legislation and Jurisprudence* (1814). His main ideas:
    - Law comes from the **Volksgeist**, the spirit of the people.
    - Law grows with the people, becomes strong with them and dies when the people lose their identity.
    - Custom comes before legislation and is more important than it.
    - Law is not universal; each nation has its own.
- **Georg Puchta**, Savigny's student, developed these ideas further.
- **Sir Henry Maine** (English, 1822-1888), *Ancient Law* (1861), studied law across many societies. His famous conclusion: "the movement of the progressive societies has hitherto been a movement from Status to Contract". In early societies your rights depended on your status (family, caste, sex). In modern societies they depend more on agreements you make freely.

### Features

1. Law is found, not made.
2. Custom is the main source; legislation should follow it.
3. Law is national, not universal.
4. Law changes slowly with the life of the people.
5. History is the right method for studying law.

### Criticism

- **Custom can be unjust.** Some customs, such as sati or untouchability, must be abolished by legislation, not followed.
- **It underrates legislation.** Modern states change society deliberately through law.
- **"Volksgeist" is vague.** A nation has many groups with different customs; whose spirit counts?
- **Borrowed law works.** Many countries adopted foreign codes successfully, which the theory cannot explain.

### Historical school and Nepal

- The **Muluki Ain of 1910 B.S. (1854 A.D.)** largely codified existing Hindu law and social custom, which fits the historical school.
- But Nepal has also used legislation to **abolish harmful customs**: sati was abolished in 1920, and caste discrimination and untouchability are now crimes. This shows the limit of the historical school.

## 2.3 Sociological School of Law

### Core idea

Law should be studied **as it works in society**, not only as words in a statute. Law is a tool for meeting social needs and balancing the interests of different groups.

### Main thinkers

| Thinker | Key idea |
|---|---|
| **Auguste Comte** (French) | Coined the word "sociology"; society should be studied scientifically. Laid the ground for this school |
| **Rudolf von Ihering** (German, 1818-1892) | Law is a means to a social end. It protects interests, and it balances individual interests against social ones |
| **Eugen Ehrlich** (Austrian, 1862-1922) | The "living law": the rules people actually follow in society matter more than the statute book |
| **Léon Duguit** (French, 1859-1928) | Law rests on "social solidarity", people's need for each other. The state is a public service, not a sovereign above people |
| **Roscoe Pound** (American, 1870-1964) | Law is **social engineering**. It must recognise and balance three kinds of interests: individual, public and social |

### Pound's theory of interests

Pound is the most important name here.

- **Individual interests:** personal life, body, reputation, property, family.
- **Public interests:** interests of the state as a legal person, such as its security and dignity.
- **Social interests:** interests of society as a whole, such as public health, morals, peace and order, and the protection of the weak.

Law's task is to satisfy as many interests as possible with the least friction and waste, like an engineer.

### Features

1. Law is studied in its social setting.
2. Law is a means to social ends, not an end in itself.
3. Focus on the effect of law, not only its text.
4. Other social sciences are used to understand law.
5. Law must change as society changes.

### Criticism

- "Social interests" are hard to define and can be used to justify anything.
- It can weaken individual rights in the name of society.
- It explains how law works better than it explains what law is.

### Sociological school and Nepal

- **Public interest litigation.** Under Article 133, the Supreme Court hears writ petitions on matters of public interest. Through these, courts have used law to address social problems such as pollution and discrimination.
- **Directive principles.** Part 4 of the Constitution sets social goals for the state.
- **Reform laws** on caste discrimination, domestic violence and women's property rights use law to change society: social engineering in action.

## 2.4 Analytical (Positivist) School of Law

### Core idea

Law is the law that **is**, laid down by a human authority, whether it is good or bad. Law and morality must be kept separate. The job of jurisprudence is to analyse law as it is.

### Main thinkers

**Jeremy Bentham** (1748-1832) separated law as it is from law as it ought to be (expository and censorial jurisprudence, Unit 1).

**John Austin** (1790-1859), *The Province of Jurisprudence Determined* (1832). He is the father of the analytical school. His **command theory**:

- Law is a **command** of the **sovereign**, backed by a **sanction**.
- The sovereign is a person or body that is **habitually obeyed** by most of society and does **not habitually obey** anyone else.
- So every law has three elements: command, sovereign, sanction.
- Rules that do not fit this, such as international law and rules of etiquette, are "positive morality", not law properly so called.

**Criticism of Austin**

1. **Customary law** is not commanded by any sovereign, yet it is law.
2. **International law** has no sovereign above states, yet states treat it as law.
3. **Constitutional law** limits the sovereign itself, which Austin's theory cannot explain.
4. **Not all laws command.** Laws that give powers, such as the power to make a will or a contract, do not order anyone to do anything.
5. **Sanction is not the essence.** People obey many laws because they accept them, not out of fear.
6. **Hart's gunman.** H.L.A. Hart said Austin's model makes law look like a gunman's order: "hand over your money or I'll shoot". That is being *obliged*, not being under a legal *obligation*.

**Hans Kelsen** (Austrian, 1881-1973), *Pure Theory of Law*:

- Law must be studied "purely", free of morality, politics and sociology.
- Law is a **hierarchy of norms**. Each norm gets its validity from a higher norm.
- At the top is the **Grundnorm** (basic norm), which is presupposed, not made. It is usually the rule that the first constitution must be obeyed.

**H.L.A. Hart** (English, 1907-1992), *The Concept of Law* (1961):

- Law is a union of **primary rules** (rules that impose duties, such as "do not steal") and **secondary rules** (rules about rules).
- The secondary rules are: the **rule of recognition** (how to identify valid law), **rules of change** (how to make and repeal law) and **rules of adjudication** (who decides disputes).
- Law exists when officials accept these rules from the **internal point of view**, as standards they ought to follow.

### Features

1. Law is what is laid down by a human authority.
2. Law and morality are separate.
3. Law is analysed as it is, not as it ought to be.
4. Focus on legal concepts: rights, duties, sovereignty, sanction.
5. Courts apply the law as written.

### Criticism

- It ignores the moral and social purpose of law.
- An evil law, such as Nazi race laws, is still "law" in this view, which troubled many after the Second World War.
- It underrates custom and the role of judges.

### Analytical school and Nepal

- **Article 1** of the Constitution of Nepal declares the Constitution the fundamental law of Nepal. Any law inconsistent with it is void to the extent of the inconsistency. This is like Kelsen's hierarchy of norms, with the Constitution at the top.
- Nepal's **written codes** (Muluki Codes 2074) and the habit of interpreting statutes by their words reflect the analytical approach.

## 2.5 Realist School of Law

### Core idea

Law is not what is written in books; it is **what courts actually decide**. To know the law, predict what judges will do.

### American realism

- **Oliver Wendell Holmes** (US judge), "The Path of the Law" (1897): "The prophecies of what the courts will do in fact, and nothing more pretentious, are what I mean by the law." Also: "The life of the law has not been logic: it has been experience." (*The Common Law*, 1881)
- **John Chipman Gray**: all law is judge-made; statutes are only sources of law until courts apply them.
- **Jerome Frank**, *Law and the Modern Mind* (1930): legal certainty is a myth. A judge's personality and the uncertainty of facts shape decisions.
- **Karl Llewellyn**, *The Bramble Bush* (1930): law is what officials do about disputes.

### Scandinavian realism

Axel Hägerström, Karl Olivecrona and Alf Ross argued that legal ideas such as "rights" and "duties" are not real things but psychological facts: feelings of being bound.

### Features

1. Law is what courts do, not what books say.
2. Distrust of fixed rules and concepts.
3. Focus on judges, facts and outcomes.
4. Law is uncertain and keeps changing.
5. Uses psychology and social science.

### Criticism

- It overstates uncertainty. Most cases are decided predictably by the rules.
- It ignores statutes and the legislature.
- A judge cannot "predict what the judge will do"; the judge needs rules to decide.

### Realist school and Nepal

- Under **Article 128(4)** of the Constitution, Supreme Court interpretations and principles bind all courts and offices. In practice, to know Nepali law you must know what the Supreme Court has decided. Students reading NKP decisions are doing what realists recommend.

## 2.6 Socialist (Marxist) School of Law

### Core idea

Law is part of the **superstructure** built on society's **economic base**. The class that owns the means of production uses law and the state to protect its interests. When classes disappear, the state will "wither away" (Engels's phrase). Later Marxists, especially Pashukanis, said law would wither away with it.

### Main thinkers

- **Karl Marx** (1818-1883) and **Friedrich Engels** (1820-1895): law reflects economic relations; it is an instrument of the ruling class. "Wither away" is Engels's phrase, used of the state in *Anti-Dühring* (1878).
- **V.I. Lenin**: after a revolution, a "dictatorship of the proletariat" (the working class) would use law until classes disappeared.
- **Evgeny Pashukanis** (Soviet jurist): law arises from the exchange of commodities in a market economy, so it would disappear under communism.

### Features

1. Law depends on economics.
2. Law serves the ruling class.
3. Law changes when the economic system changes.
4. In a classless society, the state (and, for later Marxists, law) would wither away.

### Criticism

- **The state and law did not wither away** in the Soviet Union; the state became more powerful.
- It reduces everything to economics and ignores religion, culture and morality.
- Much law, such as traffic rules or family law, protects everyone, not one class.

### Socialist school and Nepal

- **Article 4** of the Constitution describes Nepal as a "socialism-oriented" federal democratic republic. The preamble commits Nepal to socialism based on democratic norms and values.
- Rights to **food, housing, employment and social security** in Part 3, and land reform laws, reflect socialist ideas of equality within a democratic system.

??? example "Q1. Elaborate different types of school of jurisprudence. (2080 Q1, 20 marks)"
    **Introduction**

    Jurisprudence is the study of law in general. Its central question is: *what is law?* Jurists have given different answers to this question. A group of jurists who share the same basic answer is called a **school of jurisprudence**. Each school looks at law from one angle, so each explains part of the truth.

    The main schools are the natural school, the historical school, the analytical (positivist) school, the sociological school, the realist school and the socialist (Marxist) school.

    **1. Natural school**

    The natural school holds that there is a higher law above man-made law. This higher law comes from nature, human reason, morality or God, and it is universal. Man-made law is valid only if it agrees with it; an unjust law is not true law.

    The idea began with the Greeks and the Stoics. Cicero wrote that "true law is right reason in agreement with nature". In the Middle Ages, Thomas Aquinas divided law into eternal, divine, natural and human law, and held that human law must follow natural law. In the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, Hobbes, Locke and Rousseau used natural law to explain the origin of the state through a social contract. Locke's idea of natural rights to life, liberty and property became the base of modern constitutionalism.

    The school declined in the nineteenth century but revived in the twentieth, after the Second World War. Stammler spoke of "natural law with a variable content", Lon Fuller of the "inner morality of law", and John Finnis of basic human goods. The Universal Declaration of Human Rights (1948) is its modern expression.

    *Criticism:* the idea of "nature" is vague, and the school has been used to support both absolute rule (Hobbes) and revolution (Locke).

    **2. Historical school**

    The historical school, founded by the German jurist Savigny, holds that law is not made but found. It grows from the **Volksgeist**, the spirit of the people, like a language. Custom is therefore more important than legislation, and each nation has its own law. Savigny opposed the codification of German law in 1814 on this ground. Sir Henry Maine, in *Ancient Law* (1861), showed that progressive societies move "from status to contract".

    *Criticism:* customs can be unjust and need to be changed by legislation. The school underrates the reforming role of the modern state.

    **3. Analytical (positivist) school**

    The analytical school studies law as it is, not as it ought to be, and keeps law separate from morality. Bentham laid its base; John Austin is its father. In *The Province of Jurisprudence Determined* (1832), Austin defined law as a **command of the sovereign backed by a sanction**. The sovereign is the person or body habitually obeyed by the bulk of society, who does not habitually obey anyone else.

    Austin's theory was criticised because it cannot explain customary law, international law, constitutional limits on the sovereign, or laws that give powers rather than impose duties. Later positivists refined it. Hans Kelsen, in his *Pure Theory of Law*, described law as a hierarchy of norms resting on a **Grundnorm**, or basic norm. H.L.A. Hart, in *The Concept of Law* (1961), described law as a union of primary rules, which impose duties, and secondary rules of recognition, change and adjudication.

    *Criticism:* by separating law from morality, positivism treats even evil laws as valid law.

    **4. Sociological school**

    The sociological school studies law as it works in society. Law is a means to social ends. Ihering said that law protects interests; Ehrlich spoke of the "living law" that people actually follow; Duguit based law on social solidarity. Roscoe Pound described law as **social engineering**: the task of balancing individual, public and social interests with the least friction and waste.

    *Criticism:* "social interest" is vague and can be used to override individual rights.

    **5. Realist school**

    The realist school says that law is what courts actually do. Justice Holmes wrote that "the prophecies of what the courts will do in fact, and nothing more pretentious, are what I mean by the law". American realists such as Gray, Jerome Frank and Karl Llewellyn focused on judges and facts. Scandinavian realists such as Olivecrona and Alf Ross treated legal ideas as psychological facts.

    *Criticism:* it exaggerates the uncertainty of law and ignores legislation.

    **6. Socialist (Marxist) school**

    Marx and Engels saw law as part of the superstructure built on the economic base. Law is an instrument of the ruling class, and in a classless society the state would "wither away", in Engels's phrase. Pashukanis linked law to the exchange of commodities and argued that law too would wither away.

    *Criticism:* the state and law did not wither away in socialist states. The school ignores the non-economic roots of law.

    **Comparison**

    | School | Source of law | Key thinkers |
    |---|---|---|
    | Natural | Reason, morality | Aquinas, Locke, Fuller |
    | Historical | Custom, Volksgeist | Savigny, Maine |
    | Analytical | Sovereign / state | Austin, Kelsen, Hart |
    | Sociological | Society's needs | Ihering, Ehrlich, Pound |
    | Realist | Court decisions | Holmes, Frank, Llewellyn |
    | Socialist | Economic base | Marx, Engels |

    **Schools in the Nepalese legal system**

    Nepal's legal system reflects all these schools:

    - **Natural:** fundamental rights in Part 3 of the Constitution and the principles of natural justice.
    - **Historical:** the Muluki Ain of 1910 B.S. codified custom.
    - **Analytical:** Article 1 makes the Constitution the fundamental law, and inconsistent laws are void.
    - **Sociological:** public interest litigation under Article 133 and social reform laws.
    - **Realist:** Supreme Court principles bind all courts under Article 128(4).
    - **Socialist:** Article 4 describes Nepal as "socialism-oriented".

    **Conclusion**

    Each school answers the question "what is law?" from one angle. The natural school stresses justice, the historical school custom, the analytical school the command of the state, the sociological school social needs, the realist school the courts, and the socialist school economic power. None is complete on its own. A modern legal system such as Nepal's draws on all of them. The best understanding of law combines their insights.

??? example "Q2. Examine the basic features of Natural School of legal thought. (2078 Q1, 20 marks)"
    **Introduction**

    The natural school is the oldest school of jurisprudence. It holds that above the laws made by human rulers there is a higher law, called natural law, which comes from nature, reason, morality or God. Man-made law is valid only so far as it agrees with natural law. The idea is summed up in the phrase *lex iniusta non est lex*, "an unjust law is not law", associated with St Augustine and Thomas Aquinas.

    Cicero gave a classic definition: "True law is right reason in agreement with nature; it is of universal application, unchanging and everlasting."

    **Historical development**

    *Greek period.* In Sophocles' play *Antigone*, Antigone defies the king's order in the name of the unwritten laws of the gods. Aristotle distinguished natural justice, the same everywhere, from conventional justice, which varies by place. The Stoics taught that the universe is governed by reason and that human beings share in it.

    *Roman period.* Roman jurists distinguished *jus civile* (the law of the city of Rome), *jus gentium* (the law common to all peoples) and *jus naturale* (natural law). Cicero spread Stoic ideas of natural law.

    *Medieval period.* Thomas Aquinas (1225-1274) divided law into four kinds: eternal law (God's plan for the universe), divine law (revealed in scripture), natural law (the part of eternal law known by human reason) and human law. Human law that conflicts with natural law is a perversion of law.

    *Renaissance and social contract.* Natural law was now based on reason rather than religion. Grotius argued that natural law would hold even if God did not exist. Hobbes, in *Leviathan* (1651), said that people gave up their rights to an absolute ruler to escape a state of nature in which life was "solitary, poor, nasty, brutish and short". Locke, in *Two Treatises of Government* (1689), said that people formed the state to protect their natural rights to life, liberty and property, and may resist a ruler who violates them. Rousseau, in *The Social Contract* (1762), placed sovereignty in the "general will" of the people.

    *Decline.* In the nineteenth century, positivists and the historical school attacked natural law as vague and unscientific, and it lost influence.

    *Revival.* In the twentieth century, the crimes of Nazi Germany and the Nuremberg trials revived the question of whether an evil law is truly law. Stammler proposed "natural law with a variable content". Lon Fuller, in *The Morality of Law* (1964), argued that law has an inner morality. John Finnis, in *Natural Law and Natural Rights* (1980), grounded law in basic human goods. The Universal Declaration of Human Rights (1948) gave natural-rights ideas written form.

    **Basic features of the natural school**

    1. **A higher law exists.** Above human law there is a natural law that binds all rulers and all people.
    2. **Its source is reason, nature, morality or God.** In ancient and medieval thought it was linked to God; from Grotius onwards, to human reason.
    3. **It is universal.** Natural law applies to all people at all times. Stammler modified this: the aim of justice is fixed, but its content varies.
    4. **Human law must conform to it.** A man-made law that conflicts with natural law lacks true authority.
    5. **Law and morality are closely linked.** Unlike positivism, the natural school does not separate what the law is from what it ought to be.
    6. **It sets a standard for judging law.** It gives a yardstick to criticise and reform existing law.
    7. **It is the source of natural and human rights.** Locke's natural rights led to the American and French declarations and to modern human rights law.
    8. **It limits the power of the state.** Since rulers are bound by a higher law, their power is not absolute. This idea underlies constitutionalism and the rule of law.

    **Criticism**

    1. **Vagueness.** Terms such as "nature" and "reason" have no fixed meaning.
    2. **Contradictory uses.** The same theory supported absolute monarchy (Hobbes) and revolution (Locke).
    3. **Confusion of "is" and "ought".** Positivists such as Austin argue that a law may be unjust and still be law.
    4. **Not scientific.** Its claims cannot be proved by observation.

    Despite these criticisms, the school has survived because it answers a need that positivism cannot: a standard for judging whether a law is just.

    **Natural law in Nepal**

    - **Dharma.** In the Hindu legal tradition, *dharma* was a higher moral order that even the king was expected to follow.
    - **Fundamental rights.** Part 3 of the Constitution of Nepal (2015) guarantees rights such as the right to live with dignity and the right to equality. These reflect natural-rights thinking.
    - **Judicial review.** Under Article 133, the Supreme Court can declare a law void if it conflicts with the Constitution. This tests man-made law against a higher standard.
    - **Natural justice.** Nepali courts require that no one be a judge in their own cause and that both sides be heard before a decision.

    **Conclusion**

    The natural school holds that law must be measured against a higher standard of justice. It has passed through ancient, medieval, social-contract and modern phases. Its main features are a higher universal law, a close link between law and morality, and limits on state power. It is criticised as vague and unscientific, but it remains the foundation of human rights, constitutionalism and the rule of law, including in Nepal's Constitution. As long as people ask whether a law is just, the natural school will remain relevant.

??? example "Q3. Critically examine Austin's command theory of law. How did Kelsen and Hart develop the analytical school? (likely question, 20 marks)"
    **Introduction**

    The analytical, or positivist, school studies law as it is, not as it ought to be. It keeps law and morality separate and analyses the basic concepts of law. Jeremy Bentham laid its foundation, but John Austin (1790-1859) is regarded as its father. His theory of law as a command was later criticised and developed by Hans Kelsen and H.L.A. Hart.

    **Austin's command theory**

    In *The Province of Jurisprudence Determined* (1832), Austin defined law as **a command given by a sovereign to subjects, backed by a sanction**. The theory has three elements.

    1. **Command.** A command is an expression of a wish that someone do or not do something, with a threat of harm if they do not. Law is a general command addressed to the people.
    2. **Sovereign.** The sovereign is a determinate person or body of persons whom the bulk of society habitually obeys, and who does not habitually obey anyone else. Every independent political society has such a sovereign, and the sovereign's power is legally unlimited.
    3. **Sanction.** A sanction is the harm or penalty that follows disobedience. Duty exists because of the threat of sanction.

    Austin called only such commands "laws properly so called". Rules without a sovereign behind them, such as international law, rules of honour and fashion, he called **positive morality**, not law.

    **Merits of the command theory**

    - It is clear and simple, and it separates law from morality, religion and custom.
    - It shows that law depends on the authority of the state.
    - It gave jurisprudence a precise subject and influenced legal education and codification.

    **Criticism of the command theory**

    1. **Customary law.** Customs become law without any command of a sovereign. Austin said they become law only when courts adopt them, but this does not fit how custom works.
    2. **International law.** There is no sovereign above states, yet states treat international law as binding. Austin's view that it is only "positive morality" is out of step with modern practice.
    3. **Constitutional law.** Modern constitutions limit the legislature itself, through federalism, fundamental rights and judicial review. Austin's legally unlimited sovereign cannot explain this.
    4. **Power-conferring laws.** Laws allowing people to make wills, contracts or marriages do not command anything; they give powers.
    5. **Sanction is not the essence of law.** Many people obey law because they accept it as right, not out of fear. Some laws, such as many constitutional provisions, carry no sanction.
    6. **Judge-made law.** Austin's theory does not adequately explain law made by courts through precedent.
    7. **Hart's "gunman" objection.** Hart said Austin's model makes law look like a gunman's order: "your money or your life". The victim is *obliged* to obey, but is not under an *obligation*. Law creates obligations because it is accepted as a standard, not merely because of threats.
    8. **Who is the sovereign?** In federal and democratic states, power is divided, and it is hard to find one determinate sovereign.

    **Kelsen's development: the Pure Theory of Law**

    Hans Kelsen (1881-1973) kept Austin's separation of law from morality but replaced commands with **norms**.

    - Law should be studied "purely", free from morality, politics, sociology and psychology.
    - A legal system is a **hierarchy of norms**. A judgment gets its validity from a statute, the statute from the constitution, and so on.
    - At the top stands the **Grundnorm**, or basic norm, usually the presupposition that the first constitution ought to be obeyed. It is not made but assumed.
    - Sanction remains important: a legal norm says that if certain conduct occurs, a sanction ought to follow.
    - After a successful revolution, a new Grundnorm takes the place of the old one.

    *Criticism:* the Grundnorm is a hypothesis, and the theory cannot say why people actually obey law.

    **Hart's development: The Concept of Law**

    H.L.A. Hart (1907-1992), in *The Concept of Law* (1961), rejected the command model and described law as a **union of primary and secondary rules**.

    - **Primary rules** impose duties, such as rules against violence or theft.
    - **Secondary rules** are rules about primary rules:
        - the **rule of recognition**, which identifies what counts as valid law;
        - **rules of change**, which allow law to be made and repealed;
        - **rules of adjudication**, which empower courts to decide disputes.
    - Law exists when officials accept these rules from the **internal point of view**, as standards they ought to follow, and the population generally obeys them.
    - Hart remained a positivist: there is no necessary connection between law and morality. But he accepted a "minimum content of natural law", basic rules any society needs to survive.

    *Criticism:* Ronald Dworkin argued that Hart's model of rules ignores the role of legal principles, which judges use in hard cases.

    **Analytical school in Nepal**

    - **Article 1** of the Constitution of Nepal (2015) declares the Constitution the fundamental law; laws inconsistent with it are void to the extent of the inconsistency. This matches Kelsen's hierarchy of norms, with the Constitution at the top.
    - The Constitution itself limits Parliament, through fundamental rights and judicial review under Article 133. This is something Austin's theory of an unlimited sovereign cannot explain, but Hart's rule of recognition can.
    - Nepal's codified laws, such as the Muluki Codes 2074, and the practice of interpreting statutes by their words reflect the analytical approach.

    **Conclusion**

    Austin's command theory gave jurisprudence a clear method by separating law from morality and linking it to the sovereign. But it fails to explain custom, international law, constitutional limits and power-conferring rules. Kelsen improved it by treating law as a hierarchy of norms resting on a Grundnorm. Hart improved it further by describing law as a union of primary and secondary rules accepted from the internal point of view. The analytical school, so refined, still explains the structure of modern legal systems such as Nepal's. It must, however, be read together with other schools that explain the purpose and justice of law.
