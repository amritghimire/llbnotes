# Unit 6: Rights and Duties

!!! abstract "In one line"
    A legal right is an interest the law protects; a legal duty is an act the law requires. This unit covers what a right is, its five elements, the theories behind it, its kinds, what a duty is, and how rights and duties fit together.

## Why it matters for the exam

- **Asked as a full 20-mark question in both papers we have:**
    - 2080 Q4: "Describe theories of rights. Analyse the relationship between rights and duties."
    - 2078 Q3: "Explain the basic concept of rights. Briefly state the various kinds of legal rights."
- **Asked twice as a short note in 2080:** Q7(c) "kinds of duties" and Q7(d) "rights in rem and rights in personam".
- That is four questions in two papers. This is one of the most-asked units. Learn Salmond's definition, his five elements, the kinds table and Hohfeld's scheme.
- Rights also come up in Constitutional Law (fundamental rights). The Nepal material here helps in both papers.

## Start here: what is a right?

Ram owns a bicycle. He can ride it, lend it or sell it. If Shyam takes it without asking, Ram can go to the police or the court and get it back.

Three things are happening here:

1. Ram has an **interest**: he wants to use his bicycle.
2. Everyone else has a **duty**: not to take or damage it.
3. The **law backs Ram up**: if someone breaks the duty, the state steps in.

That package is a **legal right**. Take away the state's backing and Ram has only a moral claim, not a legal right.

Now flip it. Ram borrows Rs 5,000 from Sita and promises to repay in a month. Sita has a right to be paid. Ram has a duty to pay. The right and the duty are two sides of one legal relation. That link between rights and duties runs through the whole unit.

## 6.1 Definition of rights

### Plain meaning

A right is a claim that the law recognises and enforces. It lets one person demand that another do something, or not do something. "Right" in jurisprudence means a **legal right**, unless we say "moral right" or "natural right".

### Moral rights and legal rights

| Point | Moral right | Legal right |
|---|---|---|
| Source | Morality, conscience, religion | Law of the state |
| Enforcement | Social pressure, conscience | Courts and state power |
| Example | A child's claim to be thanked for a favour | A creditor's claim to be repaid |

A right can be both. The right to life is moral and also legal, because Article 16 of the Constitution of Nepal protects the right to live with dignity.

### Definitions to learn

| Jurist | Definition | What it stresses |
|---|---|---|
| **Salmond** | "A right is an interest recognised and protected by a rule of right. It is any interest, respect for which is a duty, and the disregard of which is a wrong." | Interest + duty + wrong. The most quoted definition. |
| **Ihering** | Rights are "legally protected interests". | Interest. The base of the interest theory. |
| **Holland** | A right is "a capacity residing in one man of controlling, with the assent and assistance of the state, the actions of others". | Control over others, backed by the state. Close to the will theory. |
| **Austin** | A person has a right when another or others are bound by law to do or not do something towards him. (paraphrase) | Right defined through the duty of someone else. |
| **Gray** | A legal right is the power a person has to make another do or not do an act, so far as that power comes from society imposing a legal duty on the other. (paraphrase) | Power, but only because the law imposes a duty. |
| **Kelsen** | A legal right is only the reflex of a legal duty. (paraphrase) | Duty comes first; the right follows from it. |

**What the definitions share:** a right involves (1) an interest or a will, (2) a duty on someone else, and (3) protection by the state.

### Rights in the wider sense

Salmond and Hohfeld point out that lawyers use the word "right" loosely for four different things:

| Loose word "right" | Strict name | Example |
|---|---|---|
| A claim against someone | Right (claim) | Sita's right to be repaid |
| Freedom to act | Liberty (privilege) | Your freedom to walk on a public road |
| Ability to change legal relations | Power | Your power to make a will |
| Protection from others' power | Immunity | A judge's protection from being sued for decisions made in office |

These are explained under Hohfeld in 6.5.

## 6.2 Elements and theories of rights

### Salmond's five elements of a legal right

Every legal right has five parts.

| Element | Meaning | Example: A buys a house from B |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Person of inherence** (subject or owner of the right) | The person who holds the right | A, the buyer |
| **2. Person of incidence** (subject of the duty) | The person bound by the matching duty | Everyone else, who must not trespass |
| **3. Content** of the right | The act or forbearance the right demands | Not entering or using the house without A's permission |
| **4. Object** (subject-matter) of the right | The thing or interest over which the right exists | The house |
| **5. Title** | The facts by which the right came to its owner | The sale deed and registration |

Short form to remember: **owner, duty-bearer, content, object, title**.

Notes on each element:

- **Person of inherence.** Every right must have an owner. The owner may be uncertain for a time, for example the right of an unborn child or the finder of lost goods, but there must be one. The owner can be a natural person or a legal person such as a company.
- **Person of incidence.** A right binds someone. For a right in rem it is the world at large. For a right in personam it is one definite person.
- **Content.** The right is to an act (a positive right) or a forbearance (a negative right).
- **Object.** Salmond says every right has an object. Critics say some rights have none in the material sense, for example the right to reputation. Salmond's answer is that the object may be immaterial: reputation, liberty, a patent.
- **Title.** Rights come through facts: purchase, gift, inheritance, contract, birth. Salmond called a fact that creates a right an **investitive** fact and a fact that ends one a **divestitive** fact.

### Theories of rights

Jurists disagree about what is at the core of a right. Is it the holder's **will** (choice), or the holder's **interest** (benefit)? Older theories also ask where rights come from.

#### 1. Theory of natural rights

- Rights come from nature or reason. They belong to people simply because they are human.
- Locke: everyone has natural rights to life, liberty and property. The state exists to protect them.
- This thinking shaped the American Declaration of Independence (1776), the French Declaration of the Rights of Man (1789) and the Universal Declaration of Human Rights (1948).
- **Criticism:** "nature" is vague. Bentham called natural rights "nonsense upon stilts". Positivists say only the state can create enforceable rights.

#### 2. Legal (positivist) theory

- Rights exist only because the law of the state creates them. Without the state there are no legal rights.
- Austin, Bentham and Kelsen hold this view. For Kelsen, a right is only the reflex of a duty that the law imposes.
- **Criticism:** if rights come only from the state, the state can take them away. It cannot explain human rights that people claim against the state.

#### 3. Historical theory

- Rights grow out of custom and long practice. What people have long been allowed to do becomes a right.
- Linked to the historical school (Savigny, Maine).
- **Criticism:** some long customs, such as untouchability, gave "rights" that were unjust and had to be abolished.

#### 4. Social welfare theory

- Rights are the conditions society needs for the common good. Society grants rights because they serve social welfare.
- Linked to the sociological school, Bentham's utility and Pound's balancing of interests. Laski said rights are the conditions of social life without which no person can be his best self (paraphrase).
- **Criticism:** "social welfare" is vague and can be used to cut individual rights.

#### 5. Will theory

- **Core idea:** a right is an expression of the **will** of its holder. The law gives one person control over another's duty.
- **Supporters:** Kant, Hegel, Savigny, Windscheid, Puchta. Holland's definition ("capacity ... of controlling ... the actions of others") fits it. In the twentieth century H.L.A. Hart gave a modern version, often called the **choice theory**: the right-holder is the person who can choose to enforce or waive the duty.
- **Example:** a creditor can sue for the debt, or forgive it. The choice is his. That choice is the right.
- **Criticism:**
    - Infants, mentally ill persons and unborn children have rights but no will to exercise them.
    - Some rights cannot be waived, such as protections given to workers or the right not to be enslaved.
    - It ignores why the law gives the right at all, which is to protect a benefit.

#### 6. Interest theory

- **Core idea:** a right is a **legally protected interest**. The purpose of a right is to protect the holder's benefit, not his will.
- **Founder:** Rudolf von Ihering. Salmond follows it in his definition ("an interest recognised and protected by a rule of right"). Bentham's idea that a right is a benefit from another's duty is close to it. Modern supporters include Neil MacCormick and Joseph Raz.
- Ihering said a right has two parts: a **substantial** part (the interest or benefit) and a **formal** part (the legal protection, the action in court).
- **Strength:** it explains why infants and the insane have rights. They have interests even though they have no usable will.
- **Criticism:**
    - Not every interest is a right. Many interests are not protected by law.
    - A person may benefit from a duty without having a right. A third party who benefits from a contract may not be able to sue on it.
    - Salmond himself agrees that the law protects interests only through duties, so the right really depends on the duty.

#### Will theory vs interest theory

| Point | Will theory | Interest theory |
|---|---|---|
| Core of a right | Holder's will or choice | Holder's interest or benefit |
| Main jurists | Kant, Hegel, Savigny, Holland; Hart (choice theory) | Ihering, Salmond, Bentham; MacCormick, Raz |
| Infants and the insane | Hard to explain their rights | Easy: they have interests |
| Non-waivable rights | Hard to explain | Easy to explain |
| Strength | Shows the role of freedom and control | Shows the purpose of rights |

**Balanced view.** Most writers now say both are partly right. A right protects an interest (interest theory), and usually gives the holder some control over the duty (will theory). Dias and Paton describe the two as stressing different sides of the same thing.

## 6.3 Kinds of legal rights

These are Salmond's classes. Learn each as a pair: meaning, example, one point of difference.

### 6.3.1 Perfect and imperfect rights

- **Perfect right:** recognised and **enforceable** by the law. If it is broken, you can sue. Example: the right to recover a debt within the limitation period.
- **Imperfect right:** recognised by the law but **not enforceable** by court action. Examples:
    - A debt that has become **time-barred**: the time limit for suing has passed. The debt still exists, but the court will not order payment.
    - Some claims against a foreign state, which is immune from suit in local courts.
- An imperfect right is not useless. It may be used as a defence or for set-off, and if the debtor pays voluntarily he cannot later ask for the money back. If the debtor acknowledges the debt in writing, the right may become enforceable again.

**Nepal:** the National (Muluki) Civil Code 2074 sets limitation periods (हदम्याद) for many claims. After the period expires, the court cannot hear the claim, so the right becomes imperfect. The directive principles in Part 4 of the Constitution are a similar case: Article 55 says no question can be raised in any court about whether they are implemented. They are recognised but not enforceable.

### 6.3.2 Legal and equitable rights

This distinction comes from **English** legal history.

- **Legal right:** recognised by the common law courts.
- **Equitable right:** recognised only by the Court of Chancery, which applied **equity**, a body of rules based on fairness and conscience.
- **Example:** in a trust, the trustee holds the legal right to the property; the beneficiary holds the equitable right.
- The Judicature Acts 1873-75 merged the two court systems in England, but the two kinds of rights still exist there.

| Point | Legal right | Equitable right |
|---|---|---|
| Origin | Common law courts | Court of Chancery (equity) |
| Strength | Good against everyone | Lost against a bona fide purchaser of the legal estate for value without notice |
| Example | Trustee's legal title | Beneficiary's interest under a trust |
| Two equitable interests in conflict | — | The earlier in time usually wins |

**Nepal:** Nepal never had separate equity courts, so the distinction does not exist as such. Ideas of fairness enter through statute and through the courts' general power to do justice.

### 6.3.3 Positive and negative rights

- **Positive right:** the matching duty is a duty to **do** something. The holder gets something done for him. Example: a creditor's right to be paid.
- **Negative right:** the matching duty is a duty to **refrain** (not to do something). Example: the owner's right that others do not trespass on his land.

| Point | Positive right | Negative right |
|---|---|---|
| Matching duty | To act | Not to act |
| Usual target | A definite person (mostly in personam) | Everyone (mostly in rem) |
| What the holder gets | An improvement in his position | Keeps his present position |
| Example | Right to wages | Right to reputation |

**Nepal:** the Constitution has both. The right against torture (Article 22) is negative: the state must refrain. The rights to education (Article 31), health care (Article 35) and food (Article 36) are largely positive: the state must provide.

### 6.3.4 Rights in rem and rights in personam

- **Right in rem** (real right): available against the **whole world**. Everyone has the matching duty. Example: ownership of a house; the right to reputation.
- **Right in personam** (personal right): available against a **definite person** or persons only. Example: Sita's right to be repaid by Ram under their loan contract.
- The Latin names: *in rem* = "against a thing", *in personam* = "against a person". The name is a little misleading. A right in rem is not a right against a thing; it is a right against everyone, usually over a thing.

| Point | Right in rem | Right in personam |
|---|---|---|
| Duty-bearer | The world at large | A definite person |
| Kind of duty | Mostly negative (do not interfere) | Mostly positive (do something) |
| Usual source | Ownership, status, law | Contract, quasi-contract, a wrong done |
| Example | Ownership of land | Right to payment under a contract |
| Remedy when broken | Against whoever interferes | Only against the bound person |

**Note:** a right in personam can arise from a right in rem. If X damages my car (breach of my right in rem), I get a right in personam against X to compensation.

**Nepal:** ownership and possession rights under the property provisions of the Civil Code 2074 are rights in rem. Rights under a contract governed by the contract provisions of the same Code are rights in personam.

### 6.3.5 Proprietary and personal rights

- **Proprietary right:** part of a person's **estate** (his assets). It has money value and can usually be transferred. Examples: ownership of land, a debt owed to you, a patent, goodwill of a business.
- **Personal right:** relates to a person's **status or well-being**. It has no direct money value and cannot be transferred. Examples: rights to life, liberty, reputation, privacy; the rights of a husband or wife; the rights of a parent.

| Point | Proprietary right | Personal right |
|---|---|---|
| Relates to | Estate, wealth | Status, well-being |
| Money value | Yes | Not directly |
| Transferable / inheritable | Usually yes | Usually no; ends with the person |
| Example | Ownership of a shop | Right to reputation |

Salmond notes that proprietary rights are part of a person's estate, which passes to his heirs; personal rights generally die with him.

**Nepal:** Article 25 protects the right to property (proprietary). Article 16 (life with dignity) and Article 28 (privacy) protect personal rights.

### 6.3.6 Vested and contingent rights

- **Vested right:** complete. All the facts needed to create it (its title) have happened. The holder has it now, even if he will enjoy it later.
- **Contingent right:** incomplete. Some facts of the title have happened, but the right depends on a future **uncertain** event. If the event does not happen, the right never arises.
- **Example:** A transfers property "to B for life, and then to C". C's right is vested; only the enjoyment is postponed. If A says "to C if C passes the LL.B. exam", C's right is contingent.

| Point | Vested right | Contingent right |
|---|---|---|
| Title | Complete | Partly complete |
| Depends on | Nothing uncertain (at most, time) | An uncertain event |
| On the holder's death before enjoyment | Usually passes to his heirs | Usually fails if the condition is not met |

### 6.3.7 Principal and accessory rights

- **Principal right:** stands on its own.
- **Accessory right:** attached to a principal right and exists to support it or add to its value. It goes with the principal right and usually cannot be separated from it.
- **Examples:**
    - An easement (a right of way over a neighbour's land) is accessory to the ownership of my land. If I sell my land, the right of way goes with it.
    - A mortgage or a guarantee is accessory to a debt. The creditor's right to recover the debt is principal; his right over the mortgaged property is accessory. If the debt is paid, the security ends.

### 6.3.8 Primary and sanctioning rights

- **Primary right** (antecedent right): exists on its own, independently of any wrong. Example: the right not to be defamed; the right to be paid under a contract.
- **Sanctioning right** (remedial right): arises **only when a primary right is broken**. It is the right to a remedy. Examples: the right to damages, the right to an injunction, the right to have the wrongdoer punished.

| Point | Primary right | Sanctioning right |
|---|---|---|
| When it arises | Independently of any wrong | Only after a wrong |
| Purpose | Protects an interest | Gives a remedy for its breach |
| Example | Right to reputation | Right to damages for defamation |

The maxim *ubi jus ibi remedium* ("where there is a right, there is a remedy") links the two.

**Nepal:** fundamental rights in Part 3 are primary rights. Article 46, the right to constitutional remedy, is a sanctioning right: it lets a person go to the Supreme Court (Article 133) or the High Court (Article 144) when a fundamental right is broken.

### 6.3.9 Rights in re propria and rights in re aliena

- **Right in re propria:** a right in one's **own** property. The ordinary right of an owner. Example: my ownership of my house.
- **Right in re aliena** (also called an **encumbrance**): a right in property **owned by someone else**. It limits the owner's right. Example: a tenant's lease over my house.
- Salmond names four main kinds of encumbrance:
    1. **Lease:** a right to possess and use another's property, usually for rent.
    2. **Servitude:** a right to limited use of another's land without possessing it, for example a right of way (easement).
    3. **Security:** a right over another's property to secure a debt, for example a mortgage or a pledge.
    4. **Trust:** the beneficiary's interest in property whose legal owner is the trustee.

| Point | Right in re propria | Right in re aliena |
|---|---|---|
| Whose property | Holder's own | Someone else's |
| Nature | Full right of ownership | A limited right that burdens the owner's right |
| Example | Ownership of land | Lease, easement, mortgage over that land |

**Note:** the encumbrance and the owner's right exist at the same time over the same thing. The owner's right is reduced by the encumbrance; when the encumbrance ends (the lease expires, the mortgage is paid), full ownership returns.

### Other classes some textbooks add

Some books also list: public and private rights; municipal and international rights; corporeal and incorporeal rights; rights of ownership and rights of possession. These are covered in Units 8, 10 and 11.

## 6.4 Meaning and kinds of duty

### Meaning

A duty is an act that a person is **bound** to do or to avoid. If he fails, he commits a **wrong**.

- **Salmond:** "A duty is an obligatory act, that is to say, it is an act the opposite of which would be a wrong."
- Duties and wrongs go together. A wrong is the breach of a duty.
- **Legal duty vs moral duty:** a legal duty is enforced by the state through courts and sanctions; a moral duty is enforced only by conscience and social opinion. Helping a stranger in distress is a moral duty; paying tax is a legal duty.

### Kinds of duty

#### 1. Moral and legal duties

| Point | Moral duty | Legal duty |
|---|---|---|
| Source | Morality, religion | State law |
| Sanction | Conscience, social disapproval | Court order, penalty |
| Example | Keeping a social promise | Paying tax, obeying traffic rules |

#### 2. Positive and negative duties

- **Positive duty:** to **do** something. Example: to pay a debt; to pay tax.
- **Negative duty:** to **refrain** from doing something. Example: not to trespass; not to defame.
- Positive duties usually match rights in personam; negative duties usually match rights in rem.

#### 3. Primary and secondary (sanctioning) duties

- **Primary duty:** exists on its own. Example: the duty not to injure others.
- **Secondary (sanctioning) duty:** arises when a primary duty is broken. Example: the duty to pay damages after injuring someone.

#### 4. Absolute and relative duties (Austin)

- **Relative duty:** has a **matching right** in some definite person. Example: the duty to repay Sita matches Sita's right to be paid.
- **Absolute duty:** has **no matching right** in any definite person. Austin gave four kinds:
    1. **Self-regarding duties**, such as the old duties not to attempt suicide or not to be drunk in a public place.
    2. **Duties owed to the public at large** or to persons indefinitely, such as not to cause a public nuisance.
    3. **Duties owed to the sovereign** or the state, such as duties of allegiance.
    4. **Duties towards lower animals**, such as not to treat animals cruelly.
- Austin said that breach of an absolute duty is usually a **crime**, while breach of a relative duty is usually a **civil wrong**.
- **Salmond disagreed:** he said every duty has a right on the other side. Duties to the public correspond to rights held by the state or society; duties towards animals can be seen as owed to society.

#### 5. Universal, general and particular duties

Some textbooks add this class, by the number of persons bound:

- **Universal duty:** binds everyone. Example: not to commit murder.
- **General duty:** binds a large class. Example: duties of all public servants.
- **Particular duty:** binds a definite person. Example: a contract party's duty to perform.

#### 6. Duties in rem and duties in personam

The mirror of rights in rem and in personam. A duty in rem binds everyone (not to trespass on A's land). A duty in personam binds one person (B's duty to deliver goods he sold to A).

### Nepal: duties in the Constitution

Part 3 of the Constitution of Nepal (2015) is titled "Fundamental Rights and Duties". **Article 48 (Duties of citizens)** lists four duties of every citizen:

1. to protect the nationality, sovereignty and integrity of Nepal, being loyal to the nation;
2. to abide by the Constitution and law;
3. to render compulsory service when the state requires it;
4. to protect and conserve public property.

Points to note:

- These are mostly **absolute duties** in Austin's sense: they are owed to the state or the public, not to a definite person.
- The Constitution gives no separate court remedy for breach of Article 48 as such. But duty (2), obeying the law, is enforced through every other law.
- Part 4 also sets out **obligations of the state** (directive principles). These are duties of the state, but Article 55 bars courts from asking whether they are carried out.

## 6.5 Relationship between rights and duties

### The core idea: correlation

Rights and duties are **correlative**. This means they are two sides of one legal relation. If Sita has a right to be paid, Ram has a duty to pay. You cannot describe one without the other.

- **Salmond:** every right has a matching duty and every duty has a matching right. There can be no right without a duty and no duty without a right.
- The right belongs to the person of inherence; the duty falls on the person of incidence.

### Views on the relationship

| View | Jurist | What it says |
|---|---|---|
| **Full correlation** | Salmond | Every right has a matching duty; every duty has a matching right |
| **Duties without rights** | Austin | Every right has a duty, but not every duty has a right. **Absolute duties** have no matching right |
| **Duty first** | Kelsen | The duty is primary. A right is only the reflex of a duty, when the law lets someone enforce it |
| **Only duties** | Duguit | There are no individual rights in the old sense; there are only social duties, based on social solidarity |
| **Duties as the source of rights** | Mahatma Gandhi | Rights grow from duties well done. If everyone does his duty, rights will follow (paraphrase) |
| **Correlation needs refining** | Hohfeld | "Right" is used for four different ideas. Only a claim-right has a duty as its strict correlative (see below) |

### Hohfeld's analysis of legal rights

**Wesley Newcomb Hohfeld** (American jurist, 1879-1918) published *Some Fundamental Legal Conceptions as Applied in Judicial Reasoning* in the *Yale Law Journal* in 1913. He said judges and writers confuse different ideas by calling all of them "rights". He broke them into eight basic legal relations, set out as **correlatives** and **opposites**.

- **Correlatives:** if one person has the first, another person must have the second. They describe the **same relation from both ends**.
- **Opposites:** if a person has one, he cannot have the other **in the same matter**. One is the absence of the other.

**Table of jural correlatives**

| Holder (X has) | Other party (Y has) | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Right (claim) | Duty | X has a right that Y repay Rs 5,000; Y has a duty to repay |
| Privilege (liberty) | No-right | X may walk on his own land; Y has no right that X keep off |
| Power | Liability | X can make an offer that Y can accept; Y is liable to have his legal position changed |
| Immunity | Disability | X's property cannot be taken by Y without legal process; Y has a disability |

**Table of jural opposites**

| Position | Its opposite |
|---|---|
| Right | No-right |
| Privilege | Duty |
| Power | Disability |
| Immunity | Liability |

**How to read the four pairs**

1. **Right and duty.** A right in the strict sense is a **claim** on another person. It is the only kind of "right" whose correlative is a duty.
2. **Privilege (liberty) and no-right.** A privilege is freedom from a duty. If I have the privilege to eat my salad, no one has a right that I not eat it. Its opposite is a duty: if I had a duty not to eat it, I would have no privilege.
3. **Power and liability.** A power is the ability to change legal relations by one's own act: to make a will, transfer property, appoint an agent, accept an offer. The person whose position can be changed is under a liability (Hohfeld does not use "liability" only in the sense of something bad).
4. **Immunity and disability.** An immunity is freedom from someone else's power. A disability is the lack of power. A diplomat's immunity from local courts matches the court's disability.

**First four and second four.** Right, privilege, power and immunity are advantages. Duty, no-right, liability and disability are burdens. Rights and privileges concern **conduct** (what one may or must do). Powers and immunities concern **changing legal relations**.

**Value of Hohfeld's scheme**

- It removes confusion. A court asking "does X have a right?" must say which kind.
- It shows the rule "every right has a duty" is strictly true only for claim-rights.
- It has been applied in property, contract and constitutional law.

**Criticism**

- It is formal: it tells us the structure of legal relations, not which rights people should have.
- Some say "privilege" and "no-right" are awkward terms; later writers prefer "liberty".
- Some say it does not fit criminal law or duties owed to the public well, where it is hard to name a definite right-holder.
- Hohfeld deals only with relations between two persons. Rights in rem become a bundle of many separate two-person relations, which some find artificial.

### Rights and duties in Nepal

- **Part 3 puts both together.** Its title is "Fundamental Rights and Duties". Articles 16 to 46 give rights; Article 48 sets duties of citizens.
- **The state's duty matches the citizen's right.** Most fundamental rights are claims against the state. Article 47 required the state to make laws to implement them within three years of the Constitution's start.
- **Remedy completes the right.** Article 46 gives the right to constitutional remedy under Article 133 or 144. Article 133(3) lets the Supreme Court issue writs such as habeas corpus, mandamus, certiorari, prohibition and quo warranto. This is *ubi jus ibi remedium* in practice.
- **No remedy, imperfect right.** Under Article 55, the directive principles and state policies in Part 4 cannot be enforced in court. They are state duties with no enforceable right on the other side.
- **Private law.** In the Civil Code 2074, a buyer's right to goods matches the seller's duty to deliver; an owner's right matches everyone's duty not to interfere.

| 6.3.4 | In rem / in personam | Against the world | Against a definite person | Ownership / contract claim |
| 6.3.5 | Proprietary / personal | Part of one's estate | Part of one's status or well-being | Patent / reputation |
| 6.3.6 | Vested / contingent | Complete now | Depends on an uncertain event | Remainder after a life interest / gift "if C passes" |
| 6.3.7 | Principal / accessory | Stands alone | Attached to another right | Debt / mortgage securing it |
| 6.3.8 | Primary / sanctioning | Exists before any wrong | Arises from a wrong | Right to reputation / right to damages |
| 6.3.9 | In re propria / in re aliena | In one's own property | In another's property | Ownership / lease, easement |

**Will theory, interest theory and the rest**

| Theory | Core of a right | Main names | Main weakness |
|---|---|---|---|
| Natural rights | Human nature, reason | Locke | Vague; not enforceable without the state |
| Legal (positivist) | Created by state law | Austin, Bentham, Kelsen | State can take rights away |
| Historical | Custom and practice | Savigny, Maine | Custom can be unjust |
| Social welfare | Needs of society | Bentham, Pound, Laski | Vague; can override individuals |
| Will | Holder's choice | Kant, Hegel, Savigny, Holland, Hart | Infants and the insane |
| Interest | Holder's protected benefit | Ihering, Salmond; MacCormick, Raz | Not every interest is a right |

## Model answers

??? question "2080 Q7(c): Kinds of duties"
    - **Meaning:** a duty is an act a person is legally bound to do or avoid. Salmond: "A duty is an obligatory act, that is to say, it is an act the opposite of which would be a wrong."
    - **Moral and legal:** moral duties are enforced by conscience; legal duties by the state.
    - **Positive and negative:** to do (pay a debt) or to refrain (not trespass).
    - **Primary and secondary (sanctioning):** the duty not to injure; the duty to pay damages after injuring.
    - **Absolute and relative (Austin):** relative duties have a matching right in a definite person. Absolute duties have none. Austin's four kinds: self-regarding duties, duties to the public at large, duties to the sovereign, duties towards animals. Breach of an absolute duty is usually a crime.
    - **Universal, general and particular:** by how many persons are bound.
    - **Salmond's objection:** every duty has a matching right, if only in the state or society.
    - **Nepal:** Article 48 of the Constitution lists citizens' duties: loyalty to the nation and protecting its sovereignty and integrity; obeying the Constitution and law; compulsory service when the state requires; protecting public property. These are absolute duties in Austin's sense.

??? question "2080 Q7(d): Rights in rem and rights in personam"
    - **Right in rem:** a right against the whole world. Everyone has the matching duty, usually negative. Example: ownership of a house; right to reputation.
    - **Right in personam:** a right against a definite person. The duty is usually positive. Example: a lender's right to be repaid.
    - The Latin names mean "against a thing" and "against a person". A right in rem is really a right against everyone, usually concerning a thing.
    - **Differences:** duty-bearer (world / one person); kind of duty (mostly negative / mostly positive); source (ownership, status / contract, quasi-contract, wrong).
    - **Link:** breach of a right in rem creates a right in personam. If X damages my car, I get a right against X for compensation.
    - **Nepal:** ownership under the property provisions of the Civil Code 2074 is in rem; claims under the contract provisions are in personam. Fundamental rights are mostly claims against the state.

??? question "Likely short note: Salmond's elements of a legal right"
    - Salmond: a right is "an interest recognised and protected by a rule of right".
    - Five elements, with the example of A buying a house:
        1. Person of inherence (owner of the right): A.
        2. Person of incidence (bearer of the duty): everyone else.
        3. Content: the act or forbearance due (not to enter without permission).
        4. Object (subject-matter): the house.
        5. Title: the facts that gave A the right (the sale deed).
    - **Criticism:** some rights, such as reputation, have no material object. Salmond answers that the object may be immaterial.

??? question "Likely short note: Hohfeld's jural correlatives"
    - Hohfeld (1913) said "right" covers four different ideas and set out eight basic legal relations.
    - **Correlatives:** right-duty, privilege-no-right, power-liability, immunity-disability.
    - **Opposites:** right/no-right, privilege/duty, power/disability, immunity/liability.
    - Only a claim-right strictly matches a duty.
    - **Value:** clear thinking about legal relations. **Criticism:** formal; does not say which rights people should have.

??? question "Likely short note: Will theory and interest theory"
    - **Will theory:** a right expresses the holder's will or choice. Kant, Hegel, Savigny, Holland; Hart's choice theory. Weakness: infants and the insane have rights without a usable will.
    - **Interest theory:** a right is a legally protected interest. Ihering; followed by Salmond. Weakness: not every interest is a right.
    - Most writers now combine them: a right protects an interest and usually gives control over the duty.

## Full 20-mark answers

??? example "Q1. Describe theories of rights. Analyse the relationship between rights and duties. (2080 Q4, 20 marks)"
    **Introduction**

    In everyday speech a right is something a person is entitled to. In jurisprudence a right means a **legal right**: a claim that the law recognises and enforces. Salmond defines it as "an interest recognised and protected by a rule of right. It is any interest, respect for which is a duty, and the disregard of which is a wrong." Ihering called rights "legally protected interests". Holland defined a right as "a capacity residing in one man of controlling, with the assent and assistance of the state, the actions of others."

    A **duty** is the other side of a right. Salmond defines it as "an obligatory act, that is to say, it is an act the opposite of which would be a wrong." Where one person has a right, another usually has a duty.

    Jurists disagree on two questions: what is the essence of a right, and where do rights come from? Their answers are the theories of rights.

    **Theories of rights**

    **1. Theory of natural rights.** Rights belong to people because they are human. They come from nature or reason, not from the state. John Locke said every person has natural rights to life, liberty and property, and that the state exists to protect them. This idea shaped the French Declaration of the Rights of Man (1789) and the Universal Declaration of Human Rights (1948). Its weakness is vagueness: "nature" means different things to different people. Bentham dismissed natural rights as "nonsense upon stilts".

    **2. Legal (positivist) theory.** Rights exist only because the state's law creates and protects them. Austin, Bentham and Kelsen hold this view. For Kelsen, a right is only the reflex of a legal duty. The theory is accurate about enforceable rights, but it implies that the state can take away any right, which conflicts with modern human rights.

    **3. Historical theory.** Rights grow from custom and long practice. What people have long been allowed to do comes to be recognised as a right. This follows Savigny's historical school. Its weakness is that some customs gave unjust "rights", such as caste privileges, which law had to abolish.

    **4. Social welfare theory.** Rights are conditions that society needs for the common good, and society grants them for that reason. It draws on Bentham's utility and Pound's idea of balancing interests. Laski described rights as the conditions of social life without which a person cannot be his best self. The weakness is that "social welfare" is vague and can be used to cut individual rights.

    **5. Will theory.** The essence of a right is the **will** of its holder. The law gives the holder control over another person's duty: he may enforce it or waive it. Kant, Hegel, Savigny and Windscheid supported this view, and Holland's definition reflects it. In the twentieth century H.L.A. Hart restated it as the choice theory. For example, a creditor may sue for the debt or forgive it; that choice is his right.

    *Criticism:* infants, the insane and unborn children have rights but no usable will. Some rights, such as the right not to be enslaved, cannot be waived. The theory also ignores the purpose for which the law gives the right.

    **6. Interest theory.** Rudolf von Ihering said rights are legally protected interests. The essence of a right is the **benefit** it protects, not the holder's will. A right has a substantial part (the interest) and a formal part (legal protection through an action in court). Salmond's definition follows this theory. Modern writers such as MacCormick and Raz have developed it.

    *Criticism:* not every interest is a right; many interests are left unprotected. And a person may benefit from a duty without holding the right, such as a third party who benefits from a contract.

    **Assessment of the theories.** No single theory explains everything. The natural, legal, historical and social welfare theories explain where rights come from. The will and interest theories explain what a right is. Most modern writers combine the last two: a right protects an interest, and usually gives its holder some control over the matching duty.

    **Relationship between rights and duties**

    **1. Rights and duties are correlative.** A right and a duty are two sides of one legal relation. If Sita lends Ram Rs 5,000, Sita's right to be repaid and Ram's duty to repay are the same relation seen from two ends. Salmond said every right has a corresponding duty and every duty has a corresponding right. There is no right without a duty and no duty without a right.

    **2. Austin: absolute duties.** Austin agreed that every right has a duty, but denied that every duty has a right. He called duties with a matching right **relative duties**, and duties without one **absolute duties**. His examples were self-regarding duties, duties to the public at large, duties to the sovereign, and duties towards animals. Breach of an absolute duty is usually a crime.

    Salmond answered that even these duties have a right on the other side, held by the state or society. A duty not to cause a public nuisance, for example, is owed to the public, and the state enforces the public's right.

    **3. Duty as primary.** Kelsen treated duty as the basic idea. A legal norm attaches a sanction to conduct, and that creates a duty. A right exists only when the law lets a person set the sanction in motion. So the right is a reflex of the duty.

    **4. Duguit: only duties.** Duguit denied individual rights in the traditional sense. He said every person has social duties based on social solidarity, and what we call rights are only the power to carry out those duties.

    **5. Gandhi: duties as the source of rights.** Mahatma Gandhi taught that rights flow from duties well performed. If everyone does his duty, everyone's rights will be secure.

    **6. Hohfeld's refinement.** Hohfeld (1913) showed that the word "right" covers four ideas: right (claim), privilege, power and immunity. Their correlatives are duty, no-right, liability and disability. So the rule "every right has a duty" is strictly true only for a claim-right. A privilege, such as my liberty to walk on my land, matches no duty in others; it matches their "no-right".

    **7. Mutual dependence.** Rights and duties support each other in practice. My right to free speech is safe only if others accept the duty not to stop me. Likewise, I must respect their rights. A society that stresses rights alone and forgets duties becomes selfish; one that stresses duties alone becomes authoritarian.

    **Nepal's position**

    - The Constitution of Nepal (2015) places both in one part: Part 3 is titled "Fundamental Rights and Duties". Articles 16 to 46 grant rights, such as the right to live with dignity (Article 16), freedom (Article 17), equality (Article 18) and property (Article 25).
    - **Article 48** sets four duties of citizens: to protect the nationality, sovereignty and integrity of Nepal while being loyal to the nation; to abide by the Constitution and law; to render compulsory service when the state requires; and to protect and conserve public property. These are absolute duties in Austin's sense.
    - Fundamental rights create matching duties in the state. **Article 47** required the state to make laws to implement them within three years.
    - **Article 46** gives the right to constitutional remedy under Article 133 or Article 144. Under Article 133(3), the Supreme Court can issue writs such as habeas corpus, mandamus, certiorari, prohibition and quo warranto. A right backed by a remedy is a perfect right.
    - **Article 55** says no question may be raised in any court about whether the directive principles and policies in Part 4 are implemented. These are state obligations without an enforceable right on the other side, close to Austin's absolute duties.

    **Critical view**

    The will theory suits a liberal view that stresses individual freedom. The interest theory suits a welfare state, which protects people who cannot act for themselves. Nepal's Constitution, with its rights to education, health and food, fits the interest theory better.

    The strict rule of correlation is useful but too simple. Austin's absolute duties, Hohfeld's privileges and Nepal's directive principles all show cases where a right and a duty do not match neatly. The better view is that rights and duties are closely linked and usually correlative, but the link takes several forms.

    **Conclusion**

    No single theory explains rights fully. The will theory explains the holder's control, and the interest theory explains the purpose of rights. Rights and duties are two sides of the same legal relation. Salmond's strict correlation is the general rule. Austin's absolute duties and Hohfeld's analysis show its limits. The Constitution of Nepal reflects this balance by placing fundamental rights and the duties of citizens in the same part, and by backing rights with the remedy in Article 46.

??? example "Q2. Explain the basic concept of rights. Briefly state the various kinds of legal rights. (2078 Q3, 20 marks)"
    **Introduction**

    A right, in plain terms, is a claim that the law allows a person to make against others. Ram owns a bicycle; everyone else must not take it; if someone does, the state will help Ram. That package of interest, duty and state protection is a legal right.

    Jurists have defined it in several ways:

    - **Salmond:** "A right is an interest recognised and protected by a rule of right. It is any interest, respect for which is a duty, and the disregard of which is a wrong."
    - **Ihering:** rights are "legally protected interests".
    - **Holland:** a right is "a capacity residing in one man of controlling, with the assent and assistance of the state, the actions of others."
    - **Austin:** a person has a right when another is bound by law to do or not do something towards him.

    **Basic concept of rights**

    **1. Interest, duty and protection.** All these definitions share three ideas. A right protects an interest. It places a duty on someone else. And the state enforces that duty.

    **2. Moral and legal rights.** A moral right rests on conscience and social opinion. A legal right is enforced by the state through courts. Jurisprudence studies legal rights.

    **3. Rights and duties are correlative.** Salmond says every right has a matching duty and every duty a matching right. Austin disagreed in part: he said some duties, called absolute duties, have no matching right.

    **4. Elements of a legal right.** Salmond identifies five elements. Take A buying a house from B:

    1. *Person of inherence*: the owner of the right, A.
    2. *Person of incidence*: the person bound by the duty, here everyone else.
    3. *Content*: the act or forbearance due; others must not enter or use the house.
    4. *Object* (subject-matter): the house.
    5. *Title*: the facts that gave A the right, here the sale deed.

    **5. Theories.** Under the **will theory** (Kant, Hegel, Savigny, Holland; Hart), the core of a right is the holder's choice to enforce or waive a duty. Under the **interest theory** (Ihering, Salmond), the core is a legally protected benefit. The interest theory explains better why infants and the insane have rights.

    **6. Rights in the wider sense.** Hohfeld showed that "right" is used for four ideas: a claim (matched by a duty), a privilege or liberty (matched by a no-right), a power (matched by a liability) and an immunity (matched by a disability).

    **Kinds of legal rights**

    **1. Perfect and imperfect rights.** A perfect right is recognised and enforceable by the court. An imperfect right is recognised but not enforceable. A time-barred debt is the standard example: the debt exists, but the court will not order payment. It can still serve as a defence, and money paid voluntarily on it cannot be recovered.

    **2. Legal and equitable rights.** This is an English distinction. Legal rights were recognised by the common law courts. Equitable rights were recognised only by the Court of Chancery. A trustee holds the legal right; the beneficiary holds the equitable right. An equitable right is weaker: it is lost against a bona fide purchaser of the legal estate for value without notice.

    **3. Positive and negative rights.** A positive right matches a duty to do something, such as a creditor's right to be paid. A negative right matches a duty not to do something, such as an owner's right that others do not trespass.

    **4. Rights in rem and rights in personam.** A right in rem is good against the whole world; ownership of land is an example. A right in personam is good only against a definite person; a contract claim is an example. Rights in rem usually match negative duties; rights in personam usually match positive ones.

    **5. Proprietary and personal rights.** Proprietary rights form part of a person's estate. They have money value and can be transferred: land, debts, patents. Personal rights concern status and well-being: life, liberty, reputation. They have no direct money value and usually die with the person.

    **6. Vested and contingent rights.** A vested right is complete; all facts of its title have happened. A contingent right depends on an uncertain future event. A gift "to C if C passes the LL.B. exam" gives C only a contingent right until he passes.

    **7. Principal and accessory rights.** A principal right stands alone. An accessory right is attached to it and follows it. A creditor's right to recover the debt is principal; his right under a mortgage securing it is accessory. A right of way attached to land is accessory to its ownership.

    **8. Primary and sanctioning rights.** A primary (antecedent) right exists independently of any wrong, such as the right to reputation. A sanctioning (remedial) right arises only when a primary right is broken, such as the right to damages for defamation. This reflects the maxim *ubi jus ibi remedium*: where there is a right, there is a remedy.

    **9. Rights in re propria and rights in re aliena.** A right in re propria is a right in one's own property, such as ownership. A right in re aliena, or encumbrance, is a right in property owned by another. It limits the owner's right. Salmond lists four kinds: leases, servitudes (such as easements), securities (such as mortgages) and trusts.

    Some writers add public and private rights, municipal and international rights, and corporeal and incorporeal rights.

    **Nepal's position**

    - Part 3 of the Constitution of Nepal (2015), "Fundamental Rights and Duties", lists rights from Article 16 to Article 46. They illustrate several kinds:
        - **Negative rights:** the right against torture (Article 22) requires the state to refrain.
        - **Positive rights:** the rights to education (Article 31), health care (Article 35) and food (Article 36) require the state to provide.
        - **Proprietary right:** the right to property (Article 25).
        - **Personal rights:** the right to live with dignity (Article 16) and the right to privacy (Article 28).
    - **Article 46** gives the right to constitutional remedy under Article 133 or 144. This is a sanctioning right, and it makes the fundamental rights perfect rights.
    - The directive principles and policies in Part 4 cannot be enforced; Article 55 bars any court question on their implementation. They are close to imperfect rights.
    - In private law, the Muluki Civil Code 2074 sets limitation periods. A claim brought after its period cannot be heard, so the right becomes imperfect. Ownership under the Code is a right in rem, and contract claims are rights in personam. Lease and mortgage arrangements under the Code are rights in re aliena.

    **Critical view**

    Salmond's classes are useful but overlap. A right of ownership is at once in rem, negative, proprietary, principal and in re propria. The classes are ways of looking at a right, not separate boxes. Some, like legal and equitable rights, belong to English history and have little meaning in Nepal. Hohfeld's analysis adds precision that Salmond's scheme lacks.

    **Conclusion**

    A legal right is an interest protected by law through a duty placed on others. It has five elements: owner, duty-bearer, content, object and title. Rights can be classified in at least nine ways, each showing a different feature. The Constitution of Nepal and the Muluki Civil Code 2074 contain examples of every major kind, and Article 46 ensures that fundamental rights are backed by a remedy.

??? example "Q3. Explain Hohfeld's analysis of legal rights. How far does it improve on the traditional view that rights and duties are correlative? (likely question, 20 marks)"
    **Introduction**

    In ordinary legal language, "right" is used for many different things. We speak of a right to be paid, a right to walk on a public road, a right to make a will and a right not to be arrested without cause. These are not the same kind of thing. The traditional view, stated by Salmond, is that every right has a corresponding duty and every duty a corresponding right. Salmond defines a right as "an interest recognised and protected by a rule of right."

    Wesley Newcomb Hohfeld (1879-1918), an American jurist at Yale, argued that this loose use of "right" causes confusion in judicial reasoning. In his article *Some Fundamental Legal Conceptions as Applied in Judicial Reasoning* (Yale Law Journal, 1913), he broke "rights" into four distinct ideas, each with its own correlative and opposite.

    **The traditional view**

    Salmond held that rights and duties are correlative: a right in one person always implies a duty in another. Austin accepted that every right has a duty but said some duties, called absolute duties, have no matching right. Both used "right" in a broad sense.

    **Hohfeld's eight fundamental conceptions**

    Hohfeld identified eight basic legal relations. He arranged them in two tables.

    *Jural correlatives* (if X has the first, Y has the second):

    | X has | Y has |
    |---|---|
    | Right (claim) | Duty |
    | Privilege (liberty) | No-right |
    | Power | Liability |
    | Immunity | Disability |

    *Jural opposites* (if X has one, X lacks the other):

    | Position | Opposite |
    |---|---|
    | Right | No-right |
    | Privilege | Duty |
    | Power | Disability |
    | Immunity | Liability |

    **Explanation of each pair**

    **1. Right and duty.** A right in the strict sense is a **claim** that another person act or not act in a certain way. Its correlative is a duty in that other person. If A lends B Rs 5,000, A has a claim to repayment and B has a duty to repay. Hohfeld said this is the only kind of "right" that strictly matches a duty.

    **2. Privilege and no-right.** A privilege (now often called a liberty) is freedom from a duty. If I have the privilege of walking on my own land, I have no duty to stay off it. Others have a **no-right**: they cannot demand that I keep off. The opposite of a privilege is a duty. This shows that a person can be free to do something without anyone having a duty to help him do it.

    **3. Power and liability.** A power is the legal ability to change legal relations by one's own act. Making a will, selling property, appointing an agent and accepting an offer are exercises of power. The person whose legal position may be changed is under a **liability**. When A makes an offer to B, B has the power to create a contract by accepting, and A is under a liability to have that contract created. The opposite of a power is a disability.

    **4. Immunity and disability.** An immunity is freedom from another person's power. Its correlative is a **disability**, the lack of power. If a law says a person's property cannot be taken except under law, the person has an immunity and officials have a disability to take it arbitrarily. Diplomatic immunity from local courts is another example.

    **Structure of the scheme**

    - The first four (right, privilege, power, immunity) are advantages; the second four (duty, no-right, liability, disability) are burdens.
    - Rights and privileges concern conduct: what one must or may do.
    - Powers and immunities concern the ability to change legal relations, or protection against such change.
    - Every relation is between two persons. A right in rem, such as ownership, is a bundle of many separate relations between the owner and each other person.

    **How far it improves on the traditional view**

    **1. Precision.** Hohfeld shows that the rule "every right has a correlative duty" is true only for claim-rights. A privilege, a power and an immunity each have a different correlative. The traditional view treated all four as "rights" with a matching duty, which was inaccurate.

    **2. Clearer judicial reasoning.** Courts often slip from one meaning to another. For example, from the fact that a person is free to do something (privilege), a court might wrongly conclude that others have a duty not to interfere (claim). Hohfeld's scheme exposes such errors.

    **3. Explains powers and immunities.** The traditional theory had no clear place for the power to make a will or for constitutional limits on government. Hohfeld's power and immunity explain them. Fundamental rights against the legislature, for example, are largely immunities: the legislature has a disability to take them away except as the constitution allows.

    **4. Explains Austin's problem.** Austin's absolute duties look less strange under Hohfeld. Some apparent rights without duties are really privileges; some duties lack a clear claim-holder because they are owed to the public at large.

    **Limitations of Hohfeld's analysis**

    - **Formal only.** It describes the structure of legal relations but says nothing about which rights people should have or why.
    - **Terminology.** "Privilege" and "no-right" are awkward. Later writers such as Glanville Williams preferred "liberty".
    - **Two-person model.** Breaking ownership into many two-person relations is artificial. Rights in rem are better understood as a single relation to the world.
    - **Public law and criminal law.** It is hard to fit duties owed to the public, or duties enforced by the state in criminal law, into a strict two-person scheme.

    **Nepal's position**

    Hohfeld's terms help explain Nepal's constitutional rights:

    - **Claims and duties:** fundamental rights such as the right to education (Article 31) and food (Article 36) are claims against the state, with the state under a duty. Article 47 required the state to make implementing laws within three years.
    - **Liberties:** the freedoms in Article 17, such as freedom of opinion and expression and freedom to move, are mainly liberties. Citizens may act, and the state has no right to stop them except under reasonable restrictions the Constitution allows.
    - **Immunities and disabilities:** Article 133 lets the Supreme Court declare a law void if it conflicts with the Constitution. So citizens have an immunity against laws that infringe fundamental rights, and Parliament has a disability to make them.
    - **Powers and remedies:** Article 46 gives every person the power to start constitutional remedy proceedings under Article 133 or 144. Under Article 133(3), the Supreme Court can issue writs including habeas corpus, mandamus, certiorari, prohibition and quo warranto. The respondent authority is under a liability to have its decision quashed.
    - **Duties without clear claim-holders:** the citizens' duties in Article 48, such as protecting public property, are owed to the state and society. They fit Austin's absolute duties more than Hohfeld's two-person claim-duty pair.

    **Critical view**

    Hohfeld did not reject the correlation of rights and duties; he refined it. Salmond's correlation is correct for claim-rights. For the other three kinds of advantage, the correlatives are no-right, liability and disability. The scheme is a tool for careful thinking, not a full theory of rights. It must be combined with the will or interest theory to explain why rights exist.

    **Conclusion**

    Hohfeld's analysis is one of the most important contributions to analytical jurisprudence. By separating rights, privileges, powers and immunities and setting out their correlatives and opposites, he showed that the traditional rule of correlation is true but incomplete. His scheme brings precision to legal reasoning and helps explain constitutional rights in Nepal, where fundamental rights work as claims, liberties and immunities backed by the power to seek a constitutional remedy.

- Nomita Agrawal, *Jurisprudence (Legal Theory)*, Central Law Publications, Allahabad. Syllabus reading.
- R.W.M. Dias, *Jurisprudence*, 5th edn, Butterworths, 1985 (Hohfeld's scheme).
- G.W. Paton, *A Textbook of Jurisprudence*, 4th edn, Oxford, 1972 (legal rights).
- V.D. Mahajan, *Jurisprudence and Legal Theory*, 5th edn, Eastern Book Company.
- T.E. Holland, *The Elements of Jurisprudence*, 13th edn, Oxford, 1924.
- John Austin, *Lectures on Jurisprudence* (relative and absolute duties).
- R. von Ihering, *Geist des römischen Rechts* (interest theory).
- W.N. Hohfeld, "Some Fundamental Legal Conceptions as Applied in Judicial Reasoning", 23 *Yale Law Journal* 16 (1913).
- H.L.A. Hart, "Legal Rights", in *Essays on Bentham*, Oxford, 1982 (choice theory).
- Constitution of Nepal (2015): Part 3 (Articles 16-48, including Articles 16, 17, 22, 25, 28, 31, 35, 36, 46, 47, 48), Article 55, Article 133(2)-(3), Article 144. English text checked at https://www.constituteproject.org/constitution/Nepal_2016 (November 2026).
- Muluki (National) Civil Code 2074: property, contract and limitation provisions (section numbers not cited).
