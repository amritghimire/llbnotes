## Facts

Translated and summarised by us from the Nepali judgment.

**The parties.** Samir Khan, Abdul Khan and Jan Tulla are from Bangladesh. When they came to the Supreme Court, they were held in the prison at Jagannathdewal, Tripureshwor, Kathmandu (कारागार कार्यालय, जगन्नाथदेवल). The first respondent (विपक्षी) named was the Kathmandu District Court, Babarmahal.

**Their story.** This is the petitioners' own account; the court made no finding on it. They said they came from poor families in a remote part of Bangladesh. They were not highly educated and had no work, so they wanted to go abroad for jobs. They also said that, under their country's rules, Bangladeshis could not go to Saudi Arabia for work. A Bangladeshi person took 15,000 to 20,000 Bangladeshi taka from them. He promised to make their citizenship papers and passports himself and send them to Saudi Arabia from Delhi. He took them to Delhi. On 27 June 2013 (an A.D. date in the petition), they went in to board the plane. He handed them the papers at the gate. Nobody met them in Saudi Arabia. After five days stranded there, they were sent back to Delhi. Because they carried Nepali citizenship certificates and passports, they were sent on from Delhi to Nepal. They said they had never been to Nepal before, and had made no fake documents in Nepal.

**The criminal case.** The Immigration Office (अध्यागमन कार्यालय) made a jaheri against them by letter (पत्र जाहेरी). The Government of Nepal, as plaintiff, prosecuted them for forging citizenship and passports (नागरिकता र राहदानी किर्ते). From 2070/3/18 they were held in jail while the case was tried (thuna). The prison said they had been in that prison since 2070/4/16, under a District Court order of 2070/4/15 to hold them in thuna. The judgment does not explain the gap between the two dates.

**The District Court's judgment.** On 2071/3/17 the Kathmandu District Court convicted all three. There was one incident, one charge sheet (अभियोगपत्र) and one judgment. A charge sheet is the document by which the government prosecutor brings a criminal case to court. Each petitioner got prison on three counts. A count (कलम) is a separate item of punishment in a judgment:

- 13 months under section 21(1) of the Nepal Citizenship Act 2063;
- 6 months under section 5 of the Passport Act 2024;
- 1 year and a fine of Rs 50 under the Forgery chapter of the Muluki Ain (किर्ते कागजको महल).

They paid the fines, Rs 150 in all. Nobody disputed this.

**How the term was fixed.** After a conviction, the court fixes the prison term (कैद ठेक्नु). It works out the dates from which and until which the prisoner is held. Here the District Court added the three terms together, making 2 years 7 months each. On 2071/4/18 it sent the prison one letter for each petitioner. The letters said: hold them from 2070/3/18 to 2072/10/17, and release them on 2072/10/18. A prison slip (कैदीपुर्जी) was issued. The judgment does not say whether the letters and the prison slip were separate documents. This is the court's order telling the prison to hold a prisoner, and until when.

**The petition.** The petitioners relied on Adalati Bandobasta No. 120 to deduct the time they had already spent in jail (कैद कट्टी मिनाहा). Counted from 2070/3/18, the longest term, 13 months, ended on 2071/4/17. So they should have been freed on 2071/4/18, but they were still held. They filed a writ petition (रिट निवेदन) in the Supreme Court, asking for habeas corpus. A writ is an order the Supreme Court can issue to protect a right. They filed under Articles 32 and 107(2) of the Interim Constitution of Nepal 2063, the constitution then in force. The judgment does not give the filing date.

**Show-cause order and replies.** On 2071/4/22 the Supreme Court made what we call a show-cause order. The judgment describes it as an order to send notice to the respondents. It asked the respondents to explain in writing what had happened, and why the writ should not be issued. They had seven days from receiving the order, not counting travel time. Two written replies (rejoinder, लिखित जवाफ) came in. One was from the prison. The other was from the Kathmandu District Court and its tahasil section (तहसिल शाखा). This is the section that keeps the record of sentences and carries them out.

## Issues

The judgment does not list the issues. From the arguments and paragraphs 3 to 5, they are (our framing):

1. In one case brought by one charge sheet, the petitioners got prison terms under three different laws. When the term was fixed, could the three be added up?
2. If not, were the petitioners held unlawfully after 2071/4/17, so that habeas corpus should issue?
3. The government argued that the petition should fail because another remedy was available. The judgment does not deal with this point.

## Arguments

**For the petitioners.** In the written petition:

- No. 10 of the Punishment chapter of the Muluki Ain (दण्ड सजायको १० नं.) covers a case on one document (एकै कागजका मुद्दा). If several Acts apply, prison is imposed only under the Act with the greatest punishment. The other Acts' prison terms are not added on.
- No. 41 of the same chapter says the term on several counts must not go above the greatest count.
- Their greatest term was 13 months. The prison slip that held them longer than that was wrong.
- The District Court's judgment did not say that the sentence was to be carried out under No. 10. They called this a "serious error".
- They named one exception: where cheating by making forged documents is proved, extra punishment for the forgery is added after the punishment for cheating. Apart from that, they said, no law or precedent in Nepal allows adding up terms just because one charge sheet claims punishment under different Acts. The judgment does not name the rule behind the exception.
- Neither the finding (ठहर खण्ड) nor the closing schedule (तपसिल खण्ड) of the District Court's judgment ordered extra punishment. And the Tahasildar (तहसिलदार), the officer in charge of the tahasil section, has no power to add punishment.
- They also relied on Articles 12(1)(2), 13(1) and 24(4)(6) of the Interim Constitution, as the judgment writes them. It gives only the numbers.

At the hearing, Advocate Mohan Bahadur Banjara Kshetri argued for the petitioners:

- Their greatest term, 13 months, was served on 2071/4/17.
- The District Court's prison slip that held them until 2072/10/17 was against the law.
- Since the greatest term was over, habeas corpus should issue and they should be freed.

**For the respondents** (Joint Attorney (सहन्यायाधिवक्ता) Sanjiv Raj Regmi, a senior government lawyer from the Office of the Attorney General):

- The prison's written reply: it held the petitioners under the District Court's order to hold them in thuna, and its letters fixing their terms. They were held by an authorised body and officer, so the petition should be dismissed.
- The District Court's written reply: the term was fixed according to the judgment and the record of sentence (लगत) made from it. So the petition should be dismissed.
- At the hearing: the petitioners are held under the lawful judgment of a competent court. They had another route to a remedy and did not use it, so the habeas corpus petition should be dismissed. The judgment does not say what that other remedy was.

## Decision

The Joint Bench issued the writ. Justice Joshi agreed with Justice Upadhyay's judgment.

1. **The two old rules** (paragraph 4). The Court quoted Nos. 10 and 41 of the Punishment chapter. Our summary:
    - **No. 10:** a case on one document may bring punishment under two or more Acts. Then prison is imposed only under the Act with the greatest punishment, and the other Acts' prison terms are not added on. Fines under all the Acts are added up. This applies unless an Act says the punishments are to be added (पटक वा खत खाप्नु). No. 10 also has rules for cases with both fine and prison, and for confiscation of all property (सर्वस्व).
    - **No. 41:** where one person must be imprisoned on several counts, in one case or several, the term must not go above the greatest count. If several counts carry equal limits, the term is fixed on one of them. No. 41 also covers a second count fixed before the first term is served. If the second count's limit is higher, the term is fixed up to that limit, counted from the day the person was first put in jail. If it is lower, no extra term is fixed. It is only noted in the judgment, the prison slip and the register of terms (कैद ठेक्ने किताब). But if the earlier term fell short of the greatest count's limit, the shortfall is served on the later count. A new offence committed after the judgment gets its own extra term.
2. **No adding up in a one-charge-sheet case** (paragraph 5). In a case brought by one charge sheet, prison terms under two or more Acts cannot be added up unless an Act itself says so. Only the term under the Act with the greatest term applies, and the person does not serve the others. Under No. 41, the term must not go above the greatest count.
3. **Applied to the petitioners** (paragraphs 3 and 5). There was one incident, one charge sheet and one judgment, with three counts. The greatest term was 13 months under the Citizenship Act. Adding 13 months, 6 months and 1 year to fix 2 years 7 months "does not appear lawful".
4. **The term was already served** (paragraph 6). The petitioners had been held in jail since 2070/3/18. So their 13 months were served on 2071/4/17.
5. **Order** (paragraph 6). The Court issued an order of habeas corpus in the name of the respondents. It told them to release Samir Khan, Abdul Khan and Jan Tulla from imprisonment at once. The petition was struck off the register and its file handed over under the rules.

The Court did not set aside the convictions or the three sentences. It held only that they could not be added up when the term was fixed. The judgment does not deal with the argument that another remedy was available. It does not say what happened to the petitioners after the order.

## Ratio decidendi

Our translation of the official headnote, point by point:

1. In a case brought by one charge sheet, prison sentences may have been given under two or more Acts. Then they cannot be added up unless an Act itself provides for adding them up. (प्रकरण नं. ५)
2. Where punishment is given under two or more Acts, only the prison sentence under the Act with the greatest prison sentence applies. The person punished does not have to serve the prison sentences under the other Acts, and they cannot be added on. Where prison terms must be fixed on several counts, the term must not go above the limit of the count with the greatest term. (प्रकरण नं. ५)
3. The petitioners were sentenced to prison on three counts from one charge sheet. The 13 months each under section 21(1) of the Nepal Citizenship Act 2063 was the greatest prison sentence of the three counts. So only the sentence under that Act should apply, and the other Acts' sentences could not be added on. The term should have been fixed so as not to go above 13 months. Instead, three terms were added together. These were 13 months under section 21(1) of the Citizenship Act 2063 and 6 months under section 5 of the Passport Act 2024. The third was 1 year under the Forgery chapter of the Muluki Ain. Fixing a term of 2 years 7 months this way does not appear lawful. (प्रकरण नं. ५)

**In short (our summary):** where one charge sheet leads to prison terms under several Acts, only the longest is served. The others cannot be added on unless an Act says so.

## Why it matters

- **One charge sheet, one prison term.** Where one charge sheet leads to prison terms under several Acts, only the longest is served. The exception is where an Act says the terms are to be added up. Here that meant 13 months, not 2 years 7 months.
- **Fixing the term is part of carrying out the judgment.** The District Court's judgment gave three terms. The petitioners said it did not say how they were to run. The District Court replied that it fixed the term according to the judgment. The Supreme Court did not rule on that point, and it left the convictions alone. It held that the term had to be fixed under Nos. 10 and 41.
- **Time already spent in jail counts.** Both courts counted the term from 2070/3/18, the day the petitioners were first held, not from the day of the judgment. The District Court's own letters used that start date, so it was not in dispute.
- **Habeas corpus can free a convicted prisoner.** A prison slip from a court does not make it lawful to hold someone once the lawful term is over. The Court ordered release within a month of its show-cause order.
- **A similar rule is now in the Sentencing Act.** Section 36(3) of the Criminal Offences (Sentencing and Execution) Act 2074 deals with one person who gets different prison terms for different offences in one case. Only the heaviest is carried out. Section 36(3) does not repeat the case's general exception, "unless an Act says to add up". Instead, s 36(5) lets a separate punishment that the law sets for repeat or combined offences prevail. See the table below.

## Full 20-mark answer

??? example "Exam answer (20 marks)"
    **Question (not from a past paper).** State the facts and examine the ratio decidendi of Samir Khan v Kathmandu District Court.

    **Introduction**

    A criminal judgment does not end when it is signed. It must be carried out. For a prison sentence, the court fixes the term (कैद ठेक्नु): it works out the dates from which and until which the prisoner is held. It then sends the prison a prison slip (कैदीपुर्जी). A hard question arises when one person is convicted of several offences. Are the prison terms added up, or does only the longest count?

    In *Samir Khan v Kathmandu District Court*, a Joint Bench of the Supreme Court answered this question for a case brought by one charge sheet. It held that only the longest term is served, and it freed three prisoners by habeas corpus.

    **Citation**

    Samir Khan et al. v Kathmandu District Court et al., NKP 2071, No. 9, D.N. 9245 (habeas corpus). Supreme Court, Joint Bench: Justices Baidya Nath Upadhyay and Deepak Raj Joshi. Decided 2071/5/9.

    **Facts**

    The three petitioners, Samir Khan, Abdul Khan and Jan Tulla, are from Bangladesh. They said an agent took money from them and gave them Nepali citizenship certificates and passports in Delhi. He sent them to Saudi Arabia for work, but no one met them there. They were sent back to Delhi. From there they were sent to Nepal, because they carried Nepali papers.

    On the Immigration Office's report, the Government of Nepal prosecuted them for forging citizenship and passports. They were held in jail from 2070/3/18 while the case was tried. On 2071/3/17 the Kathmandu District Court convicted them. There was one incident, one charge sheet and one judgment, but three counts. Each got 13 months under section 21(1) of the Nepal Citizenship Act 2063 and 6 months under section 5 of the Passport Act 2024. Each also got 1 year and a Rs 50 fine under the Forgery chapter of the Muluki Ain. They paid the fines.

    When the District Court fixed the term, it added the three terms together, making 2 years 7 months each. Its letters of 2071/4/18 told the prison to hold them until 2072/10/17. The petitioners said the longest term, 13 months counted from 2070/3/18, had ended on 2071/4/17. They asked the Supreme Court for habeas corpus. The government replied that they were held under a lawful judgment, and that they had another remedy.

    **Issues**

    1. In a case brought by one charge sheet, can prison terms under three different laws be added up?
    2. If not, were the petitioners held unlawfully after 2071/4/17?

    **Decision**

    The Court quoted Nos. 10 and 41 of the Punishment chapter of the Muluki Ain. Under No. 10, in a case on one document, prison is imposed only under the Act with the greatest punishment, unless an Act says to add up. Under No. 41, a term on several counts must not go above the greatest count.

    The Court found one incident, one charge sheet and one judgment. The greatest term was the 13 months under the Citizenship Act. Fixing 2 years 7 months by adding the three terms was not lawful. The petitioners had been held since 2070/3/18, so they had served the 13 months on 2071/4/17. The Court issued habeas corpus and ordered their immediate release. Justice Joshi agreed.

    **Ratio decidendi**

    The official headnote, all drawn from paragraph 5, says (our translation):

    1. In a case brought by one charge sheet, prison terms under two or more Acts cannot be added up unless an Act itself provides for it.
    2. Only the prison term under the Act with the greatest term applies. The person does not serve the others, and they cannot be added on. On several counts, the term must not go above the greatest count.
    3. So here the term could not go above 13 months. Adding the three terms to fix 2 years 7 months was not lawful.

    Three points stand out when we examine this ratio. First, it is a rule about carrying out a sentence, not about guilt. The convictions and the three sentences stood. What changed was how much of them the prisoners had to serve. Second, it rests on the text of Nos. 10 and 41. No. 10 speaks of "a case on one document", and the Court applied it to a case brought by one charge sheet. Third, the ratio keeps an exception: terms are added up where an Act itself says so.

    In applying the ratio, the Court counted the 13 months from 2070/3/18, the day the petitioners were first held, not from the judgment. So time already spent in jail counted towards the term. This was not in dispute, because the District Court's own letters used the same start date.

    **Comment**

    In my view the decision is right, because it protects personal liberty. Nobody may be held a day longer than the law allows. A prison slip, even one from a court, cannot authorise more. Habeas corpus suited the case, because holding them had itself become unlawful, and it was still going on.

    The judgment has gaps. It does not answer the government's argument that another remedy was available. Nor does it say whether the District Court should have stated in its judgment how the terms were to run, as the petitioners argued. The reasoning is mostly quotation and application. The Court does not explain why the law prefers the longest term to the total. A likely reason is that one incident should not be punished several times over just because several laws describe it.

    **Significance and conclusion**

    The case is prescribed in Unit 9.8. It is also relevant to Unit 8.1.4(iii), on fixing and enforcing the term of imprisonment. A similar rule now sits in the Criminal Offences (Sentencing and Execution) Act 2074. Section 36(3) says that where one person in one case gets different prison terms for different offences, only the heaviest is carried out. Section 11(3) says that where one act is an offence under different Acts, the punishment under the Act with the greater punishment is carried out. A separate sentence is still fixed for each offence. Unlike the case, s 36(3) has no general "unless an Act says so" exception. Section 36(5) carves out only repeat and combined offences. Section 161 of the Criminal Procedure Code 2074 deducts the time a prisoner was held during the investigation from the prison term.

    In short, one charge sheet leads to one prison term: the longest. Holding a prisoner beyond it is unlawful, and the Supreme Court will free the prisoner by habeas corpus.
