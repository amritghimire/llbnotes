## Facts

Translated and summarised by us from the Nepali judgment.

**The land and the tenancy.** Kallu Tharu, also called Kallu Chaudhary, lived in Hasuliya Village Development Committee, ward 6, Kailali district. The story of his tenancy is his own account, from his petition. He farmed two plots of land registered in the name of Savitri Devi Bhandari, as her tenant (मोही).[^land] A tenant here is a farmer who works another person's land and pays the owner a crop rent (कुत). In 2030 he applied to the Land Administration Office, Kailali, Dhangadhi, to be registered as her tenant.

**The compromise and the certificate.** While that application was still pending, he and the landowner gave a joint application to the Dang, Banke, Kailali Tribunal (ट्रिबुनल) on 2031/1/21.[^dates] They agreed that he would be her tenant and pay her 55 maunds (मन, a traditional grain measure) of paddy a year. A compromise (milapatra) was made under No. 182 of the Adalati Bandobasta. On 2031/3/10 the Land Administration Office gave him a tenancy certificate (मोहियानी हकको प्रमाणपत्र). He said neither the compromise nor the certificate had ever been cancelled. He also said he had paid the rent every year (now 22 quintals of paddy), and the owner had given him receipts.

**The Land Reform Office.** Later Kallu applied to the Land Reform Office, Kailali (भूमिसुधार कार्यालय), under the Lands Act 2021 (भूमि सम्बन्धी ऐन, २०२१).[^sections] He asked it to separate part of the land for him in return for his tenancy right, and to strike the tenancy off the record. In other words, the land would be divided between owner and tenant. The judgment does not give the date of this application. On 2056/5/32 the Office refused. It held that the documents did not prove he was a lawful tenant. They did not show a tenancy document under section 34(1) of the Act, registration as tenant under section 25(2), or a certificate based on these.

**An appeal the law did not allow.** The Office gave Kallu no notice of time to appeal (पुनरावेदनको म्याद). This notice tells a party how long he has to appeal. The Office gave none because no appeal lay. Kallu still appealed (पुनरावेदन) to the Appellate Court, Dipayal, under section 55 of the Lands Act. An appeal asks a higher court to correct a lower decision. He argued that the Office had decided his tenancy right, not just the division of land.

On 2056/11/23 the Appellate Court dismissed the appeal under Adalati Bandobasta No. 180. Its reason was section 26(च) of the Lands Act. That section says no appeal lies to any court against decisions under the land-division sections. So the appeal should never have been registered, and the court did not go into its merits (the substance of the claim). Kallu said the same court still ordered a notice of time to appeal, in the directions at the end of its judgment (तपसिल). He said he was given one.

**The writ petition.** On 2057/7/3 Kallu filed a writ petition (रिट निवेदन) in the Supreme Court. He filed it under Articles 23 and 88(2) of the Constitution of the Kingdom of Nepal 2047 (1990). A writ petition asks the Supreme Court to use its extraordinary jurisdiction (असाधारण अधिकारक्षेत्र). This is its special power to issue orders such as certiorari (उत्प्रेषण) and mandamus (परमादेश). Kallu said both decisions breached Articles 11 and 17 of that Constitution, which the judgment does not quote. He said they were illegal and made without jurisdiction (अधिकारक्षेत्रविहीन), that is, without legal power to decide.

He asked for two things. First, certiorari to quash (cancel) both decisions. Second, mandamus ordering the Land Reform Office to decide again and give him land for his tenancy right.

**In the Supreme Court.** The Court asked the respondents for written replies (लिखित जवाफ), which the syllabus calls a rejoinder. The Appellate Court replied. So did the Land Reform Office and its Land Reform Officer, jointly, and Savitri Devi. The judgment does not list every respondent. The petition was argued on 2059/12/21, and the Joint Bench gave its order on 2060/1/3.

## Issues

The court framed one question (paragraph 13): should the order the petition asks for be issued? It answered separately for each decision the petition attacked (paragraphs 15 to 18):

1. Can a writ quash the Appellate Court's judgment, when the Administration of Justice Act 2048 gives an appeal (s 9) and revision (s 12)?
2. Having lost his appeal against the Land Reform Office's decision, can Kallu attack the same decision again by writ?
3. If a writ could look at that decision, did Kallu come too late (laches)?

The first issue is the question the exam asks: is writ jurisdiction available when an alternative remedy (वैकल्पिक उपचार) exists? An alternative remedy is another route the law gives to correct a decision, such as an appeal.

## Arguments

**For Kallu, the petitioner** (Advocate Dwarika Man Joshi), from the petition and the hearing:

- He is a lawful tenant. The compromise at the Tribunal and his certificate have never been cancelled, and the owner has accepted his rent for years.
- He asked the Land Reform Office only to divide the land. The Office went beyond his claim and held that he was not a lawful tenant. It acted with prejudice, on a whim, without weighing the evidence on file.
- Because the Office decided his tenancy right, he could appeal under section 55 of the Lands Act.
- The Appellate Court contradicted itself. It dismissed his appeal as one that could not be registered, yet ordered a notice of time to appeal.
- He came by writ even though he was given that notice. He relied on section 26(च) and on two Supreme Court precedents:
    - *Lilamani Pantha v Dhundiraj Pantha* (Supreme Court Bulletin, full issue 120, 2054, p. 23). Suppose the first decision rejected a claim and the appeal was dismissed. If the two differ only in words, no further appeal lies.
    - *Gunakhar Sapkota v Padam Prasad Upadhyay* (Supreme Court Bulletin, full issue 178, 2056, p. 4). A notice of time to appeal from a court does not by itself give a right to appeal.
- Both decisions should be quashed, and the Land Reform Office ordered to give him his tenant's share.

**For the respondents** (Deputy Government Attorney Sharad Kumar Khadka for the Land Reform Office; Advocate Pawan Kumar Ojha for Savitri Devi). The judgment spells Khadka two ways, खड्का and खँड्का. A Deputy Government Attorney (उपन्यायाधिवक्ता) is a government lawyer. Their points, from the written replies and the hearing:

- **The Appellate Court:** section 26(च) of the Lands Act bars appeals against the prescribed officer's decisions under sections 26(ख), 26(घ) and 26(ङ). This dispute fell under section 26. The appeal should not have been registered, so it was dismissed under Adalati Bandobasta No. 180 without going into its merits. The court acted within the law and harmed none of Kallu's rights.
- **The Land Reform Office and its Land Reform Officer:** the evidence did not prove that Kallu was a lawful tenant. The case had been decided at two levels and was final. No law lets him reopen it by certiorari. "While another route of remedy exists", certiorari cannot create a right against a judicial body's decision.
- **Savitri Devi Bhandari, the landowner:** to quash the Appellate Court's decision, Kallu had to apply for revision under section 12 of the Administration of Justice Act 2048. Revision (दोहोर्‍याई हेर्ने) means the Supreme Court looks again at a case a lower court has already decided. Under today's Act (2073 s 12(5)), the Supreme Court must first give permission. A petition filed by the wrong process should be dismissed. Kallu knowingly chose a remedy the law did not allow, and came late. So laches defeats him.

    !!! warning "Unverified"
        We have not seen the text of s 12 of the 2048 Act, so we could not check how revision worked under it.

- **At the hearing**, Advocate Ojha said Kallu could challenge the Appellate Court's decision only by appeal or revision, not by writ. If the petition attacked the Land Reform Office's decision, it was too late. Deputy Government Attorney Khadka agreed.

## Decision

The Joint Bench dismissed the writ petition. Justice Khil Raj Regmi wrote the order, and Justice Dilip Kumar Paudel agreed.

1. **No writ against the Appellate Court's judgment** (paragraph 16). Against an Appellate Court judgment, the route to the Supreme Court is set by the Administration of Justice Act 2048. It is an appeal under section 9, or revision under section 12. "Apart from that, it is not a matter for the writ jurisdiction at all." So the claim to quash that judgment was "meaningless from the outset" (प्रारम्भतः निरर्थक).
2. **The Land Reform Office decided the division, not the tenancy** (paragraph 15). Kallu asked for the land to be divided, and being a tenant was only the ground for that request. A reason given for a decision is not the decision itself. The decision was on division, and section 26(च) allows no appeal against it. That is why the Office gave no notice of time to appeal. Kallu appealed on his own reasoning (मनोगत तर्क), and lost.
3. **No second route against the same decision** (paragraph 17). Kallu appealed against the Land Reform Office's decision, lost, and then attacked the same decision by writ. It is not our judicial tradition or principle to review the same decision again and again. The principle of judicial certainty (न्यायिक निश्चितता) does not let a party who fails by one judicial route take another. Judicial certainty means a decided matter should stay settled, not be reopened by one route after another.
4. **Laches** (paragraphs 17 and 18). The court then took Kallu's own argument: the Appellate Court dismissed his appeal without deciding the merits, so a writ could look at the decision. Even on that view, the respondents' point about delay was relevant. The court said:
    - A person seeking justice must come to court in time. Our system has adopted the principle that, for writs, "delay defeats equity" (विलम्बले समन्यायलाई पराजित गर्छ).
    - No fixed period makes a delay laches. Matters such as whether the petitioner, knowing his right, came in due time to use it are relevant.
    - The petitioner must give a satisfactory and reasonable reason for any delay. Otherwise the writ is refused.
    - Time spent seeking a remedy the law does not provide also counts as delay. The court cited this principle in English: "If he (petitioner runs after a remedy not provided in the statute or the statutory rules,the court cannot condone delay". The judgment prints it that way, without the closing bracket. To condone means to excuse.

    Kallu filed on 2057/7/3, more than a year after the Office's decision of 2056/5/32. By our count, that was also over seven months after the Appellate Court's judgment. The judgment records no reason from Kallu for the delay. The court followed its earlier decision in NKP 2043, No. 10, D.N. 2891. Suppose a party goes to the wrong body through his own jurisdictional mistake, then files a writ late. The writ jurisdiction cannot help him benefit from that mistake. A jurisdictional mistake is a mistake about which body has power to decide. So laches applied.

5. **Order** (paragraph 19). There was no legal or principled ground to issue the order asked for. The writ petition was dismissed (खारेज).

The court never decided whether Kallu was in fact a lawful tenant. The judgment does not say what Kallu did after the order.

## Ratio decidendi

Our translation of the official headnote, point by point:

1. To have an Appellate Court judgment quashed, a party comes to the Supreme Court by the route the Administration of Justice Act 2048 provides. That is an appeal under section 9 or revision under section 12. Apart from that, it is not a matter to be examined under the writ jurisdiction at all. So the writ petition's claim to quash the Appellate Court judgment here appears meaningless from the outset. (प्रकरण नं. १७ र १८)[^headnote]
2. It is not our judicial tradition or principle to review the same decision again and again, asking whether it was right. And the principle of judicial certainty does not let a party who has failed by one judicial route be given, or take, another. (प्रकरण नं. १७ र १८)
3. Suppose a petitioner spends time seeking a remedy that the law does not provide, and so comes late. The court must still count that as delay (laches). (प्रकरण नं. १७ र १८)
4. Here the petitioner spent time by going to the wrong body, and this writ petition came after a long time. It cannot be said that the principle of laches does not apply. (प्रकरण नं. १७ र १८)

**In short (our summary):** a writ is not a substitute for appeal or revision. A party who loses on one route cannot start again on another. Time spent chasing a remedy the law does not give counts as delay.

## Why it matters

- **A writ is not a substitute for appeal or revision.** Where the law gives a route to challenge a judgment, the party must use it. A writ petition to quash an Appellate Court judgment was "meaningless from the outset". This is the alternative remedy rule, relevant to writ proceedings in Unit 7.1.
- **One route, once.** A party who loses on one route cannot attack the same decision again on another. The court called this judicial certainty. It is close to the idea behind res judicata, though the judgment does not use that term.
- **Laches counts the time spent on a wrong remedy.** The court counted more than a year from the Land Reform Office's decision. It also called the gap after the Appellate Court's dismissal a long time. Chasing an appeal the law did not allow did not stop the clock. This is the point for Unit 2.1.4.
- **Laches has no fixed period.** Whether the petitioner came in time is relevant. He must give a satisfactory and reasonable reason for any delay.
- **Procedure can decide a case.** The court never decided whether Kallu was a lawful tenant. He lost because he took the wrong route and came late.
- **The constitutional limit continues.** For a legal right other than a fundamental right, a writ lies only where no other remedy exists, or the remedy is inadequate or ineffective. Article 88(2) of the 1990 Constitution said this when Kallu filed. Articles 133(2) and 144(1) of the Constitution of Nepal 2015 say it now. Rule 40(1)(घ) of the Supreme Court Rules 2074 asks writ petitions to show it. The court itself relied on the appeal and revision routes in the Administration of Justice Act 2048, not on Article 88(2). Its holdings on judicial certainty and laches remain court-made. See the table below.

## Full 20-mark answer

??? example "Exam answer (20 marks)"
    **2077 Q3.** Whether the writ jurisdiction is availabel [sic] or not when there is existence of alternative remedy? Explain on the basis of the case of Kallu Chaudhary Vs. Appellate court Dipayal.

    **Introduction**

    A writ is a special order of a higher court. Certiorari quashes an unlawful decision, and mandamus orders an authority to do its legal duty. In Nepal the Supreme Court issues writs under its extraordinary jurisdiction (असाधारण अधिकारक्षेत्र). It is called extraordinary because it is not the normal way to correct a decision. The normal ways are appeal and revision, which statutes provide.

    The Constitution draws the line itself. Article 88(2) of the Constitution of the Kingdom of Nepal 2047 let the Supreme Court enforce fundamental rights. For any other legal right, it could act only where no other remedy had been provided, or where that remedy appeared inadequate or ineffective. Article 133(2) of the Constitution of Nepal 2015 keeps the same test in almost the same words. Article 144(1) gives the High Courts a similar power. This is the rule of alternative remedy (वैकल्पिक उपचार). *Kallu Chaudhary v Appellate Court, Dipayal* shows how the Supreme Court applies it.

    **Citation**

    Kallu Tharu alias Kallu Chaudhary v Appellate Court, Dipayal, Doti, et al., NKP 2060, No. 3/4, D.N. 7209 (Writ No. 3063 of 2057). Supreme Court, Joint Bench: Justices Dilip Kumar Paudel and Khil Raj Regmi. Order of 2060/1/3.

    **Facts**

    Kallu farmed land registered in the name of Savitri Devi Bhandari in Kailali. He said a compromise in 2031 had made him her tenant, and he held a tenancy certificate. He asked the Land Reform Office, Kailali, under section 26 of the Lands Act 2021, to divide the land between owner and tenant. On 2056/5/32 the Office refused, holding that he was not proved to be a lawful tenant.

    Section 26(च) of the Lands Act allows no appeal to any court against such a decision. Kallu still appealed to the Appellate Court, Dipayal. On 2056/11/23 it dismissed the appeal without hearing its merits. On 2057/7/3 Kallu filed a writ petition in the Supreme Court. He asked for certiorari to quash both decisions, and mandamus ordering the Office to divide the land.

    The respondents argued that the Appellate Court's judgment could be challenged only by appeal or revision under the Administration of Justice Act 2048. They also said the petition came too late.

    **Issues**

    1. Can a writ quash an Appellate Court judgment when the law gives an appeal and revision against it?
    2. Having lost his appeal, can Kallu attack the Land Reform Office's decision again by writ?
    3. Was the petition barred by delay (laches)?

    **Decision**

    The Joint Bench dismissed the writ petition. Justice Khil Raj Regmi wrote the order, and Justice Paudel agreed.

    1. Against an Appellate Court judgment, the route to the Supreme Court is set by the Administration of Justice Act 2048. It is an appeal (section 9) or revision (section 12). Apart from that, it is not a writ matter at all. So the claim to quash that judgment was meaningless from the outset (paragraph 16).
    2. Kallu had already appealed against the Land Reform Office's decision and lost. Judicial certainty does not let a party who fails by one route try another (paragraph 17).
    3. Even if a writ could look at that decision, Kallu came more than a year after it. Time spent on a remedy the law does not provide counts as delay (paragraphs 17 and 18).

    **Ratio decidendi**

    1. Against an Appellate Court judgment, the route is appeal or revision under the Administration of Justice Act 2048, not a writ.
    2. The same decision cannot be reviewed again and again. A party who fails by one judicial route cannot take another.
    3. Time spent seeking a remedy the law does not provide counts as delay, so laches applies.

    **Is writ jurisdiction available when an alternative remedy exists?**

    According to this case, no. Where the law gives a remedy such as appeal or revision, the party must use it. The writ jurisdiction does not open because a party prefers it. Kallu said the Appellate Court had even given him a notice of time to appeal. He came by writ instead, and the Supreme Court refused to look at the merits at all.

    The case also shows the other side of the rule. Against the Land Reform Office's decision there was no appeal, because section 26(च) bars it. So an appeal was never a real alternative remedy. Kallu treated it as one, and his writ came more than a year after the decision. The court held that this time counts against him. An alternative remedy must be one the law actually gives. Chasing a remedy the law does not give is no excuse for delay.

    The rule has an exception, written into the Constitution. A writ may still lie where the alternative remedy appears inadequate or ineffective (Article 88(2) then, Articles 133(2) and 144(1) now). Rule 40(1)(घ) of the Supreme Court Rules 2074 now asks a writ petition to show this. The Kallu judgment does not discuss the exception.

    **Comment**

    In my view, the decision is right on the main point. If writs were open whenever a party liked, appeal and revision would lose their meaning. The rule keeps each route in its place and gives finality to decisions.

    But the result is hard on Kallu. The court never decided whether he was a lawful tenant. He said he held a certificate and had paid rent for years. Kallu argued that no appeal lay to the Supreme Court, citing two precedents. The court did not answer this directly. But its holding also names revision under section 12, which the landowner's reply had pointed to. So even if no appeal lay, he could have applied for revision. The judgment does not say whether it would have been granted. Finally, the court set no fixed period for laches. It asked whether he came in due time, and for a satisfactory and reasonable reason for any delay.

    **Significance and conclusion**

    This case is prescribed in Unit 9.6, and is relevant to Units 2.1.4 (laches) and 7.1 (writ proceedings). The constitutional limit it applies was in Article 88(2) of the 1990 Constitution. It continues in Articles 133(2) and 144(1) of the Constitution of Nepal 2015, and rule 40(1)(घ) of the Supreme Court Rules 2074. The holdings on judicial certainty and laches remain court-made.

    So writ jurisdiction is generally not available where the law provides an alternative remedy, such as appeal or revision. It is available where no other remedy exists, or where the remedy is inadequate or ineffective. Even then, the petitioner must take the right route promptly, because time spent on the wrong one counts as delay.
