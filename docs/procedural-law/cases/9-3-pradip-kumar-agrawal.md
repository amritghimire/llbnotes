## Facts

Translated and summarised by us from the Nepali judgment.

**The industry and its exemption.** Pradip Kumar Agrawal was the proprietor of P.K. Chemicals, in ward 9 of Biratnagar Municipality, Morang. It made dry cell batteries and started production on 2041/12/4.[^dates] He relied on section 10(क)(1) of the Industrial Enterprises Act 2038 (औद्योगिक व्यवसाय ऐन, २०३८), as it then stood. It gave a productive industry a 100% income-tax exemption for six years from the start of production. Section 10(क)(2) let an industry that got the facility keep it for the whole period.

**The Gazette notice.** A government notice in the Nepal Gazette (नेपाल राजपत्र) of 2043/1/15 said industries making dry cell batteries would get no income-tax exemption. The petitioner said the notice could not touch an industry already enjoying the facility.

**The tax assessment.** The Tax Office, Morang, Biratnagar, fixed the industry's net income for fiscal year 2042/43 and assessed income tax on it. A tax assessment (कर निर्धारण) is an official order fixing how much tax is due. The judgment gives two dates for this order. The petition, as summarised, says 2048/9/28, served on him on 2048/10/8. Justice Bastola's opinion, repeated in the dissent, says 2047/4/25.

**The writ petition.** The petitioner did not use the remedies in the Income Tax Act. He went straight to the Supreme Court with a writ petition (रिट निवेदन). A writ is an order, such as certiorari or mandamus, that the Supreme Court issues under its extraordinary power. He filed under Article 71 of the Constitution of Nepal 2019 B.S. (नेपालको संविधान, २०१९). He said the assessment blocked his rights to equality and property under that Constitution, and his rights under the Industrial Enterprises Act. He said he had no other legal remedy. He asked for certiorari quashing the assessment, and mandamus ordering the six-year exemption.

**The replies.** A single justice ordered the respondents to file a written reply (लिखित जवाफ), which the syllabus calls a rejoinder.

- **The Tax Department** said the Act let the government name, by Gazette notice, cottage industries (घरेलु उद्योग) that would get no exemption. The Ministry of Industry's notice of 2043/1/15 named this industry. The industry had not received the exemption before the notice, and could not show that it had.
- **The Tax Office, Biratnagar** said a dry-cell industry was not a productive industry under section 9(1)(क) of the Act. The industry could not produce a certificate of exemption. Section 33(1) of the Income Tax Act 2031 obliged the Office to assess within four years of the return being filed. So it assessed as the law required. If unhappy, the petitioner could have made the deposit the law requires. He could then either apply to the Director General under section 34(2), or appeal to the Revenue Tribunal (राजस्व न्यायाधिकरण) under section 57.

**The Joint Bench splits.** On 2049/1/3 the case came before a Joint Bench.

- **Justice Gajendra Keshari Bastola** held that the industry was entitled to the six-year exemption. He would quash the assessment by certiorari and order the exemption by mandamus.
- **Justice Laxman Prasad Aryal** said an appeal is a strong remedy. The petitioner had skipped it and chosen the writ, a remedy for extraordinary situations. He would dismiss the writ.

As they disagreed, the case went to a Full Bench under rule 3(क) of the Supreme Court Rules 2021.

**Two Full Bench decisions in conflict.** The Full Bench found two earlier Full Bench decisions pointing different ways (paragraph 11):

- **Parshuram Jha v His Majesty's Government, Council of Ministers Secretariat** (2048/6/10; NKP 2048, p. 233; D.N. 4300). The petitioner could have applied for leave to appeal, that is, permission to appeal. Even so, the Court gave him relief by writ.
- **HMG, Department of Customs v Ram Rikshpal Agrawal** (2048/8/9; NKP 2049, p. 731). The writ was dismissed because the petitioner had come by writ without using his right of appeal.

The Full Bench asked which principle to follow. It sent the case to a Larger Full Bench under rule 3(2) of the Supreme Court Rules 2049. The dissent adds the reason. This writ was registered on 2047/6/2, before Ram Rikshpal, when writs were heard even where an appeal lay to the Revenue Tribunal.

## Issues

The majority did not number its issues. From paragraphs 11 to 16, the Larger Full Bench had two questions (our framing):

1. **The referred question** (paragraphs 11 and 12). Should the Court follow Parshuram Jha, where a writ was granted despite another remedy, or Ram Rikshpal, where it was refused? Behind it lies the question for Unit 2: when is an earlier decision a binding precedent (नजिर)?
2. **The question that split the Joint Bench** (paragraphs 13 to 16). Should the writ be dismissed because the Income Tax Act 2031 gave other remedies? Counsel gave three reasons why not. The deposit made those remedies ineffective. The Industrial Enterprises Act gave no appeal. And a fundamental right had been violated.

The dissent also asked whether the industry was entitled to the exemption under the Industrial Enterprises Act 2038.

## Arguments

**For Pradip Kumar Agrawal, the petitioner** (Advocate Harishankar Niraula):

- The industry has made dry cell batteries since 2041/12/4. Under section 10(क)(1) of the Industrial Enterprises Act 2038, as it then stood, it owed no income tax for six years.
- That Act gives no appeal when its exemption is refused, so he had to come by writ. Industries refused this exemption have long come by writ.
- Under the Constitution of Nepal 2019 B.S., a writ was dismissed as soon as another remedy existed. Under Article 88(2) of the Constitution of the Kingdom of Nepal 2047, a writ may issue if that remedy is ineffective.
- An appeal under section 57 of the Income Tax Act needs a 50% deposit, with a 25% fine if the appeal fails. That makes the remedy ineffective.
- The majority records three more points (paragraphs 15 and 16). The Industrial Enterprises Act is a powerful Act whose facility a tax assessment cannot take away. The assessment took away the fundamental right to property. And the tax officer lacked jurisdiction (अधिकार क्षेत्र), the legal power to decide.

**For the respondents** (Advocate Balram K.C.; paragraph 10 says he appeared for the Ministry of Finance and others):

- A taxpayer unhappy with an assessment must appeal to the Revenue Tribunal under section 57 of the Income Tax Act 2031. He cannot skip it and come by writ.
- The Court has dismissed many writs where another remedy existed, under both the 2047 Constitution (Article 88(2)) and the 2019 Constitution (Article 71).
- The law set up the Revenue Tribunal to hear exactly these appeals. A writ instead would leave that body pointless.
- The petitioner should first appeal to the Tribunal. Only if its remedy proved inadequate or ineffective could he come to the Supreme Court under Article 88(2).

## Decision

The Larger Full Bench dismissed the writ petition by 5 to 2. Chief Justice Upadhyay wrote the majority order. Justices Mohan Prasad Sharma, Arbinda Nath Acharya, Uday Raj Upadhyay and Hari Prasad Sharma agreed.

1. **Parshuram Jha laid down no general rule** (paragraph 12). There the Court held that leave to appeal under section 9(3) of the former Civil Service Act 2013 was an effective remedy. Yet it granted the writ. Earlier cases, from Kul Narayan Dangol v Chief District Officer, Lalitpur, onward, had refused to treat leave to appeal as a certain remedy. So it was unjust to blame Parshuram Jha for having "lost the way". That ruling turned on its own situation. It did not say a writ may issue whenever another remedy exists. No decision had held an ordinary appeal to be uncertain or ineffective. Several had dismissed writs because an appeal was available.
2. **Unreasoned decisions are not precedents** (paragraph 12). Some decisions had granted writs despite a right of appeal. They never explained why the appeal was inadequate or ineffective, or how that fitted Article 88(2). Such decisions "cannot take the place of an authoritative precedent". Only an interpretation of law, or a legal principle, on which a case is decided is a precedent. So the referred question needed no further answer.
3. **Two certain and effective remedies** (paragraph 13). The taxpayer could apply to the Director General under section 34(2). Suppose the application showed that an irregular or careless assessment had raised his tax. The Director General then had to cancel it and order a fresh one. Or the taxpayer could appeal to the Revenue Tribunal under section 57. He had to choose one.
4. **A deposit does not make a remedy ineffective** (paragraph 14). The petition had not raised this; counsel did. If it were so, any taxpayer could bypass sections 34 and 57 against any assessment. Nepali law often requires a deposit, bail or surrender before an appeal. Appealing on a deposit, instead of paying everything first, is only a temporary facility.
5. **The Industrial Enterprises Act does not shut out other laws** (paragraph 15). It governs facilities for industries, not their duties under other laws. Section 42(2) of the Income Tax Act gave the tax officer jurisdiction to assess an exempt industry separately. Section 42(4) required such an industry still to file returns. If he wrongly refused the exemption, sections 34 and 57 gave the remedy.
6. **Fundamental rights and void decisions** (paragraph 16). The Court treated the assessment as a judicial or quasi-judicial decision. A quasi-judicial authority is not a court, but decides disputes like one. Its decision within jurisdiction stands until set aside, even if it hits someone's property. But the Court can and must help at once where the decision is void (शून्य), that is, of no effect in law. That means one made without jurisdiction, under an unconstitutional law, by a body without power, or without a hearing. None of these applied here.
7. **Order** (paragraph 16). Justice Aryal's opinion, dismissing the writ, was right. A copy was to go to the respondents through the Office of the Attorney General.

The majority did not decide whether the industry was entitled to the exemption. It held that the tax remedies were the way to raise that. The judgment does not say what the petitioner did next.

**The dissent** (Justice Trilok Pratap Rana; Justice Krishna Kumar Barma agreed):

- **Follow Parshuram Jha.** The Court had granted many similar writs, including several on exemptions under the Industrial Enterprises Act. This writ should be granted too. Only petitions registered "after the decision" should be dismissed; the dissent does not say which decision.
- **The exemption was due.** Production started on 2041/12/4, before the Gazette notice, and section 10(क)(2) kept the facility for six years. The Act is a special Act, and its section 21 makes it prevail over other law.
- **The assessment was void.** Taxing an exempt industry went beyond the Tax Office's authority. As the Industrial Enterprises Act gives no appeal, a writ under Article 88(2) was proper. Where authority is exceeded, the alternative remedy need not be used. A hard and burdensome appeal may also be ineffective.
- **Full justice.** Article 88(2) aims at full justice and rejects rigidity. "A precedent is just like law." The petitioner followed the precedents of his time; it is not just to say he lost his way.
- **His order.** Quash, by certiorari, the Tax Office's decision, its written note (पर्चा) and the assessment. Order, by mandamus, the six-year exemption from 2041/12/4. He agreed with Justice Bastola.

## Ratio decidendi

Our translation of the official headnote, point by point. The headnote shortens प्रकरण नं. (paragraph number) to प्र.नं.

1. In fact, not every court decision interprets the law or lays down a legal principle. Decisions without such interpretation or discussion are not precedents. They do not bind anyone except the parties to the case. (प्रकरण नं. १२)
2. A court may interpret a law or lay down a legal principle, and decide the case on that basis. Only then does that interpretation or principle become a precedent for other cases of the same kind. (प्रकरण नं. १२)
3. For the purpose of precedent, a court must look at the discussion of the law and of legal principle. (प्रकरण नं. १२)
4. This Court has, in one or a few cases, given relief by writ although the legal remedy of appeal was available. That alone does not mean those decisions must be accepted as precedents. (प्रकरण नं. १२)
5. There is no reasonable ground to treat these legal remedies as inadequate or ineffective merely because a deposit or an extra fee is required. If they were, the same would be true of every tax assessment order. Taxpayers could then come straight to this Court's extraordinary jurisdiction against any assessment order. Sections 34 and 57 would in effect become meaningless and inactive. (प्रकरण नं. १४)
6. Whatever decision a judicial or quasi-judicial officer or body makes within its jurisdiction is final until a higher authority sets it aside under the law. The party concerned must obey it. The law lets that party apply or appeal to the higher authority without paying at once the penalty, claim or other sum the decision imposes. Instead, the party gives a deposit or security for all or part of it. Or he must pay an extra fee of some per cent if the appeal fails. This is only a temporary facility the law gives. It is not reasonable to say that it makes the legal remedy meaningless or ineffective. (प्रकरण नं. १४)
7. Every Act is strong and effective within its own field and limits. In that sense, the Industrial Enterprises Act is certainly more effective than other Acts on the facilities and concessions given to industries. But that does not mean an industry or entrepreneur enjoying those facilities need not perform the duties, or bear the liabilities, that other laws impose. (प्रकरण नं. १५)
8. Section 42(2) of the Income Tax Act deals with industries wholly or partly exempt from income tax under the law on industrial enterprises. For their industrial income, the tax officer makes a separate assessment and gives the exemption. Section 42(4) requires those who enjoy an income-tax exemption still to file the income statement the Act requires, with all supporting evidence. So an exemption under the Industrial Enterprises Act does not free an industry from filing statements. Nor does it stop the tax officer from working out which income comes from industrial production, and assessing the tax. Section 42(2) requires even such an industry to be assessed separately, with the exempt tax left out. That provision shows that the tax officer has jurisdiction to make the assessment. (प्रकरण नं. १५)
9. A judicial or quasi-judicial officer or body gets its power from the Constitution or law. Acting within its jurisdiction, it may make a decision that affects a person's property or puts a financial liability on him. That alone does not mean the decision has taken away or invaded a fundamental right. (प्रकरण नं. १६)
10. Every dispute that a judicial officer or body settles involves a person's life, property, honour or status. The Constitution or law gives them power to settle such disputes. They may make mistakes in weighing evidence or in interpreting or applying the law. Even so, the decision is valid in law until a higher authority sets it aside. So the party must first follow the path of the legal remedy to correct the mistake. Otherwise any party harmed by such a decision could go straight to a writ, claiming a hurt to his fundamental right to property or life. Giving judicial or quasi-judicial power, and providing applications or appeals against its decisions, would then serve no purpose. (प्रकरण नं. १६)
11. A party may come to this Court's extraordinary jurisdiction claiming one of these things about a judicial or quasi-judicial officer's decision or order. It was made without jurisdiction. The law applied is itself unconstitutional. A body with no power took jurisdiction. Or, against natural justice or the law, the affected party was given no chance to defend himself. If the claim appears proper, this Court certainly can, and must, help. Such a decision is as good as void in law, so an appeal or other legal remedy against it has no meaning. (प्रकरण नं. १६)

**In short (our summary):** a decision binds as precedent only through its reasoned interpretation of law or legal principle; a bare result binds only the parties. A writ is not a shortcut past an effective remedy, and a deposit does not make a remedy ineffective. But a void decision can be challenged by writ at once.

    आयकर ऐनको दफा ४२ को उपदफा (२) मा औद्योगिक व्यवसाय सम्बन्धी प्रचलित नेपाल कानून अनुसार पूर्ण वा आंशिक रुपले आयकर नलाग्ने उद्योगको औद्योगिक आयको हकमा कर अधिकृतले छुट्टै कर निर्धारण गरी त्यस्तो छुट दिने व्यवस्था भएको र सोही दफाको उपदफा (४) मा आयकर छुटको सुविधा पाएका व्यक्तिहरुले पनि सो ऐन बमोजिम दाखिल गर्नु पर्ने आयको विवरण र त्यस्को पुष्ट्याईको निमित्त चाहिने सबै प्रमाण कर अधिकृत समक्ष दाखिल गर्नु पर्ने व्यवस्था भएको देखिनाले कुनै उद्योगले औद्योगिक व्यवसाय ऐन अन्तर्गत आयकर छुटको सुविधा पाएको छ भन्दैमा त्यस्तो उद्योगले ती कानूनी व्यवस्थाहरु बमोजिम विवरण दाखिल गर्नु पर्ने कर्तव्यको पालन गर्न नपर्ने अथवा कर अधिकृतले सो उद्योगको आय मध्ये कुन औद्योगिक उत्पादनबाट भएको आय हो र कुन होइन भन्ने कुरा यकिन गर्न र आयकरको रकम निर्धारण गर्न नपाउने अवस्था देखिन्न । त्यस्तो उद्योगको हकमा पनि कानून बमोजिम छुट दिनु पर्ने करको रकम छुट दिइ छुट्टै कर निर्धारण गर्नु पर्ने उपयुक्त उपदफा (२) को कानूनी प्रावधानले तत्सम्बन्धमा कर अधिकृतलाई कर निर्धारण गर्ने अधिकार क्षेत्र भएकै देखिन आउने ।

    (प्र.नं. १५)

    संविधान वा कानून बमोजिम अधिकार प्राप्त न्यायिक वा अर्ध न्यायिक अधिकारी वा निकायले आफ्नो अधिकार क्षेत्र भित्र रहेर गरेको कुनै न्यायिक निर्णयको परिणामस्वरुप कुनै व्यक्तिको सम्पत्तिमा असर पर्न गएको वा निज उपर कुनै आर्थिक दायित्वको सृजना हुन गएको छ भने पनि सो कारणले मात्र सो निर्णयबाट मौलिक हकको अपहरण भएको वा त्यस्तो हकमा सो निर्णयले अतिक्रमण गरेको सम्झन नमिल्ने ।

    (प्र.नं. १६)

    न्यायिक अधिकारी वा निकायबाट निरुपण हुने प्रत्येक विवादमा मानिसको जिउ ज्यान, सम्पत्ति, इज्जत वा हैसियत सम्बन्धी कुराहरु समावेश भएका हुन्छन् । त्यस्तो विवादको निरुपण गर्ने भनेर संविधान वा कानूनले त्यस्ता अधिकारी वा निकायलाई अधिकार प्रदान गरेको हुन्छ । त्यस्तो विवादको निर्णय गर्दा प्रमाणको मुल्यांकनमा वा कानूनको व्याख्या वा प्रयोगमा त्रुटी भएको हुन सक्दछ, तथापि कानून बमोजिम माथिल्लो अधिकारी वा निकायबाट बदर नभएसम्म त्यो निर्णय कानूनको दृष्टिमा वैध नै हुन्छ । त्यसैले त्यस्तो त्रुटीलाई सच्याउन सम्बन्धित पक्षले पहिले कानूनी उपचारको बाटो नै अनुशरण गर्नु पर्छ । अन्यथा, त्यस्तो निर्णयबाट प्रतिकूल असर पर्ने पक्षले आफ्नो सम्पत्ति वा जिउज्यान सम्बन्धी मौलिक हकमा आघात परेको छ भनी सोझै रिट क्षेत्रबाट उपचार खोज्न पाउने हो भने संविधान वा कानूनले विवादको निरुपण गर्न न्यायिक वा अर्धन्यायिक अधिकार प्रदान गर्नु र त्यस्तो निर्णय उपर निवेदन वा पुनरावेदन लाग्ने व्यवस्था गर्नुको कुनै अर्थ वा प्रयोजन नै नहुने ।

    (प्र.नं. १६)

    न्यायिक वा अर्धन्यायिक अधिकारीको निर्णय वा आदेश अधिकार क्षेत्रविहिन छ वा निजले प्रयोग गरेको कानून नै संविधान सम्मत छैन वा अधिकारै नभएको निकायले अधिकार क्षेत्र ग्रहण गरेको छ अथवा प्राकृतिक न्यायको सिद्धान्त वा कानून विपरित मर्का पर्ने पक्षलाई प्रतिवाद गर्ने मौका नै नदिई निर्णय भएको छ भन्ने जिकिर लिएर सम्बन्धित पक्ष त्यस्को उपचारको लागि यस अदालतको असाधारण अधिकारमा आएको छ र निजको जिकिर उचित देखिन्छ भने त्यस अवस्थामा निजलाई यस अदालतले निश्चय नै मद्दत गर्न सक्दछ र गर्नु पर्छ । त्यस्तो निर्णय कानूनको दृष्टिमा शून्य सरह हुने हुनाले तत्सम्बन्धमा पुनरावेदन वा अन्य कानूनी उपचारको कुनै अर्थ नै नहुने ।

    (प्र.नं. १६)

## Why it matters

- **What makes a decision a precedent.** Only the interpretation of law, or legal principle, on which a case is decided binds later courts. A decision that gives relief without reasons is not a precedent; it binds only its parties. This is the idea of the ratio decidendi, though the judgment does not use the term.
- **Bare results do not bind.** The earlier writs granted despite a right of appeal gave no reasons. So the Court would not treat them as authoritative precedent. Lawyers say such a point passed sub silentio; the judgment does not use that term either.
- **Conflicting precedents.** The Court did not overrule Parshuram Jha. It distinguished it, that is, showed that the earlier case rested on different facts. That case turned on the special history of leave to appeal and laid down no general rule. The result matches Ram Rikshpal, though the majority does not say so.
- **A writ is not a shortcut, unless the decision is void.** Use an effective remedy, such as an appeal, first; a deposit does not make it ineffective. A void decision can be challenged by writ at once. One example is a decision made without a hearing. That breaks the natural justice rule audi alteram partem, though the judgment says only "natural justice".
- **The dissent's warning about fairness.** Justice Rana said a precedent is like law, and a litigant who followed the precedents of his day should not be turned away. In our view his answer, applying the stricter rule only to later petitions, is close to prospective overruling: changing a rule only for future cases.
- **The same rules today.** Article 128(4) of the Constitution of Nepal 2015 binds everyone to the Supreme Court's interpretations and legal principles. Article 133(2) keeps the "inadequate or ineffective" test for writs. See the table below.

## Full 20-mark answer

??? example "Exam answer (20 marks)"
    **Practice question** (not asked in the 2077 or 2080 papers, or in 2079 Q1-6; our 2079 copy is cut off at Q7). State the facts and examine the ratio decidendi of Pradip Kumar Agrawal v Tax Office, Morang.

    **Introduction**

    A precedent (नजिर) is an earlier court decision that guides or binds courts in later similar cases. Under stare decisis, courts stand by what has been decided. But not everything in a judgment binds. Only the ratio decidendi, the legal principle on which the case is decided, binds. Remarks made in passing are obiter dicta, which only persuade. In Nepal, Article 128(4) of the 2015 Constitution binds everyone to the Supreme Court's interpretations of law and to the legal principles it lays down.

    *Pradip Kumar Agrawal v Tax Office, Morang* is a leading Nepali case on what makes a decision a precedent. A Larger Full Bench of seven justices held that only a reasoned interpretation of law or legal principle binds. It also held that a writ is not a shortcut past an effective appeal.

    **Citation**

    Pradip Kumar Agrawal v Tax Office, Morang, Biratnagar, et al., NKP 2052, No. 7, p. 542, D.N. 6032. Supreme Court, Larger Full Bench. The bench was Chief Justice Bishwanath Upadhyay and Justices Trilok Pratap Rana, Mohan Prasad Sharma, Arbinda Nath Acharya, Uday Raj Upadhyay, Hari Prasad Sharma and Krishna Kumar Barma. Decided 2052/1/11, by 5 votes to 2.

    **Facts**

    The petitioner was the proprietor of P.K. Chemicals, Biratnagar, which began making dry cell batteries on 2041/12/4. Section 10(क)(1) of the Industrial Enterprises Act 2038 then gave productive industries a six-year income-tax exemption. A Gazette notice of 2043/1/15 said dry-cell industries would get no exemption. The Tax Office, Morang, assessed income tax on the industry for fiscal year 2042/43.

    The Income Tax Act 2031 gave two remedies against an assessment. A taxpayer could apply to the Director General (s 34) or appeal to the Revenue Tribunal (s 57). The petitioner used neither. He filed a writ petition in the Supreme Court for certiorari and mandamus.

    A Joint Bench split. Justice Bastola would grant the writ. Justice Aryal would dismiss it, because the petitioner had skipped an appeal. A Full Bench then found two conflicting Full Bench decisions. In Parshuram Jha (2048), a writ was granted although the petitioner could have sought leave to appeal. In Ram Rikshpal (2048), a writ was dismissed because the petitioner had not appealed. The Full Bench referred the case to a Larger Full Bench.

    **Issues**

    1. Which of the two conflicting decisions should be followed? In other words, when does an earlier decision bind as a precedent?
    2. Should the writ be dismissed because the Income Tax Act gave effective alternative remedies?

    **Decision**

    The majority, in the Chief Justice's order, dismissed the writ. Parshuram Jha did not lay down a general rule. Earlier cases had refused to treat leave to appeal as a certain remedy, so it was unfair to blame Parshuram Jha for coming by writ. No such history existed for an ordinary appeal. The decisions that had granted writs despite a right of appeal gave no reasons, so they were not authoritative precedents.

    On the remedies, the Income Tax Act gave two certain and effective options, and the petitioner had to use one. A deposit or extra fee did not make them ineffective. The Industrial Enterprises Act did not shut out the tax law, and section 42 of the Income Tax Act gave the tax officer jurisdiction. A decision within jurisdiction that affects property does not by itself violate a fundamental right.

    Justice Rana, joined by Justice Barma, dissented. He said a precedent is like law. The petitioner had followed the precedents of his day, so only later petitions should be dismissed. He also held the assessment void, because it taxed an exempt industry.

    **Ratio decidendi**

    1. Not every decision interprets the law or lays down a principle. Decisions without such interpretation or discussion are not precedents and bind only the parties (paragraph 12).
    2. An interpretation or principle becomes a precedent for similar cases only where the case is decided on its basis. To find the precedent, look at the discussion of law and principle (paragraph 12).
    3. Writ relief given in a few cases despite a right of appeal does not, by that fact alone, make those decisions precedents (paragraph 12).
    4. A deposit or extra fee does not make a legal remedy inadequate or ineffective. Otherwise every taxpayer could bypass sections 34 and 57 (paragraph 14).
    5. A decision within jurisdiction is valid until set aside, and the party must first use the legal remedy. But the Court can and must help at once where the decision is void. That means one made without jurisdiction, under an unconstitutional law, by a body without power, or without a hearing (paragraph 16).

    Points 1 to 3 state the law of precedent. Points 4 and 5 are the ratio on alternative remedies.

    **Comment**

    In my view the majority's test is sound. It matches the common-law idea that only the ratio decidendi binds. A result reached without discussion is decided sub silentio. The Court held that such a decision is not a precedent and binds only its parties. The Court also showed how to deal with conflicting precedents without overruling either. It distinguished Parshuram Jha on its special facts.

    The dissent raises a fair point. According to the dissent, the writ was registered on 2047/6/2, when such writs were being heard. It was dismissed in 2052, more than four years later. The judgment does not say whether the petitioner could still use the tax remedies after so long. Justice Rana would have applied the stricter rule only to later petitions. That is close to prospective overruling, which protects people who relied on the law as it stood.

    Still, the majority protects the system the legislature built. If every tax dispute could go straight to the Supreme Court, the Revenue Tribunal would lose its role.

    **Significance and conclusion**

    The case is prescribed in Unit 9.3, relevant to Unit 2.1.5 on stare decisis and precedent. It teaches that a precedent is the reasoned principle, not the bare result. Article 128(4) of the 2015 Constitution now makes such principles binding on all. Article 133(2) keeps the rule that a writ for a legal right lies only where other remedies are absent, inadequate or ineffective. The Income Tax Act 2058 now normally sends tax disputes first to administrative review and then to the Revenue Tribunal (ss 115-116). The 2031 Act let the taxpayer choose either.

    In conclusion, *Pradip Kumar Agrawal* holds that a court must look for reasoning, not results, when it decides what binds. And a writ is no substitute for an effective appeal, unless the decision challenged is void.
