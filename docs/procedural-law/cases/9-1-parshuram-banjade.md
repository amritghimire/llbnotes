## Facts

Translated and summarised by us from the Nepali judgment.

**The arrest.** Yagyamurti Banjade was arrested in Kathmandu on the evening of 2027/1/18. The petition says men in plain clothes seized him in New Road at about 5 pm. He was not told why, on what charge or under what power. The police say they arrested him at 6:30 pm, on a charge under the Associations and Institutions (Control) Act 2019 (संघ संस्था (नियन्त्रण गर्ने) ऐन, २०१९). The petition adds that many students had been arrested on the same charge before him.

**Custody, then a different Act.** On 2027/1/20 the police produced him in court. They held him for investigation with permission from the Bagmati Zonal Court (बागमती अञ्चल अदालत). They say this was in time, as 2027/1/19 was a Saturday. The police then decided the evidence was not enough to prosecute him under the Associations Act. Instead, they charged him under the National Guidance Act 2018 (राष्ट्रिय निर्देशन ऐन, २०१८). The offence, they said, was opening the Nepal Students' Union (नेपाल विद्यार्थी संघ) without the prior approval of His Majesty's Government (श्री ५ को सरकार). The police said the evidence was too weak under the Associations Act. The petition said the government switched Acts because it could not prove the first charge. The court found nothing on the record showing what evidence supported the new charge. It asked "with what thought" (के विचारले) the police did so (paragraph 15).

**The officer who tried him.** The Nepal Gazette (नेपाल राजपत्र) is the government's official journal for laws and notices. By a notice in it dated 2027/1/22, the government delegated (प्रत्यायोजन), that is, handed over, all its powers under section 6 of the National Guidance Act. They went to Durgadas Shrestha, chairman of the Bagmati Special Court (बागमती विशेष अदालत). Section 5(a) of the Act allowed this. The power let him fine a person up to Rs 500, jail him for up to six months, or both. The petition said the notice was really printed a week after its date. The Ministry of Home and Panchayat denied this.

**The hearing.** The petition says Durgadas Shrestha had the police bring Yagyamurti and others to a room near Putali Bagaincha in Singha Durbar, and questioned them there. When they would not give statements, he forced them by threatening them with the police constables present. Yagyamurti was not allowed a lawyer at any stage: the police and Durgadas Shrestha kept saying a lawyer had no role in this case. At 5 pm on 2027/2/9 Durgadas Shrestha ended the proceeding by holding Yagyamurti liable to punishment. Yagyamurti was not told the sentence at the time. Durgadas Shrestha denied any threat. He said Yagyamurti had signed a paper saying he had heard the order, and that no one had asked for a lawyer.

**The sentence.** The order of 2027/2/9 gave Yagyamurti one month and 15 days in jail and a fine of Rs 15. On 2027/2/12 the record (लगत) went to the Kathmandu Shresta Adalat Tahasil (काठमाडौं श्रेस्ता अदालत तहसिल). This office fixes jail terms and collects fines under the decisions of courts and authorised offices in Kathmandu. It turned the fine into 15 more days in jail, at Rs 30 a month under No. 53 of the Punishment chapter (दण्डसजायको ५३ नं.). That made two months in all. He was held in the Central Jail, Kathmandu. His "prisoner slip" (कैदी पुर्जी) of 2027/2/16 said the jail and fine came from an order of the Bagmati Special Court.

**The court or its chairman?** A written reply from member (सदस्य) Sher Bahadur Thapa said the Bagmati Special Court had taken no action against the petitioner. The jail and fine came from an order of its chairman, Durgadas Shrestha. The Special Court had no connection with them. The judgment does not say what cases the Bagmati Special Court normally heard, or under what law it was set up.

**The petition.** Yagyamurti's elder brother, Parshuram Banjade, filed a writ petition in the Supreme Court. A writ is a special order of a higher court that protects a right or stops an unlawful act. He asked for habeas corpus (बन्दी–प्रत्यक्षीकरण), which orders a detained person to be brought before the court and freed if the detention is unlawful. He said the jailer had not let Yagyamurti file the petition himself; the jailer denied this. The petition argued:

- No order, slip or gazette said under what law opening the Nepal Students' Union was a crime. Nor did any say where the Bagmati Special Court got power to try it.
- Under Adalati Bandobasta No. 35 of the Muluki Ain, a decision in a case the body had no power to hear is void.
- The detention slip (थुनुवा पुर्जी) is the written order for holding an arrested person. Under Adalati Bandobasta No. 121, it must name the law, the section and the charge. His did not.
- He had not been told why he was arrested, and was produced in court after more than 24 hours. He was forced to make a statement and denied a lawyer. All this broke his fundamental rights under Article 11 of the Constitution.
- As the popular saying goes, "where there is a right, there is also a remedy". In Latin this is ubi jus ibi remedium; the judgment gives it only in Nepali.

It asked for habeas corpus, or any other suitable order, under Article 71 of the Constitution. It also asked for his release on bail (धनजमानी) until the case was decided.

**Five other detainees.** Five more detainees were heard with Yagyamurti: Durga Acharya, Pradip Koirala, Machhindranath Pathak, Tika Prasad Pokharel and Rajeshwar Acharya. Their petitions were filed on different dates. But they faced the same kind of charge, asked for the same thing and raised the same questions. So the court decided all of them in one order (paragraph 12).

**In the Supreme Court.** On 2027/2/21 the Division Bench issued a show-cause order. It asked the respondents why the order sought should not be issued. They had to send written replies (लिखित जवाफ, which the syllabus calls a rejoinder) by noon on 2027/2/25. Replies came from the police, Durgadas Shrestha, Sher Bahadur Thapa, the Tahasil, the Home Ministry and the jailer. The bench later ordered the detainees to be brought to court on each hearing day. It heard the case on five days between 2027/2/27 and 2027/3/7.

## Issues

The court framed three questions (paragraph 11):

1. Did the case of opening a student organisation fall within the jurisdiction (अधिकारक्षेत्र) of Durgadas Shrestha, the officer appointed under the National Guidance Act 2018? Jurisdiction is the power, given by law, to hear and decide a matter.
2. If not, can habeas corpus be issued?
3. Does section 6(1) of the National Guidance Act conflict with Article 11(2)(c) of the Constitution? The petition relied on that clause for the freedom to open a students' union.

While answering the first question, the court also asked whether Durgadas Shrestha had followed the procedure the law requires (paragraph 16). It decided the first two questions and left the third open (paragraph 33).

## Arguments

**For the petitioners** (Advocates Kusum Shrestha, Ram Raja Prasad Singh and Ganesh Raj Sharma; Ganesh Raj Sharma appeared for Yagyamurti Banjade):

- Student organisations are outside the National Guidance Act, so Durgadas Shrestha had no power to punish them. He held his bench in the middle of the night and jailed them as soon as the case reached him. This was unauthorised and in bad faith (paragraphs 13 and 14).
- They were arrested under one Act and then prosecuted under another (paragraph 15).
- Words removed from an Act are out of it. Maxwell's book on interpreting statutes gives the example of a tax on carriage horses, race horses and other horses. Once "race horses" was removed, race horses fell outside the tax (paragraph 18).
- Under Article 71 the Supreme Court may issue any suitable writ or order, not only the one asked for (paragraph 26).
- A preamble (प्रस्तावना), the opening statement of an Act's purpose, is not part of the Act and cannot be amended. An amending Act that limits fundamental rights must itself state the Article 17(2) purposes (paragraphs 31 to 33).

**For the respondents** (Government Advocates Ratanlal Kanaudia and Prachanda Raj Anil, the government's lawyers):

- The written replies said the arrest and the detention slip were lawful and no one was threatened. An authorised officer had punished Yagyamurti under a lawful delegation (paragraphs 3 to 8).
- The case was put to Durgadas Shrestha, the evidence was enough and he had the power, so he decided (paragraph 14).
- His bench is a "court" under section 2(b) of the Government Cases Act 2017 and section 2 of the Jail Act 2019 (paragraph 16).
- "Organisation" (सङ्गठन) and "association" (संघ संस्था) mean the same. The definition of "class and professional organisation" (वर्गीय तथा व्यावसायिक सङ्गठन) ends with "other such class and professional organisations", which catches student organisations. And section 3(8), added in 2024, now covers students' associations (paragraphs 17 to 19).
- The writ of habeas corpus does not lie against detention under a lawful order of an authorised officer. That order must first be quashed by certiorari (paragraphs 26, 27 and 29). An order on the file is enough (paragraph 30).
- An amending Act need not repeat the words of Article 17(2) (paragraph 33).

## Decision

The Division Bench issued habeas corpus. Justice Ranganath Upreti agreed with Justice Khatri's opinion.

1. **The officer was quasi-judicial, so he had to act judicially** (paragraph 16). Durgadas Shrestha was not a court "in the strict sense". But anyone with power to fine and jail makes a judicial or quasi-judicial (अर्धन्यायिक) decision. A quasi-judicial decision is made by someone who is not a judge but must decide like one. The court followed the Full Bench in Mukti Sharma v S.P. Tek Bahadur Raymajhi (NKP 2017, p. 101). A judge's decision is judicial. An administrative officer's decision made "with a judicial mind" is quasi-judicial. An officer's decision about administration, with more discretion (तजबिज), is administrative.
2. **The delegation was valid, but the case was outside his power** (paragraphs 17 to 19). Section 5(a) allowed the delegation. But the National Guidance (Second Amendment) Act 2024 had removed "student organisation" from sections 2(a) and 3(5). By removing words, the legislature shows that it means to put them outside the Act. Different words also carry different meanings, so "association" is not "organisation". The ejusdem generis rule says general words cover only things like the specific words before them. It could have helped only if "student organisation" had never been in the definition. To read it back in would be "to put one's own meaning into the words of the Act".
3. **What the law does not forbid may be done** (paragraph 20). It was argued that what the law forbids directly cannot be done indirectly; the judgment does not say by whom. The court agreed that what is forbidden cannot be done in any way. But a democracy has more freedom than a dictatorship, and the Panchayat democracy (पञ्चायती प्रजातान्त्रिक व्यवस्था) of the time gave full freedom. So what the law does not forbid may be done.
4. **His procedure was unfair** (paragraphs 21 to 25). "Justice is not done merely because one has power." Anyone who decides must hear every party and weigh the merits first. Here the petitioners were brought before Durgadas Shrestha after office hours, and their statements were not recorded in the form courts use. No lawyer could have been found at that hour. "And why is work done in such a hurry in the middle of the night?" Jailing them without waiting for a lawyer hinted at collusion with the police. The right to a lawyer of one's choice is a fundamental right under Article 11(6). So it is not just to say that they did not ask for one. Nor can the government be a judge in its own cause, which the court called a basic principle of natural justice. And under Article 11(1), liberty may be taken away only by following the law to the letter. That means both substantive law (what is an offence) and procedural law (how a case is run).
5. **Habeas corpus lies, and certiorari need not come first** (paragraphs 26 to 30). "Where there is unlawful detention, the order of habeas corpus becomes active." Once the grounds are shown, the court must issue it; it has no discretion. Certiorari wipes out an unlawful decision, while habeas corpus frees an unlawfully held person, so neither replaces the other. A person held under a lawful order of an authorised officer cannot be freed this way. But "if an order has been given without jurisdiction, the detention is unlawful", even though the order is on the file. The court cited earlier cases, among them Bhola Kumar Sherchan v Chitwan District Court (Full Bench, 2026/6/10). There a man jailed under a judgment for stealing a rhino horn was freed, because he was not the person convicted.
6. **The third question was left open** (paragraphs 31 to 33). A preamble is part of an Act and can be amended. But the court did not need to decide whether section 6(1) conflicts with Article 11(2)(c). The first two questions already showed unlawful detention.
7. **Order** (paragraph 34). Durgadas Shrestha had jailed and fined the petitioner without jurisdiction. The court issued habeas corpus and ordered the petitioner released. A copy went to the respondents through the Office of the Attorney General (महान्यायाधिवक्ताको कार्यालय).

The order rests on the lack of jurisdiction; the findings on procedure support it. The court did not rule on the earlier police steps, because habeas corpus looks at the detention as it stood when the replies were filed (paragraph 15). Nor did it rule on the detention slip or the 24-hour limit. The judgment does not say when each detainee was actually released.

## Ratio decidendi

Our translation of the official headnote, point by point:

1. Several petitions may be decided by one order or one judgment, even if they were registered on different dates. This applies where all the petitioners were proceeded against on the same kind of charge and ask for the same thing. The questions to be resolved must be the same, and the petitions heard on the same dates. (प्रकरण नं. १२)
2. A judge's decision is a judicial decision. An administrative officer's judicial decision is a quasi-judicial decision. An administrative officer's decision about administration, where there is more discretion, is an administrative decision. A quasi-judicial decision, too, must be made with a judicial approach. (प्रकरण नं. १६)
3. Why does an Act give definitions? To stop some words being repeated again and again, and to explain words that could have two meanings. Also to make a word include or exclude something. Definitions are given to explain the meaning of words. (प्रकरण नं. १७)
4. If an amendment removes a word from an Act, the removed word falls outside the scope of that Act. (प्रकरण नं. १८)
5. A democratic system has more freedom than a dictatorship. So, under Panchayat democracy, what the law does not forbid may be done. (प्रकरण नं. २०)
6. Article 11(6) of the Constitution of Nepal: the officer should have said "you may engage a lawyer". It is not just to say instead that "they did not ask to engage a lawyer". (प्रकरण नं. २२)
7. A basic principle of natural justice: a person cannot be a judge in his own cause. In the same way, the government cannot be a judge in its own cause. (प्रकरण नं. २४)
8. Article 11(1) of the Constitution of Nepal: anyone's life or personal liberty may be taken away only by following the law to the letter. Both the substantive Act and the procedural Act must be followed. The matter must be examined with great care and caution. (प्रकरण नं. २५)
9. Article 71 of the Constitution of Nepal: where one kind of writ is sought, the Supreme Court may issue another writ or order if it seems appropriate. (प्रकरण नं. २६)
10. Article 71 of the Constitution of Nepal: certiorari issues against a judicial or quasi-judicial officer, and mandamus against an administrative officer. The writ of habeas corpus issues against anyone who holds a person in unauthorised and unlawful detention. In the other writs, especially mandamus, whether to issue an order is for the court's discretion. With habeas corpus it is not: once the proper grounds are established, the court must issue it, as if it were a right. (प्रकरण नं. २८, २९)
11. A preamble is an integral part of an Act. It does not control the main sections. If a main section is unclear or can carry two meanings, the preamble may be used for help. It is read to understand what the legislature meant. A preamble can be amended. (प्रकरण नं. ३२)
12. In a petition for habeas corpus, the court may find the detention unlawful after examining only some of the questions raised. It may then issue the order without examining the rest. (प्रकरण नं. ३३)

**In short (our summary):** an officer with power to fine and jail decides quasi-judicially. So he must act like a judge: stay within his powers, hear the accused and let him have a lawyer. Words removed from an Act by amendment are outside it, so the officer had no jurisdiction over a student organisation. And, in words from paragraph 30 that the headnote leaves out, a detention under an order made without jurisdiction is unlawful, so habeas corpus must issue.

## Why it matters

- **The leading case on quasi-judicial bodies** (Unit 3.7). An officer who is not a judge, but can fine or jail people, makes quasi-judicial decisions. He must decide with a "judicial mind": stay within his powers, hear the accused, weigh the evidence and allow a lawyer.
- **Both rules of natural justice** (Unit 2.1.1). The court said a decision-maker must hear every party before deciding, and that no one may be a judge in his own cause. These are the rules audi alteram partem and nemo judex in causa sua, though the judgment does not use the Latin names. It applied the second rule to the government itself. In a political offence the crime is against the government, so an impartial judge is needed.
- **The right to a lawyer is for the officer to respect, not for the accused to demand.** It was not enough to say that the accused "did not ask". A hurried hearing at night, with no time to find a lawyer, broke Article 11(6).
- **Jurisdiction comes from the law, not from a title** (Unit 3.6). Durgadas Shrestha chaired a Special Court, but here he acted as the officer named in the gazette notice, not as that court. His delegated power covered only offences under the National Guidance Act. Once the legislature removed "student organisation" from that Act, the case was outside his power.
- **An order made without jurisdiction cannot keep anyone in jail.** The writ of habeas corpus lies even when a judgment or order is on the file, if it was made without jurisdiction. The detainee need not first get the order quashed by certiorari. And unlike mandamus, the writ is not a matter of discretion: once unlawful detention is shown, the court must issue it.
- **The present Constitution keeps these rights and adds to them.** Article 20 keeps the right to know the grounds of arrest and to have a lawyer. It adds the right to a lawyer from the time of arrest. It also adds a fair hearing by an independent, impartial and competent court or judicial body. See the table below.

## Full 20-mark answer

??? example "Exam answer (20 marks)"
    **2079 Q5.** State and explain the facts and ratio of the case Parshuram Banjade Vs. Durgadas Shrestha (NKP 2027, No. 1, Page 157).

    **Introduction**

    In Latin, habeas corpus (बन्दीप्रत्यक्षीकरण) means "you shall have the body". It is a writ by which a court has a detained person brought before it, and frees him if the detention is unlawful. In this case the Supreme Court called it a powerful remedy for protecting personal liberty. A quasi-judicial (अर्धन्यायिक) authority is an officer who is not a judge but has power to decide rights or to punish, like a court.

    In *Parshuram Banjade v Durgadas Shrestha*, the Supreme Court joined these two ideas. It held that an officer who tries and punishes people is quasi-judicial, and must follow natural justice. If he acts without jurisdiction, the detention is unlawful and habeas corpus must issue.

    **Citation**

    Parshuram Banjade (for Yagyamurti Banjade) v Durgadas Shrestha, Bagmati Special Court, et al., NKP 2027, No. 1, p. 157, D.N. 547 (Writ No. 864 of 2027). Supreme Court, Division Bench: Justices Nayan Bahadur Khatri and Ranganath Upreti. Decided 2027/3/16.

    **Facts**

    1. Yagyamurti Banjade was arrested in Kathmandu on 2027/1/18, on a charge under the Associations and Institutions (Control) Act 2019. The police then found the evidence too weak to prosecute him under that Act.
    2. Instead, they charged him with opening the Nepal Students' Union without the government's prior approval. They said this was an offence under the National Guidance Act 2018.
    3. By a gazette notice of 2027/1/22, the government had delegated its powers under that Act to Durgadas Shrestha, chairman of the Bagmati Special Court. He could fine up to Rs 500, jail for up to six months, or both.
    4. He took statements after office hours. On 2027/2/9 he sentenced Yagyamurti to one month and 15 days in jail and a Rs 15 fine. The detainees said they were refused lawyers; he said no one asked for one.
    5. A member, Sher Bahadur Thapa, replied that the Special Court itself had taken no part.
    6. Yagyamurti's elder brother, Parshuram, filed a habeas corpus petition. Five other detainees' petitions were heard and decided with it.

    **Issues**

    1. Was a case about opening a student organisation within Durgadas Shrestha's jurisdiction under the National Guidance Act?
    2. If not, could habeas corpus be issued?
    3. Did section 6(1) of that Act conflict with Article 11(2)(c) of the Constitution, the freedom to form unions and associations?

    **Decision**

    The court issued habeas corpus and ordered the petitioner released. It held that:

    - Durgadas Shrestha was not a court, but his power to fine and jail made his decision quasi-judicial. So he had to act judicially.
    - The 2024 amendment had removed "student organisation" from the Act. Words removed by amendment are outside the Act, so the case was outside his jurisdiction.
    - His hurried hearing at night, with no chance of a lawyer, was unfair and went against the right to counsel in Article 11(6).
    - An order made without jurisdiction makes the detention unlawful. Habeas corpus then lies, without first quashing the order by certiorari.

    The third issue was left undecided, because the first two were enough.

    **Ratio decidendi**

    The ratio can be explained in six points.

    1. *Quasi-judicial decisions need a judicial approach.* A judge's decision is judicial. An administrative officer's decision made with a judicial mind is quasi-judicial. An officer's decision about administration, with more discretion, is administrative. The name of the office does not decide this; the power does. Anyone who can fine or jail must act like a judge (paragraph 16).
    2. *Words removed by amendment are outside the Act.* By removing them, the legislature shows its intention. A court that reads them back in puts its own meaning into the Act (paragraph 18).
    3. *Natural justice binds every decision-maker.* Before deciding, he must hear every party and weigh the merits. No one may be a judge in his own cause, and that includes the government, above all in a political offence (paragraphs 21 and 24).
    4. *The right to a lawyer must be offered, not merely allowed on request.* The officer should have said "you may engage a lawyer". It is not just to say instead that "they did not ask" (paragraph 22).
    5. *Liberty can be taken only by following the law to the letter.* Under Article 11(1), both the substantive and the procedural law must be followed with great care (paragraph 25).
    6. *Habeas corpus is not discretionary.* It lies against anyone who holds a person unlawfully, judicial or administrative. Detention under an order made without jurisdiction is unlawful even though the order is on the file. The court may also issue a different writ from the one asked for (paragraphs 26 to 30).

    **Comment**

    In my view the judgment is strong for its time. Under the Panchayat system, the Supreme Court held that students jailed by an officer acting under a government notice were unlawfully detained. It did not let the label "authorised officer" hide a lack of jurisdiction. It also gave natural justice real content: a hearing at night, with no time to find a lawyer, is not a fair hearing. Its line that "justice is not done merely because one has power" is still worth quoting.

    There are weaknesses too. The order itself rests on the lack of jurisdiction (paragraph 34); the findings on procedure only support it. The court did not decide whether section 6(1) itself offended the freedom of association, so the larger question stayed open. Its remarks on preambles were not needed for the result, so they are obiter dicta, though the headnote lists them. And it chose not to examine the police's switch from one Act to another, because habeas corpus looks only at the present detention.

    **Significance and conclusion**

    The case is prescribed in Unit 9.1 and is relevant to Units 2.1.1 (natural justice), 3.6 (special courts) and 3.7 (quasi-judicial bodies). Today the Constitution of Nepal 2015 keeps and adds to its rules. Article 20 keeps the right to know the grounds of arrest and to have a lawyer. It adds the right to a lawyer from the time of arrest. It also adds a fair hearing by an independent, impartial and competent court or judicial body. Article 133(3) keeps habeas corpus. Article 152(2) keeps offences punishable with more than a year in prison for courts and judicial bodies. That would not cover a six-month offence like the one in this case.

    In short, power must come from the law and must be used fairly. A detention that rests on an order made without jurisdiction is unlawful, and habeas corpus will end it.
