## Facts

Translated and summarised by us from the Nepali judgment.

**The incident.** Tripura Devi Jha lived in ward 9 of Paudeshwar Village Development Committee, a local body in Dhanusha district. The defendants, Kali Devi Jha and others, lived in the same place. In her plaint she said that on 2049/9/9, at about 10:30, she was cooking rice in her courtyard. Her husband was away. The defendants suddenly came in, shouting abuse, and forced their way into her house. One of them, Bhalchandra Jha, said: "This is the chance. Beat her hard; I will bear whatever happens." The others, and he too, kicked, slapped and punched her and her two-year-old daughter, Pratibha Kumari, whom she held in her lap. While she lay unconscious, they carried off goods from her house.

**The claim.** On 2049/12/9 she filed a plaint (फिराद) in the Dhanusha District Court. She listed the goods. They were Mansuli rice (Rs 480), wheat (Rs 425), two steel plates (Rs 200), two lotas, or metal water pots (Rs 200), and a bucket (Rs 100). She asked the court to punish the defendants and make them pay her the value of the goods, Rs 1,405. The value of the property in a claim is called the बिगो. She brought the case herself, as plaintiff, though it was a criminal case: the Supreme Court numbered her later appeal as a criminal appeal (फौ.पु.नं.).

**The defence.** The defendants filed a joint written reply (प्रतिउत्तर). They denied the robbery and said she had sued them out of a household grudge (घरायसी रिसइवी). The Supreme Court later noted that some defendants also said they were not there that day. One defendant, Rita Devi Jha, was served a summons (समाह्वान) on 2050/2/12. A summons is a court notice telling a defendant to answer the case within a time limit (myad). She filed no reply and let the time run out.

**District Court.** On 2051/3/7 the Dhanusha District Court held that the defendants had robbed the goods, as she claimed.

**Appellate Court.** The defendants appealed to the Appellate Court, Janakpur. They said the witnesses had not clearly stated that they saw a robbery. When appealing, on 2051/11/5, the five defendants later named in the Supreme Court paid Rs 195 of the fine at the Appellate Court. On 2052/3/1 the Appellate Court ordered Tripura Devi Jha, the respondent in that appeal, to be called under Adalati Bandobasta No. 202. That was the rule under which an appeal court called the other side to the hearing. Its reason: the District Court had found robbery without proof, so its judgment might change. On 2053/2/1 the Appellate Court reversed the District Court. It held that she had not proved her claim, as section 25 of the Evidence Act 2031 requires. It acquitted all the defendants, that is, found them not guilty.

**The assault case.** The same parties also had an assault case (कुटपिट मुद्दा) about an incident on the same date, Criminal Appeal No. 661. In it, the Appellate Court, Janakpur held on 2052/3/1 that these defendants had beaten Tripura Devi Jha. That judgment became final.

**In the Supreme Court.** Tripura Devi Jha appealed to the Supreme Court under section 9(1) of the Administration of Justice Act 2048. She made only five of the defendants respondents: Kali Devi Jha, Saroj Devi Jha, Sindula Devi Jha, Bhalchandra Jha and Tapeshwar Jha. The schedule at the end of the judgment spells Sindula as Sindhula.

A Joint Bench ordered the five to be called under No. 202. Its reason was about the time limit for appeals. The Summary Procedure Act 2028 sets a shorter, faster procedure for small cases, called summary procedure (संक्षिप्त कार्यविधि). The ordinary procedure under the general law is called the general procedure (सामान्य कार्यविधि). The Joint Bench thought the Appellate Court's judgment might be wrong on the time limits (हदम्याद). These are in sections 11(क) and 8 of the Summary Procedure Act. The judgment does not date this order. Nor does it say why the Summary Procedure Act applied to this case, or when the defendants learned of the District Court's judgment.

## Issues

The judgment states the question in paragraph 11: was the Appellate Court right to reverse the District Court and hold that robbery was not proved? In paragraph 13 the Court dealt with three points:

1. Whom could the appeal reach, when Tripura Devi Jha had named only five of the defendants as respondents?
2. Should the Appellate Court's proceedings be set aside because it used the general procedure, and not the time limits of the Summary Procedure Act 2028?
3. Did the evidence prove that the five committed robbery?

The second point matters for Unit 1.3. What happens when a case meant for the summary procedure is handled under the general procedure?

## Arguments

The judgment names no counsel for either side. The lines for counsel, and for any precedent relied on, read "X". It does not record what was argued at the hearing. The points below come from the plaint, reply and appeals that the judgment summarises.

**For Tripura Devi Jha, the appellant** (her appeal to the Supreme Court, paragraph 9):

- The defendants had registered their appeal in the Appellate Court after the time limit had passed.[^late]
- The defendant himself (प्रतिवादी स्वयंले) had admitted the incident in writing. We read this as one defendant, since the next words say "his witnesses" (निजको साक्षीहरु), but the singular may stand for all of them. Yet the Appellate Court relied on the defendants' witnesses, who falsely said no incident took place. The judgment does not say who made the admission, or in what document.
- So the Appellate Court's judgment was not based on objective facts. It was subjective and against the law.
- It did not even quote the argument made by her agent (वारेस), a person she had authorised to act for her in court. And it set the evidence aside.
- She asked the Court to set aside the Appellate Court's judgment and uphold the District Court's.

**For Kali Devi Jha and the other defendants, the respondents:**

- In their joint reply in the District Court, they denied the robbery and blamed a household grudge (paragraph 3). Some also said they were not there that day (paragraph 13).
- In their appeal to the Appellate Court, they said the witnesses had not clearly stated that they saw a robbery (paragraph 6).
- The judgment records no reply or argument from the respondents in the Supreme Court.

[^late]: The text reads "हदम्याद नधाई". We read नधाई as a slip for नघाई ("having passed"). This fits the Joint Bench's order and paragraph 13, which both deal with the time limit.

## Decision

The Joint Bench allowed Tripura Devi Jha's appeal. Justice Lamsal agreed with Justice Sharma's judgment. All the reasoning is in paragraph 13. The orders are in the schedule (तपसिल) at the end of the judgment.

1. **The appeal reached only the five respondents** (paragraph 13). Tripura Devi Jha had not made all the defendants respondents. So the Court decided the appeal only for the five she named. It says nothing more about the others.
2. **The time-limit point failed** (paragraph 13). Section 14 of the Summary Procedure Act 2028 is headed "Saving and repeal" (बचाउ र खारेजी). "Saving" here means keeping something valid. The section says that using the general procedure in a case meant for this Act's procedure does not, by itself, make the proceedings void. The Appellate Court had used the general procedure to register and decide the appeal. So there was no ground to set its proceedings aside on the time-limit point. The Court did not decide whether the defendants' appeal was in fact late.
3. **Robbery was proved** (paragraph 13). Her witnesses, Birendra Mitra and Maharudra Jha, said they saw the defendants enter her courtyard at about 10:30 on 2049/9/9. They saw them beat her and carry off paddy, rice, a lota and a bucket. They named the goods and the defendants. The defendants' witnesses backed neither the grudge defence nor the claim that some were elsewhere. The final judgment in the assault case also showed that the incident happened that day. So the Appellate Court should have upheld the finding of robbery. Its judgment was reversed, and the five were held to have committed robbery.
4. **Fine** (schedule). Under No. 6 of the Robbery chapter of the Muluki Ain (लुटपिटको ६ नं.), the fine was 25 per cent of the Rs 1,405 value. The judgment puts it at Rs 351.30.[^fine] The five had paid Rs 195 when appealing. The other Rs 156.30 was split equally, Rs 31.26 each, among Bhalchandra Jha, Sindhula Devi Jha, Kali Devi Jha, Tapeshwar Jha and Saroj Devi Jha. The District Court was told to record it for recovery.
5. **Recovering the value** (schedule). Tripura Devi Jha could apply within the time the law allows, pointing out the defendants' property. The District Court was then to recover the Rs 1,405 for her (bigo bharaune), taking a 10 per cent fee. The file was to be handed over under the rules.

The judgment does not say whether the money was later recovered.

[^fine]: 25 per cent of Rs 1,405 is Rs 351.25. The judgment writes Rs 351.30, and its later figures (Rs 156.30, and Rs 31.26 each) follow from that.

## Ratio decidendi

Our translation of the official headnote, point by point:

1. This Court's Joint Bench ordered the respondents to be called under Adalati Bandobasta No. 202, on a ground about the time limit. As to that ground, the law is in section 14 of the Summary Procedure Act 2028, headed "Saving and repeal". It provides: "In a case that must follow the procedure under this Act, the general procedure under other prevailing law may have been followed instead. If so, the proceedings already taken shall not be void for that reason alone." The Appellate Court followed the general procedure, registered the appeal and gave its judgment. So those proceedings need not be set aside on that ground. (प्रकरण नं. १३)
2. The plaintiff's witnesses gave specific statements supporting her claim. They said they saw the defendants enter her courtyard at about 10:30 on 2049/9/9. They saw them beat her and carry off paddy, rice, a lota and a bucket, among other things. They named the goods taken and the defendants. The defendants' witnesses did not support the defences. Some defendants had said the case came from a grudge, and some said they were not there that day. The same plaintiff and defendants had an assault case, Criminal Appeal No. 661, about an incident on the same date. In it, the Appellate Court, Janakpur held on 2052/3/1 that these defendants had beaten the plaintiff, and that judgment became final. That case and the witnesses' statements together confirm that the incident happened on 2049/9/9, as she said. With specific statements from her witnesses, and the incident established by the assault case, robbery should have been held proved. The Appellate Court reversed the District Court and held there was no robbery. That judgment is wrong and is reversed. As the plaintiff's appeal asks, the five defendants named are held to have committed robbery. (प्रकरण नं. १३)

**In short (our summary):** using the general procedure in a case meant for the summary procedure does not, by itself, make what was done void. On the facts, specific eyewitness statements, backed by a final judgment about the same incident, proved the robbery.

## Why it matters

- **Using the wrong procedure does not, by itself, undo a case.** Take a case meant for the summary procedure that was handled under the general procedure. Under s 14 of the Summary Procedure Act 2028, what was done is not void for that reason alone. The Court used it to refuse to set aside an appeal that the Appellate Court had registered and decided under the general procedure. This is the point for Unit 1.3.
- **Three kinds of procedure.** The general procedure is the ordinary one, now in the Muluki Civil and Criminal Procedure Codes 2074. The summary procedure is a shorter one; the Act's preamble says it exists so that small cases are decided quickly. A special procedure (विशेष कार्यविधि) is one that another Act sets for its own cases. The Summary Procedure Act does not affect it (s 3(2)).
- **Which cases go by the summary procedure today.** Under s 3(1), the Act's procedure applies to the trial and the appeal of the cases in Schedule 1. It also applies to civil cases worth up to Rs 1,000. And it applies to criminal cases punishable by a fine up to Rs 1,000, prison up to six months, or both. Cases in Schedule 2, such as theft, are left out. The judgment does not say why this case came under the Act.
- **The Act also moves cases the other way.** If a case turns out not to be a summary case, the court records why and goes on under the general procedure (s 12(1)). If only part of a case is a summary case, the whole case goes by the general procedure (s 12(2)).
- **The summary procedure runs on short time limits.** Today a defendant gets seven days, plus travel time, to answer a summons (s 8(2)). The court must decide the case within 90 days, counted from the defendant's reply or the end of the time for it (s 10(1)). Normally a lapsed time limit can be restored only for a cause beyond the party's control, for 15 days in all (s 8(1), proviso (क)). The restoration and substitution rules of the two 2074 Procedure Codes also apply (s 8(1), proviso (ख)). An appeal must be filed within 30 days of learning of the judgment (s 11क). It must be decided within 90 days of the file reaching the appeal court (s 11ख).
- **An appeal reaches only those made respondents.** The Court decided the appeal only for the five defendants Tripura Devi Jha named.
- **A final judgment in a linked case can help prove the facts.** The assault judgment about the same incident backed up the eyewitnesses.

## Full 20-mark answer

??? example "Exam answer (20 marks)"
    **Question** (not in the 2077, 2079 or 2080 papers we have). State the facts and examine the ratio decidendi of Tripura Devi Jha v Kali Devi Jha.

    **Introduction**

    Procedure is the set of steps a case goes through in court. Nepali law has three kinds. The general procedure is the ordinary one, now found in the Muluki Civil and Criminal Procedure Codes 2074. The summary procedure is a shorter set of steps for small cases, under the Summary Procedure Act 2028. Its preamble says it exists so that small cases are decided quickly. A special procedure is one that a particular Act sets for its own cases.

    Sometimes a court uses the general procedure in a case that should have gone by the summary procedure. Is what it did then void? Section 14 of the Summary Procedure Act answers this, and *Tripura Devi Jha v Kali Devi Jha* applies it.

    **Citation**

    Tripura Devi Jha v Kali Devi Jha et al., NKP 2058, No. 3, p. 211, D.N. 6996. Supreme Court, Joint Bench: Justices Hari Prasad Sharma and Bhairab Prasad Lamsal. Decided 2057/10/6.

    **Facts**

    Tripura Devi Jha said that on 2049/9/9 the defendants, who lived in the same place, forced their way into her house in Dhanusha. They beat her and her two-year-old daughter. While she lay unconscious, they carried off rice, wheat, plates, water pots and a bucket worth Rs 1,405. She filed a plaint for robbery (लुटपिट), asking for the value of the goods and for the defendants to be punished. The defendants denied it and said she had sued out of a household grudge.

    On 2051/3/7 the Dhanusha District Court held the robbery proved. The defendants appealed. On 2053/2/1 the Appellate Court, Janakpur reversed the District Court. It held that she had not proved her claim, as section 25 of the Evidence Act 2031 requires, and acquitted all the defendants.

    She appealed to the Supreme Court against five of the defendants. Among other things, she said their appeal to the Appellate Court had been filed out of time. A Joint Bench called the five under Adalati Bandobasta No. 202. It thought the Appellate Court's judgment might be wrong on the time limits in sections 11(क) and 8 of the Summary Procedure Act 2028.

    **Issues**

    1. Should the Appellate Court's proceedings be set aside because it used the general procedure, not the Summary Procedure Act's time limits?
    2. Did the evidence prove the robbery?
    3. Whom could the appeal reach, when only five defendants were made respondents?

    **Decision**

    The Joint Bench allowed the appeal. On the procedure point, it relied on section 14 of the Summary Procedure Act. Under it, if a summary case is handled under the general procedure, what was done is not void for that reason alone. The Appellate Court had used the general procedure to register and decide the appeal. So there was no ground to set its proceedings aside on the time-limit point.

    On the merits, two eyewitnesses, Birendra Mitra and Maharudra Jha, named the defendants and the goods taken. The defendants' witnesses did not support their defences. An assault case between the same parties, about an incident on the same date, had ended in a final judgment that the defendants beat her. So the robbery was proved.

    The Court reversed the Appellate Court, but only for the five respondents she had named. Under No. 6 of the Robbery chapter, it fined them 25 per cent of the value, less the Rs 195 they had paid. It ordered the Rs 1,405 to be recovered for her from their property if she applied in time.

    **Ratio decidendi**

    The official headnote gives two points, both from paragraph 13.

    1. A case may need the summary procedure but be handled under the general procedure. If so, the proceedings are not void for that reason alone (section 14). The Appellate Court had registered and decided the appeal under the general procedure. So there was no ground to set the appeal aside on the time-limit point.
    2. Where eyewitnesses give specific statements supporting the claim, and a final judgment in a linked case establishes the incident, robbery should be held proved.

    The first point is a rule of procedure. The case is prescribed in Unit 9.4, and this point makes it relevant to Unit 1.3. The second applies the law of evidence to these facts.

    **Comment**

    In my view, the ruling rightly puts substance before form. Procedure exists to serve justice. A party should not win or lose only because the court chose the wrong set of steps, if nothing unfair came of it. Here the Court looked past the technical point and decided on the evidence. That did not hurt the plaintiff, who still won on the merits.

    But the reasoning on the procedure point is thin. It is two sentences quoting section 14. The Court did not say why the case came under the Summary Procedure Act. Nor did it discuss when the defendants' appeal was filed. The schedule only notes the Rs 195 they paid "when appealing" on 2051/11/5. It did not say whether the appeal was late under either procedure. Section 14 stops proceedings being void because of the choice of procedure. It is less clear that it should save an appeal filed out of time. A time limit decides whether there is a right to appeal at all, and the judgment does not discuss this.

    On the evidence, the Court was right to use the assault judgment. The same Appellate Court had already held, in that case, that the incident happened. It was hard to then acquit the defendants of robbery for lack of proof.

    Finally, the Court limited its decision to the five respondents. An appeal reaches only those made parties to it, so the other defendants were not judged without a hearing.

    **Significance and conclusion**

    This case shows how the general and summary procedures fit together. The Summary Procedure Act sends a case to the general procedure when it turns out not to be a summary case (section 12). And under section 14, using the general procedure in a summary case does not by itself undo what was done. Section 14 states the same rule today; the current text shows no amendment to it. The appeal time in section 11क was amended in 2074 and 2075. The Appellate Courts are now High Courts. In criminal appeals, the respondent is now called under section 140 of the Muluki Criminal Procedure Code 2074.

    The rule to remember: a slip in choosing the procedure does not by itself undo a case. The court will look at the evidence, and here the evidence proved the robbery.
