## Facts

Translated and summarised by us from the Nepali judgment.

**The family.** Dil Bahadur was the head of the family (मूल पुरुष). He had two wives. The elder, Laxmi Kumari, had two sons: Bhakta Bahadur, the elder, and Shambhu Kumar. The younger wife, Man Kumari, had a son, Kumar Jang. The plaint (paragraph 2) says these six were coparceners (अंशियार).[^names]

**The land.** The plaint says the family's home was in Siddheshwar village in Sindhuli district.[^home] The family sold some land there. It then bought paddy land (धनहर खेत) at Hariwan in Sarlahi, in the name of the eldest son, Bhakta Bahadur. Later Bhakta Bahadur sold some of the land in his name. Asked why, he said he would take no share in the land left at Hariwan, so the family kept quiet.

**The two sales.** On 2042/6/20 Bhakta Bahadur sold plot No. 180 (कित्ता नं. १८०) at Hariwan, measuring 2-0-10, to Padam Bahadur Karki. On the same day Padam Bahadur sold the same plot to Jamuni Das and Ramchhabila Das. Each sale was made by a sale deed (राजिनामा), registered at the Land Revenue Office (मालपोत कार्यालय), Sarlahi. This step is called likhat pass. The first deed was registration No. 1058 (र.नं. १०५८) and the second No. 1063. The family's consent was not taken.

**How the family learned of it.** The plaint says Bhakta Bahadur's own wife and sons met the family on 2043/6/12. They said the land had been sold, and that they had filed their own suit to cancel its registration. The family took a copy (नक्कल) on 2043/6/13 and so learned of the sale. They filed their plaint in the Sarlahi District Court on 2043/7/9 (paragraphs 2 and 7).

**The claim.** The plaintiffs were the rest of the family. The judgment calls them Bhakta Bahadur's father, mother and brothers (paragraphs 18 and 20). They asked the court to cancel the registration for their share, five of six parts (६ खण्डको ५ खण्ड). They also asked for that share to be registered in their names.

**The defence.** Padam Bahadur Karki, Jamuni Das, Ramchhabila Das and others filed a joint written defence, a pratiuttar (paragraph 3). They said:

- Bhakta Bahadur had two mothers, so he could not live in the joint family. He came to the Terai and bought the Hariwan land from his own skill and earnings.
- He had filled in a "Form No. 7" (७ नं. फाराम) separately. Paragraph 9 calls it a ७ नं. फांटवारी. The judgment does not say what this form is.
- He had earlier sold plots 405 and 406, on 2039/1/17 and 2041/2/22. The plaintiffs did not challenge those sales, because they lived apart.
- So the plaintiffs were not his coparceners. The land was his private earnings (निजी आर्जन), and its registration should not be cancelled.

Bhakta Bahadur filed his own defence (paragraph 4). The land was his own earnings, so under No. 19 of the Partition chapter (अंशबण्डाको १९ नं.) of the Muluki Ain he needed nobody's consent.[^18]

**First round: the plaint is dismissed as late.** On 2044/7/16 the Sarlahi District Court dismissed the plaint without deciding the claim (paragraph 5). Bhakta Bahadur's wife and sons belonged to the same joint household (एकासगोल), a family living together with its property undivided. They had taken a copy on 2043/5/29. The court held that everyone in the joint household must be taken to have known from then. So the plaint came after the time allowed by No. 10 of the Lending and Transactions chapter (लेनदेन व्यवहारको १० नं.) of the Muluki Ain. In other words, it was time-barred.

The plaintiffs appealed to the then Janakpur Zonal Court (अञ्चल अदालत), which heard appeals from District Courts. On 2045/12/21 it set the dismissal aside (paragraph 7). The plaintiffs had filed on 2043/7/9, after learning of the sale from the copy they took on 2043/6/13. The defendants had produced no evidence that they knew earlier. So the plaint was in time. The Zonal Court sent the case back to the District Court to take the evidence and decide.

**Second round: the deeds are cancelled.** On 2052/4/16 the District Court decided for the plaintiffs (paragraph 8). The defendants had not proved that Bhakta Bahadur lived apart, or that the land was his private earnings. So it was joint family property, and the coparceners' consent was needed before selling it. The court cancelled five of six parts of the deeds and ordered those parts registered in the plaintiffs' names.

The defendants other than Bhakta Bahadur appealed to the Appellate Court, Janakpur (paragraph 9). They said Bhakta Bahadur had long lived at Hariwan in the Terai, had filled in a separate Form No. 7, and had lived apart since 2021. On 2052/11/8 the Appellate Court ordered the plaintiffs, as respondents, to be called to argue (paragraph 10). It thought the District Court's judgment might change, for two reasons:

- The plaintiffs were not heirs (हकदार) of Padam Bahadur, who made the second deed.
- Bhakta Bahadur's wife and sons had brought civil case No. 1096 (दे.नं. १०९६) about the same deed. A final judgment in that case had held that Bhakta Bahadur could dispose of the land at will (आफूखुस गर्न पाउने). That means he could sell or give it away without anyone's consent.

After the hearing, the Appellate Court upheld the District Court on 2054/2/20. The plaintiffs too had a right in the land (paragraph 11 and opening paragraph).

**In the Supreme Court.** The defendants' side asked the Supreme Court to revise the Appellate Court's judgment (paragraph 12). Revision (मुद्दा दोहोर्‍याई हेर्ने) means asking the Supreme Court to look again at a case already decided on appeal. The Court must first give leave (निस्सा). The petition said that under Adalati Bandobasta No. 72 one plaint could not cancel two deeds, and that the land was Bhakta Bahadur's private earnings. It added that a suit to cancel a deed must be filed within 35 days of learning of it, so this plaint was late.

The Court gave leave under section 12(1)(a) of the Administration of Justice Act 2048 (paragraph 13). In case No. 1096, the Appellate Court, Janakpur had finally held that the sale could not be cancelled. Here the same court had cancelled it. The leave order said the two decisions contradicted each other and caused confusion. The case carries a civil appeal number, 6288 of 2056 (दे.पु.नं.), and the judgment calls the defendants the appellants (पुनरावेदक).

**A part settlement.** After leave was given and the plaintiffs had appeared, part of the plaintiffs' side settled (paragraphs 14, 16 and 21). Dil Bahadur had died. Man Kumari Basnet and Kumar Jang Basnet had taken over his case (मुद्दा सकार, shortened to मु.स.). On 2057/1/21 they made a compromise, a milapatra, with the appellants in the Supreme Court, and gave up the claim. Only the claim of Shambhu Kumar Basnet, who had taken over Laxmi Kumari's case, was left to decide.

## Issues

The Court first noted that, after the settlement, it had to decide only Shambhu Kumar Basnet's claim, for Laxmi Kumari. It then framed three questions (paragraph 16):

1. Was the plaint defective under Adalati Bandobasta No. 72, so that two separate plaints were needed for the two deeds?
2. The seller's wife and son, of the same joint household, knew of the deed. Are the other coparceners taken to have known too, so that the limitation period runs from that date?
3. Was the land in the disputed deeds Bhakta Bahadur's private earnings, which he could dispose of at will?

For the syllabus, the first two matter most: what one plaint may cover (Unit 4A), and when the limitation period starts (Unit 2.1.3).

## Arguments

**For Padam Bahadur Karki and others (समेत), the appellants** (Senior Advocate Krishna Prasad Bhandari and Advocate Rajendra Ghimire):

- Bhakta Bahadur bought the land from Pabitra Devi Paudel in 2025. It was his private earnings, and he could dispose of it at will.
- The plaint challenged two separate deeds: Bhakta Bahadur to Padam Bahadur, and Padam Bahadur to Jamuni Das and Ramchhabila Das. Adalati Bandobasta No. 72 requires a separate plaint for each separate act.
- An earlier precedent (NKP 2031, p. 110, D.N. 817) supports this. Claims on several deeds are separate subjects where the deeds, land, dates and creditors differ, even if the debtor is the same (paragraph 17).
- Bhakta Bahadur's wife, of the same household, knew of the deed through the earlier case. The plaintiffs did not sue within the time counted from then. Instead they treated the day they took a copy as the day they learned. So the plaint was time-barred.

**For the plaintiffs, the respondents** (Advocate Dwarika Man Joshi):

- One plaint against deeds of the same plot, passed in a chain on the same day, does not breach No. 72.
- The father sold joint land and, with that money, bought the plot in Bhakta Bahadur's name, to stay joint. So it is not private earnings.
- Under section 6(a) of the Evidence Act 2031, land in any member's name must be presumed joint unless proved otherwise.
- In a partition case brought in as evidence, the date of separation was fixed in 2044, and that case became final.
- So the Appellate Court's judgment was right and should be upheld.

## Decision

The Joint Bench upheld the Appellate Court's judgment, but only for Shambhu Kumar Basnet's part. Justice Rayamajhi agreed with Justice Regmi's judgment.

1. **One plaint was enough** (paragraph 17). No. 72 let one person bring any number of disputes against one person in one plaint. It also let any number of persons bring one subject of dispute (झगडाको विषय) against any number of persons. The court then had to decide all of it on that plaint. Both deeds concerned plot No. 180. They were passed in a chain, on the same day, through one continuous process. So their subject was one, not two. The appellants' precedent did not apply, because here the date and the land were the same. The Court followed another precedent instead (NKP 2035, No. 8, D.N. 1192, p. 176). That case concerned the same defendants committing lutpit (robbery) at one time, on one day, on land with a common boundary (एकै सांधको जग्गा). It was held to be one subject of dispute. Padam Bahadur's same-day sale was part of the chain set off by Bhakta Bahadur's single transaction, and it too affected the plaintiffs' rights.
2. **Time runs from each person's own knowledge** (paragraph 18). On a question of right, each person must complain for himself. No one's right ends just because others knew, or let their time pass. The limitation period starts only when the person himself learns. Case No. 1096 was brought by Bhakta Bahadur's wife and sons, not by these plaintiffs. That case did not affect the plaintiffs' rights, and the wife and sons' knowledge could not be counted as theirs. The plaintiffs sued within time from when they learned, so the time-bar argument failed.
3. **The land was joint property** (paragraph 19). The land was bought from Pabitra Devi Paudel by a deed registered on 2025/1/5. The plaintiffs said Dil Bahadur had sold joint ancestral land on 2024/12/22. With that money, they said, he bought this land in the name of his adult eldest son. The defendants showed no source of private earnings to rebut this. Section 6(a) of the Evidence Act 2031 requires the court to presume that property in any coparcener's name is joint, unless proved otherwise. The defence said Bhakta Bahadur had separated (मानो छुट्टिनु), that is, begun to live and eat apart from the joint family, back in 2034.[^sep] But in a partition case Bhakta Bahadur brought against Dil Bahadur, the date of separation was fixed as 2044/8/15, and that case was final. So the land sold in 2042 could not be treated as land in which the plaintiffs had no right.
4. **Case No. 1096 did not decide this claim** (paragraph 20). Bhakta Bahadur acquired the land in his own time. So, as against his wife and sons, he could dispose of it at will under No. 19(2) of the Partition chapter. But the plaintiffs here were his father, mother and brothers, and different law applied to them. No. 19 deals with the property of a man whose wife, sons, unmarried daughters or widowed daughters-in-law have not taken their share. So it applies to a father, husband or father-in-law, not to a son. The Court relied on NKP 2056, No. 3, D.N. 6695, p. 239. That case held that No. 19 gives this power only to the main father of the house (घरको मुख्य बाबु), not to other coparceners such as brothers. So the wife and sons' failed case did not defeat the plaintiffs' claim.
5. **Order** (paragraph 21 and the schedule, तपसील). The Appellate Court had rightly upheld the cancellation of five of six parts, so its judgment was upheld. But Man Kumari and Kumar Jang, for the late Dil Bahadur, had settled on 2057/1/21 and given up their claim. So the cancellation stood only for two of six parts, for Shambhu Kumar Basnet (for Laxmi Kumari). The District Court's record of a five-of-six cancellation was to be struck out. The District Court was to write to the Land Revenue Office to cancel the deed for two of six parts. If Shambhu Kumar came within the time set by law, the land was to be registered in his name.

The judgment does not explain how the two parts are made up. Nor does it say what happened afterwards.

## Ratio decidendi

Our translation of the official headnote, point by point:

1. In this case, on 2042/6/20, defendant Bhakta Bahadur sold [the land] to defendant Padam Bahadur Karki by registration No. 1058. On the same day Padam Bahadur sold it to Jamuni Das and Ramchhabila Das by registration No. 1063. Even so, the subject of both deeds is the disputed land, plot No. 180. Both deeds were passed in a chain, on one day and one date, through a continuous process. Both pass the same disputed land. So the subjects of deeds No. 1058 and 1063 in the plaint cannot be treated as separate. (प्रकरण नं. १७ देखि २१)
2. In this dispute the deeds' date and land are the same, so the precedent cited does not apply. An earlier precedent concerned a claim that the same defendants robbed and beat [people] at one time, on one day, on land with a common boundary. It held that such a claim is not several separate subjects of dispute. Here, the plaintiffs' coparcener Bhakta Bahadur made a single transaction that affected the plaintiffs. As part of that chain, Padam Bahadur passed a sale deed the same day, which also affects the plaintiffs' rights. So [the Court] cannot agree with the appellants' lawyers that the plaintiffs had to file two separate plaints under Adalati Bandobasta No. 72 to cancel these two deeds. (प्रकरण नं. १७ देखि २१)
3. The wife and sons in the joint household took a copy on 2043/5/29. Must everyone in the joint household be taken to have known from then, and to have had to sue within time on that basis? On this question, each person has to make his own complaint. It cannot be said that anyone's right ends just because others knew or let the time pass. The limitation period starts only after the person himself knows, and only then does a person become alert to protect his right. In this case, these plaintiffs are the father, mother and brothers of defendant Bhakta Bahadur, who made the deed. The persons who filed case No. 1096 about the same deed, as plaintiffs, are his wife and sons. So that case does not affect these plaintiffs' rights. It cannot be justified to hold that these plaintiffs knew just because those persons knew. (प्रकरण नं. १७ देखि २१)
4. To say that one person's acts affect the rights of another, who must bring a separate claim for his own right, is neither lawful nor just. Such acts do not end anyone's right to claim, or his time limit. The plaintiffs filed within the time limit set by law, counted from the date they themselves learned. This cannot be said otherwise. (प्रकरण नं. १७ देखि २१)
5. The defence said Bhakta Bahadur had separated from these plaintiffs back in 2034 and lived apart. But Bhakta Bahadur Basnet, one of these defendants, brought a partition case against Dil Bahadur Basnet, one of these plaintiffs. That case was brought in as evidence. Its file shows that 2044/8/15 was fixed as the date of separation, and that the case has become final. On this and other evidence, the land sold in 2042 cannot be treated as land in which these plaintiffs have no right. (प्रकरण नं. १७ देखि २१)
6. Defendant Bhakta Bahadur's wife and sons sued, and their claim failed. It cannot be said that, merely for that reason, the other coparceners' rights are affected, the plaintiffs have no right and their claim cannot succeed. (प्रकरण नं. १७ देखि २१)
7. The land was land that could not be disposed of at will, and in which these plaintiffs also have a right. Without their consent, defendant Bhakta Bahadur sold it to Padam Bahadur, and Padam Bahadur sold it to Jamuni Das and Ramchhabila Das. The District Court cancelled five of six parts of the deed, as the plaintiffs claimed, and the Appellate Court, Janakpur upheld that judgment. The Appellate Court's judgment is proper and is upheld. (प्रकरण नं. १७ देखि २१)

The headnote gives one marker, paragraphs 17 to 21, for all seven points. Points 1 and 2 come from paragraph 17, and points 3 and 4 from paragraph 18. Points 5, 6 and 7 come from paragraphs 19, 20 and 21.

**In short (our summary):** deeds passed in a chain on one day over the same land are one subject, so one plaint may challenge them all. The limitation period runs from when each claimant himself learned, not from when a relative did. And land in a coparcener's name is joint unless he proves it is his own earnings. This last point comes from paragraph 19, not from the headnote.

## Why it matters

- **One chain, one plaint.** One act may set off a chain of deeds over the same land on the same day. Those deeds form one subject of dispute, and one plaint may challenge them all. The Court's test was the same land, the same day and one continuous process. This is the Unit 4A point on what a plaint may contain.
- **Time runs from your own knowledge.** The limitation period starts when the person who must sue learns of the act. It does not start because a relative in the same household learned of it, or let his own time pass. This is the Unit 2.1.3 point on limitation.
- **Joint until proved private.** Land in one coparcener's name is presumed to be joint family property. Whoever says it is private earnings must prove it. Here the defendants could not show where the money came from.
- **A case between some family members does not bind the others.** The wife and sons lost their own deed case. But the father, mother and brothers stood in a different legal position, so that result did not bind them. This fits the idea behind res judicata, which binds only the same parties, though the judgment does not use the term.
- **The two Appellate Court decisions did not really clash.** The Supreme Court gave leave because they seemed to contradict each other. It then explained that the law treats the two sets of claimants differently.
- **The present law is broadly similar.** Sections 87-88 of the Muluki Civil Procedure Code 2074 carry No. 72 forward. But s 87(1) now limits one person's several claims against another to related matters. The Muluki Civil Code 2074 requires written consent to transfer joint property (s 419). Section 420 makes an exception for the head of the household. The Code still counts time from when the person learned (s 434). See the table below.

## Full 20-mark answer

??? example "Exam answer (20 marks)"
    **Question.** State the facts and examine the ratio decidendi of Padam Bahadur Karki v Man Kumari Basnet.

    **Introduction**

    A civil case begins with a plaint. Two procedural rules decide whether it can go ahead. The first says what one plaint may cover: how many claims, and how many plaintiffs and defendants. The second is the limitation period: the plaint must be filed within the time the law allows. A plaint filed late is time-barred and is dismissed without a decision on the merits.

    In *Padam Bahadur Karki v Man Kumari Basnet*, the Supreme Court applied both rules to a family land dispute. It held that two deeds passed in a chain on one day over the same land could be challenged in one plaint. It also held that the limitation period runs from the date each claimant himself learned of the deed.

    **Citation**

    Padam Bahadur Karki et al. v Man Kumari Basnet (for the late Dil Bahadur Kshetri) et al., NKP 2061, No. 2, D.N. 7336 (syllabus: 2 NKP 212 (2061)). Supreme Court, Joint Bench: Justices Min Bahadur Rayamajhi and Khil Raj Regmi, who wrote the judgment. Decided 2061/1/24.

    **Facts**

    Dil Bahadur's family had six coparceners: Dil Bahadur, his two wives and his three sons. Plot No. 180 at Hariwan, Sarlahi, was registered in the name of the eldest son, Bhakta Bahadur. On 2042/6/20 Bhakta Bahadur sold it to Padam Bahadur Karki. The same day, Padam Bahadur sold it on to Jamuni Das and Ramchhabila Das. The family's consent was not taken.

    Bhakta Bahadur's wife and sons took a copy on 2043/5/29 and brought their own suit to cancel the deed. The rest of the family said they learned of the sale only on 2043/6/12-13. They filed a plaint on 2043/7/9 to cancel five of six parts of the deeds. The defendants said the land was Bhakta Bahadur's private earnings. In the Supreme Court they added that one plaint could not attack two deeds, and that the plaint was late.

    The Sarlahi District Court dismissed the plaint as late, counting time from the wife and sons' copy. The Janakpur Zonal Court set this aside and sent the case back. The District Court then cancelled five of six parts, and the Appellate Court, Janakpur upheld it. The Supreme Court gave leave to revise, because in the wife and sons' own case the same court had refused to cancel the sale. Before the hearing, the late Dil Bahadur's side settled. Only Shambhu Kumar's claim, for Laxmi Kumari, remained.

    **Issues**

    1. Under Adalati Bandobasta No. 72, did the plaintiffs need two separate plaints for the two deeds?
    2. Does the limitation period run from the date the seller's wife and sons learned of the deed?
    3. Was the land Bhakta Bahadur's private earnings, which he could sell at will?

    **Decision**

    The Court answered all three questions for the plaintiffs.

    On the first, No. 72 allowed many persons to bring one subject of dispute against many persons in one plaint. Both deeds concerned the same plot. They were passed on the same day, in one continuous chain. So they were one subject, and one plaint was enough. The precedent the appellants cited (NKP 2031, D.N. 817) did not apply, because there the deeds, land and dates differed. The Court followed NKP 2035, D.N. 1192 instead.

    On the second, each person must claim his own right. Time starts only when the person himself learns. The wife and sons' knowledge, and their case, did not bind the father, mother and brothers. The plaint was in time.

    On the third, the plaintiffs said the land was bought in the adult son's name with money from the father's sale of ancestral land. The defendants showed no source of private earnings. Under s 6(a) of the Evidence Act 2031, property in a coparcener's name is presumed joint. A partition case had fixed Bhakta Bahadur's date of separation as 2044/8/15, after the 2042 sale. And No. 19 of the Partition chapter gives the power to dispose at will only to the father of the house, not to a son.

    So the Appellate Court's judgment was upheld. Because of the settlement, the deed was cancelled for two of six parts only, in Shambhu Kumar's favour.

    **Ratio decidendi**

    1. Deeds passed in a chain, on the same day, over the same land, form one subject of dispute. One plaint may challenge them all under Adalati Bandobasta No. 72 (paragraph 17).
    2. Each person must claim his own right. The limitation period starts only when that person himself learns of the act. Another person's knowledge, or delay, does not end his right or his time (paragraph 18).
    3. Land in which other coparceners have a right cannot be sold without their consent. Such a sale is cancelled to the extent of their shares (paragraphs 19 to 21).

    **Comment**

    The joinder ruling is practical. Joinder means joining several claims or parties in one suit. Padam Bahadur could sell only because Bhakta Bahadur had first sold to him. Two suits would have meant the same evidence twice, and a risk of conflicting judgments. The Court's test (same land, same day, one chain) is narrow, so No. 72 keeps its meaning.

    The limitation ruling is fair. A limitation period punishes delay, and a person cannot delay before he knows. But it has a cost: a buyer cannot be sure when the time for a challenge ends.

    The presumption of joint property also makes sense. The family member in whose name land stands knows best how it was paid for.

    Some gaps remain. The judgment does not count the days; it accepts the plaintiffs' own date of learning. It does not explain why Shambhu Kumar's part comes to two of six. And it does not ask whether the last buyers knew of the family's claim.

    **Significance and conclusion**

    The case is prescribed for limitation (Unit 2) and for filing a civil suit (Unit 4A). Its rules are now in the 2074 Codes. Sections 87 and 88 of the Muluki Civil Procedure Code allow one plaint for related matters, and for many parties on one subject. Section 90 requires a plaint within the limitation period. The Muluki Civil Code bars transferring joint property without written consent (s 419), subject to the head of the household's power in s 420. It allows a challenge within six months of learning of the transfer (s 434), so time still runs from knowledge. Section 6(a) of the Evidence Act 2031 still presumes property in a coparcener's name to be joint.

    In short, the case teaches three things. A chain of deeds over one plot is one dispute. Time runs from each person's own knowledge. And land in a coparcener's name is joint until he proves it is his own.
