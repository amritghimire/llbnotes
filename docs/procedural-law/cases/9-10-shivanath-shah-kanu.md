## Facts

Translated and summarised by us from the Nepali judgment.

**The family and the claim.** Suraj Shah Kanu had two sons. Bishwanath, the elder, was the plaintiff. Shivanath, the younger, was the defendant. Both parents had died. Bishwanath said that Shivanath had started hiding the family property, and refused him his share when he asked on 2055/5/25. On 2055/6/21 he filed a plaint in the Bara District Court. He asked for the property to be split into two equal shares, with one share registered in his name.

**The defence.** Shivanath agreed with the family tree. But he said some land was not family property. This was a plot he had bought from the Birgunj Town Development Committee, and land he had received by gift deeds. He said these were his private earnings and gifts under No. 18 of the Partition chapter (अंशबण्डाको १८ नं.), so they should not be shared.

**District Court.** Each side filed a list of the property, called an inventory (तायदाती फाँटवारी), and witnesses gave statements. Both sides agreed that they were brothers and that the property had never been partitioned. On 2057/2/29 the court held that Bishwanath gets one of two shares of the land listed in the two inventories, registered in his name.

**Two appeals, two judgments.** Both brothers appealed against this one judgment to the Appellate Court, Hetauda.

- **Bishwanath's appeal was decided first**, on 2058/3/25. The court upheld the District Court's judgment. It decided before Shivanath had been sent the notice of his time to appeal (पुनरावेदनको म्याद). It did not call Shivanath under Adalati Bandobasta No. 202, the rule under which an appeal court called the other side to the hearing. The judgment does not say why Bishwanath, who had won, appealed. Shivanath later said that this judgment read the District Court's judgment as covering even his gift-deed land.
- **Shivanath appealed later.** His appeal was registered on 2059/4/3. His advocates said he appealed after the District Court's notice of time to appeal was served on him. He read the District Court's judgment as leaving out his private and gift land, so he did not appeal on that point. He appealed only about three plots: 151, 153 and 657.
- **The second judgment came on 2060/5/30.** This time the court called Bishwanath under No. 202 and heard both sides. It said it should have checked whether every party had appealed and decided all the appeals together. It added that "it is not just for a party to be punished for the court's mistake". So it would decide Shivanath's appeal, even though Bishwanath's had already been decided. It partly reversed the District Court. Plots 151 and 153 were not to be shared. There was no proof that they were registered in the name of a family member entitled to a share. It also corrected a plot number in the District Court's judgment from 650 to 657.

**In the Supreme Court.** Shivanath went to the Supreme Court twice:

- On 2059/5/18 he applied for the first Appellate Court judgment to be revised. Revision (मुद्दा दोहोर्‍याई हेर्ने) means asking the Supreme Court to look again at a case already decided on appeal. The Court must first give permission.
- On 2061/4/22 he appealed against the second Appellate Court judgment. He feared the first judgment might be treated as final. He asked the Court to hold that his private property could not be shared, as he said the District Court had held.

On 2061/7/18 the Court ordered the two to be heard together. On 2064/11/8 one bench made two orders on the same day. It refused permission to revise the first judgment. In the appeal, it called Bishwanath under No. 202 to argue. The reason was that the Appellate Court had not said clearly whether some land could be shared. This was the land Shivanath listed as private, in clause 2 of his inventory.

**The Joint Bench splits.** When the appeal was heard, the two justices of the Joint Bench disagreed.

- **Justice Ram Kumar Prasad Shah** held that refusing permission to revise on 2064/11/8 had settled the dispute for good. The partition had already been carried out under the Appellate Court's judgment. Shivanath could have challenged that execution order under Adalati Bandobasta No. 17 and No. 61 of the Punishment chapter (दण्ड सजायको ६१ नं.), but did not. So the appeal must fail.
- **Justice Bharat Raj Upreti** held that refusing permission to revise did not mean the appeal could be ignored. The Appellate Court had not decided whether the clause 2 land was private, so its judgment was wrong, also under No. 192 (a Muluki Ain court rule that Shivanath's appeal had cited). He would set it aside and send the case back to the Appellate Court to decide that question.

Because the two justices disagreed, the case went to the Full Bench.

## Issues

The Full Bench framed two questions, just before paragraph 2 of the judgment:

1. Did the Supreme Court's order of 2064/11/8, refusing permission to revise the first Appellate Court judgment, finally settle this dispute?
2. Should the disputed question that Shivanath raised be decided by the Full Bench?

Behind both lies the question the exam asks. What is the effect of an appeal judgment given on one side's appeal, while the other side's time to appeal is still running?

## Arguments

**For Shivanath, the appellant** (Senior Advocate Shambhu Thapa, Advocates Ishwari Prasad Bhattarai and Ram Bilas Raut):

- Bishwanath's appeal was filed and decided before Shivanath was sent his notice of time to appeal. Shivanath appealed after the notice was served, and the second judgment partly reversed the District Court.
- The second judgment affects Shivanath, so his right to appeal against it is intact.
- The first judgment, given while Shivanath still had time to appeal, cannot be carried out at all.
- Bishwanath did not appeal against the second judgment.
- Refusing permission to revise the first judgment, which did not even harm Shivanath, cannot end his right to appeal. Of the two judgments, the later one is the one that applies to him.
- The Full Bench could decide the land question itself, or send it to a Joint Bench.

**For Bishwanath, the respondent** (Senior Advocate Kishor Adhikari, Advocates Man Mohan Timalsena and Bijay Kumar Gupta):

- Shivanath knew of the first Appellate Court judgment by 2059/3/17, as his own revision application shows. That earlier knowledge cannot be ignored just because his appeal notice was served later.
- The appeal is against the second judgment, but its real aim is to undo the first one.
- A judgment that has lawfully become final cannot be appealed again. With permission to revise refused, the appeal has no basis and should be dismissed.

## Decision

The Full Bench disagreed with both justices of the Joint Bench, for different reasons. Justices Upadhyay and Das agreed with the Chief Justice's judgment.

1. **The refusal of permission to revise did not end the case** (paragraphs 6 to 8). The first Appellate Court judgment was given on Bishwanath's appeal alone, while Shivanath's time to appeal was still running and without calling him under No. 202. So it could not harm Shivanath's rights, and his right to appeal against the District Court's judgment stayed intact. The same bench, on the same day, refused permission to revise and called the respondent in the appeal. That itself showed the main dispute was still open. So the Full Bench did not agree with Justice Shah.
2. **Shivanath's appeal was properly before the Court** (paragraphs 3 and 7). The second Appellate Court judgment partly reversed the District Court. So an appeal against it lay to the Supreme Court under section 9(1)(c) of the Administration of Justice Act 2048.
3. **The later judgment counts** (paragraphs 4 and 5). The second Appellate Court judgment made the first one ineffective. One court cannot give two judgments in one dispute. Where it has done so by mistake, the later judgment, given after hearing both sides and weighing the evidence, is that court's authoritative judgment.
4. **The Supreme Court should decide the land question itself** (paragraph 9). Neither the District Court nor the Appellate Court had clearly decided whether the clause 2 land was Shivanath's private property. The Joint Bench could weigh the evidence and the law and decide it, so it should have done so. So the Full Bench rejected Justice Upreti's order to send the case back. It called his opinion wrong "to that extent", which implies it accepted his view that the appeal had to be heard.
5. **Order** (paragraph 10). The case was taken off the Full Bench's list and sent to a Joint Bench to decide the disputed question.

The judgment does not tell us how the Joint Bench later decided the land question.

## Ratio decidendi

Our translation of the official headnote, point by point:

1. Where the law gives a party a right to appeal, the court hearing an appeal has a legal duty to find out the real facts. These include whether that party's time to appeal is still running. It must follow the procedure the law requires, make sure whether that party has appealed, and then decide the matter at its level once, all together. (प्रकरण नं. ४)
2. Suppose the Appellate Court decides the plaintiff's appeal alone, before the notice of time to appeal has been issued to the defendant as the law requires. That judgment must not narrow, restrict or cancel the defendant's legal right to appeal. (प्रकरण नं. ४)
3. One court at one level cannot give two judgments in one dispute. Suppose that, through a procedural error or its own mistake, a court first gives one judgment. Later, after completing the procedure the law requires, it gives another judgment in the same dispute. Then the later judgment, given after hearing both sides and weighing the evidence, must be treated as that court's authoritative judgment. (प्रकरण नं. ५)

**In short (our summary):** a court's procedural slip cannot take away a party's right to appeal. An appeal court must deal with every party's appeal together. If two judgments result, the later one, given after hearing both sides, prevails.

## Why it matters

- **A court's own mistake cannot take away the right to appeal.** A party who has not yet been sent the notice of his time to appeal keeps that right. A judgment on the other side's appeal, given without hearing him, cannot cut it down.
- **One judgment per court per dispute.** If a court slips and gives two, the later one, given after hearing both sides, prevails. This fits the natural justice rule audi alteram partem ("hear the other side"), though the judgment does not use that term.
- **Refusing permission to revise one judgment does not close a valid appeal** against another judgment in the same dispute.
- **The Supreme Court should decide a question itself when it can**, instead of sending the case back to a lower court.
- **The present Code covers part of this expressly.** Section 218 of the Muluki Civil Procedure Code 2074 requires all parties' appeals against one decision to be heard together. If one was decided first, the rest must still be decided. Related rules in sections 200 and 205 say when the notice of judgment and of time to appeal must be sent, and when the time to appeal starts. See the table below.

## Full 20-mark answer

??? example "Exam answer (20 marks)"
    **2080 Q6.** Unsatisfied party can appeal to higher court. If a party to a case files an appeal when the other party of the case has not obtained the copy of the judgement and also has not been issued notice of appeal, and if the court gives an ex parte judgement, will the judgement be legitimate or not? Explain in reference to the procedure set in the case of Shivanath Shah Kanu v Bishwonath Shah Kanu.

    **Introduction**

    An appeal is a request to a higher court to correct the judgment of a lower court. In Nepal it is a legal right. Under section 15 of the Muluki Civil Procedure Code 2074, a party not satisfied with a first-instance judgment has the right to appeal. Section 205 sets the time: thirty days from learning of the judgment. A right that runs from knowing about the judgment is real only if the party is told of it. So the law makes the court tell an absent party of its judgment and of the time to appeal.

    In *Shivanath Shah Kanu v Bishwanath Shah Kanu*, a Full Bench of the Supreme Court dealt with this problem. An appeal court had decided one side's appeal before the other side was even sent that notice.

    **Citation**

    Shivanath Shah Kanu v Bishwanath Shah Kanu, NKP 2069, No. 5, D.N. 8820. Supreme Court, Full Bench: Chief Justice Khil Raj Regmi, Justices Baidya Nath Upadhyay and Kamal Narayan Das. Decided 2068/12/23.

    **Facts**

    Bishwanath sued his younger brother Shivanath in the Bara District Court for half of the family property. Shivanath said some land was his private property and should not be shared. On 2057/2/29 the District Court gave Bishwanath one of two shares.

    Both brothers appealed to the Appellate Court, Hetauda. It decided Bishwanath's appeal first, on 2058/3/25, and upheld the District Court. It did so before Shivanath had been sent the notice of his time to appeal, and without calling him. Later, Shivanath appealed too. The same court called both sides and, on 2060/5/30, partly reversed the District Court. So one court had given two judgments on one District Court judgment.

    Shivanath asked the Supreme Court to revise the first judgment, and also appealed against the second. On 2064/11/8 the Supreme Court refused permission to revise, but called Bishwanath to argue the appeal. The Joint Bench hearing the appeal then split. Justice Ram Kumar Prasad Shah held that refusing permission to revise had ended the case. Justice Bharat Raj Upreti would have sent the case back to the Appellate Court. So the case went to the Full Bench.

    **Issues**

    1. Did the order refusing permission to revise the first judgment finally settle the dispute?
    2. Who should decide the land question that Shivanath raised?
    3. Behind both: what is the effect of an appeal judgment given while the other side's time to appeal was still running?

    **Decision**

    The Full Bench held that the dispute was not settled. The first judgment was given on Bishwanath's appeal alone, while Shivanath's time to appeal was running and without calling him. It could not harm Shivanath's rights, and his right to appeal stayed intact. The second judgment, given after hearing both sides, made the first one ineffective, and Shivanath's appeal against it was valid. The Full Bench disagreed with both justices of the Joint Bench. It sent the case to a Joint Bench to decide the land question itself.

    **Ratio decidendi**

    1. Where the law gives a right to appeal, the appeal court must check whether the other party's time to appeal is still running. It must check whether that party has appealed, follow the proper procedure, and then decide the matter once, all together (paragraph 4).
    2. Suppose an appeal court decides one party's appeal alone, before the other party has been sent the notice of time to appeal. That judgment cannot narrow, restrict or cancel the other party's right to appeal (paragraph 4).
    3. One court cannot give two judgments in one dispute. If it does so by mistake, the later judgment, given after hearing both sides and weighing the evidence, is its authoritative judgment (paragraph 5).

    **Is such a judgment legitimate?**

    Apply this to the question. A party appeals. The other party has not received a copy of the judgment and has not been sent the notice of time to appeal. The appeal court decides the first appeal ex parte, after hearing only the appellant.

    Such a judgment cannot bind the other party in a way that harms him. Under the Shivanath ratio, it cannot cut down his right to appeal. Under the present Code, failure to serve notice prevents the ordinary service-based period from starting. Section 205(4) nevertheless requires a copy within six months of authentication and an appeal within thirty days of that copy. A timely appeal must be heard. If it then hears both sides and gives a second judgment, the later judgment is the authoritative one. So the ex parte judgment is not a legitimate final answer to the dispute. At most it decides one appeal, and it gives way to the judgment given after both sides were heard.

    The present Code covers related points. Under section 200(3), the court must send an absent party a notice of the judgment and of the time to appeal. It must do so within three days of preparing the judgment. Under section 205, the thirty days to appeal run from the service of that notice, or from the day the party heard the judgment in court. If neither happened, the party may appeal within thirty days of taking a copy of the judgment, if the copy is taken within six months of authentication. Under section 213, the appeal court may decide without calling the respondent only if the lower court's decision does not look likely to change. Otherwise it must call the respondent. Section 218 requires separate appeals by different parties against one decision to be heard together. If one was already decided, the rest must still be decided.

    **Comment**

    The judgment protects a basic idea of fair procedure: a party should not lose because of the court's own mistake. The Appellate Court put it this way in its second judgment: it is not just for a party to be punished for the court's error. The ruling also fits the natural justice rule audi alteram partem, because it prefers the judgment given after hearing both sides. And it is practical. It tells the Supreme Court to decide a question itself when it can, instead of sending the case back. In our view, this also avoids years of delay: this dispute began in 2055 and was still open in 2068.

    One gap remains. Justice Shah noted that the partition had already been carried out under the Appellate Court's judgment. The Full Bench said the first judgment had become ineffective, but did not say what should happen to anything already done under it.

    **Significance and conclusion**

    This prescribed case (Unit 9.10) is the one on appeals (Unit 6). Related rules are now written into the Muluki Civil Procedure Code 2074. Sections 200 and 205 cover the notice of judgment and the time to appeal. Sections 213-214 cover calling the respondent, and section 218 requires all appeals to be heard together.

    So the answer to the question is no. Suppose an appeal judgment is given ex parte, while the other side has not been told of the judgment or sent the notice of time to appeal. That judgment cannot work against that side and cannot take away its right to appeal. When both appeals are heard, the later judgment, given after hearing both sides, prevails.
