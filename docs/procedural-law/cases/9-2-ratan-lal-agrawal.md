## Facts

Translated and summarised by us from the Nepali judgment.

**The parties.** The plaintiff (वादी) was Father James Dressman (फाडर जेम्सड्रेस म्यान), chairman of an organisation (संस्था) that the judgment names नेपाल जे सुईज संस्था. Paragraphs 13 and 15 spell it नेपाल जे सुइस संस्था. The header gives the address as Jawalakhel, ward 4 of Lalitpur Nagar Panchayat (the town council). The judgment does not say what the organisation is or does, beyond the facts below; we call it "the organisation". The main defendant was Ratanlal Agrawal of Ganabahal, Kathmandu. Shambhu Das and Thakur Prasad also answered as defendants. The case reached the Supreme Court after Ratanlal petitioned the King. There the judgment calls him the petitioner (निवेदक), and the organisation's side the respondent.

**The organisation's story.** The organisation said it had employed Thakur Prasad on a monthly salary to handle its work with government offices. Through him, it had bought plots of land at Thapathali, in ward 11 of Kathmandu Nagar Panchayat, on several dates. It said he had been asked to get a building plan approved and had not done so. He had also taken money to pay tax and had not paid it. The judgment gives the sum as "२०।२५ लाख" rupees, which we read as 20-25 lakh. The organisation petitioned the King about this. Under the royal order that followed, 36 civil and criminal cases were brought in the Kathmandu District Court. An office the judgment calls only वि.प्र.वि. also took action.

**The application.** On 2038/1/24 the organisation applied under section 17(1) of the Civil Rights Act 2012 (नागरिक अधिकार ऐन, २०१२). It said the defendants were carrying bricks and sand onto two of the plots, numbers 56 and 217, to build a house. It feared a house would be built. It asked for an injunction (निषेधाज्ञा) against building a wall or a house there until the pending cases were finally decided.

**The defence.** Each defendant filed a written reply (लिखित जवाफ).

- **Ratanlal Agrawal** (2038/3/10) said he had bought plots 56 and 217 on 2032/10/16 by a registered sale deed (राजीनामा). The seller was Prabhakar Shamsher J.B.R. of Soaltee Hotel Pvt. Ltd. The land survey recorded them in his name, and he held a permanent land certificate (स्थायी निस्सा). He had nothing to do with the organisation's purchases at Thapathali. His building plan had been approved on 2037/6/10, and he had put up a compound wall. As the land was not the organisation's, the application should be dismissed.
- **Shambhu Das** (2038/3/16) said Ratanlal had bought and used the plots, and was about to build a house on land registered in his own name. The judgment does not say who Shambhu Das is.
- **Thakur Prasad** (2038/5/19) said the organisation had engaged him as an adviser, paid when there was work. In 2033 he had gone with it to buy land at Thapathali, and some deeds were registered using its seal. He had not registered all the land it claimed, and he had not taken the disputed papers. A case about plots 56 and 217 was pending in the Kathmandu District Court, and that case would decide whose land it was. Until then, the organisation could not claim an undisputed right. He himself had done no building there.

**Bagmati Zonal Court.** On 2038/8/1 the Bagmati Zonal Court (बागमती अञ्चल अदालत) dismissed the application. Ratanlal said he had bought the land from Prabhakar Shamsher, and he had filed a photocopy of his building plan approval. So the organisation could not be said to have an undisputed right (निर्विवाद हक), and no injunction could be issued.

**The title case.** On 2038/8/26 the organisation filed a plaint against Ratanlal in the Kathmandu District Court. It asked for the registration of the land to be cancelled, and alleged fraud (दर्ता बदर र जालसाजी). This came after the Zonal Court's decision. Such a case raises the question of title (हक बेहक): who owns the land.

**Central Regional Court.** The organisation appealed to the Central Regional Court (मध्यमाञ्चल क्षेत्रीय अदालत). On 2038/8/20 that court ordered the other side to be called for discussion under Adalati Bandobasta No. 202. On 2039/9/28 it set aside the Zonal Court's decision. As the organisation asked, it issued an injunction against the defendants: do no work on the disputed land until a court decides who owns it.

**Petition to the King.** Ratanlal then petitioned the King to have the decision examined (इन्साफ जाँची पाउँ). The King ordered that it be examined and dealt with under the law. The order was passed on through the King's Special Complaints Department (विशेष जाहेरी विभाग).

**Supreme Court, Division Bench.** On 2041/8/14 a Division Bench did not agree with the Regional Court's conclusion. That court had said no one could tell who legally owned the land until a court decided whether its registration should be cancelled. The bench ordered the organisation to be notified under No. 202. When the case was heard, the two justices disagreed, and they gave their opinions on 2042/4/16.

- **Justice Dhanendra Bahadur Singh** would refuse the injunction. The organisation had not shown how it came to have any right in plots 56 and 217. It had filed no proof that the land was registered in its name or that it paid the land tax. No case about these plots had been filed against the defendant before this application. The title case came only on 2038/8/26, after the Zonal Court had decided. So the organisation had no undisputed right, and where a question of title arises, an injunction cannot be issued.
- **Justice Hargovind Singh Pradhan** would grant it. The title case against Ratanlal was pending. Until it decided whose land it was, no wall or house should be allowed on it. The written reply showed a fear that a house would be built there. It would not be just to refuse an injunction only because a question of title had arisen.

Because the two justices disagreed, the case went to the Full Bench.

## Issues

The judgment does not list the issues. From the split in the Division Bench and paragraphs 16 to 19, they were (our framing):

1. What is an injunction under the Civil Rights Act 2012, and when may a court issue one?
2. Can an injunction be issued where the applicant's right to the land is disputed, and a case about who owns the land is pending? This is the point on which the Division Bench split.
3. On the facts, was there a real fear that the defendants would build a house and wall on plots 56 and 217?

## Arguments

**For Ratanlal Agrawal, the petitioner** (Senior Advocate Ratanlal Kanaudiya):

- Plots 56 and 217 were surveyed in Ratanlal's name, and he holds a permanent land certificate.
- His building plan was approved on 2037/6/10, and he has put up a compound wall.
- The organisation has not shown an undisputed right to the land.
- No injunction can be issued where the right is not undisputed. So the application must be dismissed, and Justice Dhanendra Bahadur Singh's opinion upheld.

**For the organisation, the respondent** (Advocate Sudhanath Pant):

- Cases have been filed over plots 56 and 217, and a court will decide who owns them.
- In that situation, the land must be kept as it is.
- The other side cannot be allowed to build a house or wall on the disputed land.
- So the injunction must be issued, and Justice Hargovind Singh Pradhan's opinion upheld.

## Decision

The Full Bench agreed with Justice Pradhan. Justice Surendra Prasad Singh wrote the judgment. Justices Babbar Prasad Singh and Prachanda Raj Anil added: "We agree with this opinion."

1. **Who owns the land is for the pending cases to decide** (paragraph 16). Cases about the disputed land, including the question of title, were pending, including against Ratanlal. Until they were finally decided, no one could say whether the land belonged to the plaintiff or the defendant.
2. **The Muluki Ain barred building on disputed land** (paragraph 16). No. 4 of its House Building chapter (घर बनाउनेको ४ नं.) said a house must not be built on land one has no right to. Nor could one be built on land under dispute. So a house could not be allowed on this land.
3. **What an injunction is** (paragraph 17). Section 17(2) of the Civil Rights Act 2012 lets a court issue an injunction. It is for cases where another person is feared to be about to harm a citizen's right. It stops the feared act, so that things stay as they were. It is basically issued only where such an act is feared.
4. **There was a real fear** (paragraph 18). Ratanlal's own written reply showed a "full fear" (पूर्ण आशंका) that he would build a house and wall on plots 56 and 217.
5. **A disputed right does not bar an injunction** (paragraph 19). Ratanlal's counsel argued that no injunction can be issued for a disputed right. The Court did not accept this. If injunctions were issued only for undisputed rights, a defendant could do the feared act while the title case went on. Injunctions would then be pointless. An injunction does not decide who owns the land. It keeps things as they are and makes the parties follow the path of law. It would not be just to refuse one only because a question of title had arisen.
6. **Order** (paragraph 20). An injunction was issued against the defendants: do not build a house or wall on the disputed land until the question of title is finally decided. The Court upheld Justice Pradhan's opinion, which had upheld the Central Regional Court's injunction.

The judgment does not tell us how the title case in the Kathmandu District Court was later decided.

## Ratio decidendi

Our translation of the official headnote, point by point:

1. An injunction under the Civil Rights Act 2012 is an order a court issues under section 17(2) of that Act. It is issued where there is a fear that another person will harm a citizen's right. It stops the feared act and lets each thing stay in the state it was in before. An injunction is basically issued only where there is a fear that an act will be done. (प्रकरण नं. १७)
2. Suppose an injunction were issued only where an undisputed right and possession are shown. Take land whose title is in question, with the two sides fighting it out in court. The defendant would then be allowed to do the feared act on that land before the court finally decides. The court would also refuse an injunction to keep the status quo (यथास्थिति: things as they are) while the case is pending. That would make the injunction pointless. An injunction does not decide who has the right. But it keeps the status quo and makes the parties follow the legal path. (प्रकरण नं. १९)

**In short (our summary):** an injunction keeps things as they are while a dispute is in court. The applicant does not first have to prove an undisputed right, because the injunction does not decide who owns the property.

## Why it matters

- **A disputed right can be protected.** The applicant need not prove ownership first. Here it was enough that ownership was really in dispute before a court and a harmful act was feared. The Court also relied on Muluki Ain House Building No. 4, which barred building on land under dispute.
- **An injunction decides nothing about ownership.** It keeps the status quo (यथास्थिति) and makes the parties settle their dispute in court, not by building first.
- **A feared act is needed.** An injunction is basically issued only where an act is feared, so it looks forward. The fear, or apprehension (आशंका), can be shown by the defendant's own words, as Ratanlal's written reply showed here.
- **It protects the final judgment.** Without it, the winner of the title case might get back land with someone else's house on it.
- **The same ideas are in today's law.** Section 7(3) of the Administration of Justice Act 2073 lets a District Court issue an injunction where harm to a legal right is feared. Section 156 of the Muluki Civil Procedure Code 2074 allows an interlocutory order: any order during a case other than the final decision. It may be made where, without it, the claim would become pointless. Section 158(2)(d) allows an order to keep the subject of a petition as it is.
- **Today's Code adds safeguards the judgment did not discuss.** The court must give reasons (ss 156(2), 158(8)). For an interim order, it may require a written promise to pay for any harm (s 158(2)(c)). A party harmed by an interim order later cancelled may claim compensation (s 160).

## Full 20-mark answer

??? example "Exam answer (20 marks)"
    **Practice question.** State the facts and examine the ratio decidendi of Ratan Lal Agrawal v Father James Dressman.

    **Introduction**

    A civil case can take years. Meanwhile, one party may change the property in dispute: sell it, pull it down or build on it. The final judgment may then come too late to help the winner. So courts can make temporary orders that hold things as they are until the case ends. The best-known example is the injunction (निषेधाज्ञा): an order not to do something.

    Today, section 7(3) of the Administration of Justice Act 2073 lets a District Court issue an injunction where harm to a legal right is feared. Sections 156 to 160 of the Muluki Civil Procedure Code 2074 deal with interlocutory and interim orders. In *Ratanlal Agrawal v Father James Dressman*, a Full Bench of the Supreme Court explained what an injunction is for. It held that a court may stop building on land whose ownership is being fought over in court. This is so even though the applicant's right is disputed.

    **Citation**

    Ratanlal Agrawal v Father James Dressman, chairman of नेपाल जे सुईज संस्था, NKP 2044, No. 2, D.N. 2987. The syllabus gives page 148 and spells the names Ratan Lal Agrawal and Father James Dressmar. Supreme Court, Full Bench: Justices Surendra Prasad Singh (who wrote the judgment), Babbar Prasad Singh and Prachanda Raj Anil. Decided 2043/11/22.

    **Facts**

    An organisation, whose chairman was Father James Dressman, said it had bought land at Thapathali, Kathmandu, through its employee Thakur Prasad. It said he had not got a building plan approved and had not paid tax money he had taken. After a petition to the King, 36 civil and criminal cases were brought in the Kathmandu District Court.

    On 2038/1/24 the organisation applied to the Bagmati Zonal Court under section 17(1) of the Civil Rights Act 2012. It said the defendants were carrying bricks and sand onto plots 56 and 217 to build a house. It asked for an injunction against any wall or house there until the pending cases ended.

    Ratanlal Agrawal replied that he had bought the plots by a registered sale deed on 2032/10/16. The land survey recorded them in his name. His building plan was approved on 2037/6/10, and he had built a compound wall. He was about to build the house.

    The Zonal Court dismissed the application on 2038/8/1, because the organisation's right was not undisputed. On 2038/8/26 the organisation sued Ratanlal in the Kathmandu District Court to cancel the land's registration, and for fraud. On appeal, the Central Regional Court issued the injunction on 2039/9/28. Ratanlal petitioned the King, who ordered the decision to be examined.

    In the Supreme Court, a Division Bench split. Justice Dhanendra Bahadur Singh would refuse the injunction because the right was disputed. Justice Hargovind Singh Pradhan would grant it. So the case went to the Full Bench.

    **Issues**

    1. What is an injunction under the Civil Rights Act 2012, and when may it be issued?
    2. Can it be issued where the applicant's right is disputed and a case about ownership is pending?
    3. Was there a real fear that the defendants would build?

    **Decision**

    The Full Bench agreed with Justice Pradhan. Cases about who owned the land were pending, so no one could yet say whose it was. No. 4 of the House Building chapter of the Muluki Ain forbade building a house on land under dispute. Ratanlal's own written reply showed a "full fear" that he would build. So the Court issued an injunction against the defendants: no house or wall on the disputed land until its ownership is finally decided.

    **Ratio decidendi**

    1. An injunction under section 17(2) of the Civil Rights Act 2012 is a court order. It stops an act feared to harm a citizen's right, so that things stay as they were. It is basically issued only where such an act is feared (paragraph 17).
    2. If an injunction were issued only for undisputed rights, a defendant could do the feared act on disputed land while the case went on. The injunction would then be pointless. An injunction does not decide who owns the property. It keeps the status quo and makes the parties follow the legal path (paragraph 19).

    The first point makes an injunction forward-looking. The question is whether an act is feared, not whether harm has already been done. Here the fear came from the defendant himself: Ratanlal said he was about to build.

    The second point rejects the "undisputed right" test used by the Zonal Court and Justice Dhanendra. That test asks the applicant to prove ownership first. But ownership is the very question the main case must decide. The Full Bench kept two questions apart. Who owns the land is for the title case. Whether the land should be kept as it is meanwhile is for the injunction. Because the injunction decides nothing about ownership, a disputed right was enough here, once a real dispute was before a court.

    **Comment**

    In my view the ruling is sound and practical. Suppose Ratanlal had built a house and later lost the title case. Today's Muluki Civil Code 2074 shows how messy that would be. Under section 279, the landowner may buy the house at a discount, or the builder must pull it down. Keeping the land as it is avoids that waste.

    Two gaps remain. First, the organisation applied on 2038/1/24, but its title case against Ratanlal was filed only on 2038/8/26. Justice Dhanendra relied on this timing; the Full Bench did not discuss it. Second, the judgment says nothing about protecting the defendant if the applicant loses. Today's Code fills this gap for interim orders. When making one, a court may attach a condition that the applicant promises in writing to pay for any harm (section 158(2)(c)). A party harmed by an interim order later cancelled may claim compensation (section 160).

    **Significance and conclusion**

    This case is prescribed in Unit 9.2 and is relevant to Unit 4A (court orders, interim orders and stay). The Civil Rights Act 2012 was repealed from 1 Bhadra 2075, but the idea survives. Section 7(3) of the Administration of Justice Act 2073 still speaks of feared harm to a right. Section 156 of the Code allows an interlocutory order where the claim would otherwise become pointless. Section 158(2)(d) allows an order to keep the subject of a petition as it is.

    In conclusion, an injunction protects the court's own process. It does not decide rights. It keeps things as they are, so that the final judgment still means something.
