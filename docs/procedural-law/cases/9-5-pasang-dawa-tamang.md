## Facts

Translated and summarised by us from the Nepali judgment.

**The complaint.** Pasang Dawa Tamang (Lopchan) lived in Ward No. 1 of Yarsa Village Development Committee, Rasuwa District. On 2047/6/20 a jaheri was filed against him. The judgment names Lakki Tamangni and Pode Tamangni as the informants (जाहेरवाली), the people who reported the crime. The complaint said Pasang had been coaxing them since 2041. He told them there was no point staying in a village where life was hard, and that abroad they would be well fed and clothed. On the morning of 2042/1/1 they had gone to cut grass. He took them via Pati Bhanjyang and Kathmandu to Bombay, "a place of brothels". They stayed in Bombay until Shrawan 2047 and came home in Bhadra 2047.

**The women's statements.** Lakki and Pode also gave statements that the judgment calls "attested" (प्रमाणित बयान); it does not say before whom. They said that from 2041 Pasang had tempted them in many ways. Because they were poor, four of them went abroad with him on 2042/1/1, hoping to earn money. The four were Lakki, Pode, Sisang Tamangni and Dikki Tamangni. "But the opposite happened."

**The police inquiry.** Two police head constables reported that Pasang could not be found in his village. On 2047/7/19 Risang Tamangni, one of the four, gave a written statement (ठाडो कागज). She said Pasang had lured four of them to Bombay: herself, Lakki, Pode and Dikki. (The judgment spells her name Sisang in one paragraph and Risang in another.) On the same day, most of the local people gave a matching account, recorded in a muchulka of a local inquiry (सर्जमिन मुचुल्का). They said Pasang had lured Lakki, Dikki and Pode while they were cutting grass in the Siflep forest on 2042/1/1, and taken them to Bombay.

**The charge.** The police filed a report in court (प्रहरी प्रतिवेदन) and asked for the case to start from it. It said Pasang had lured Lakki, Pode and Dikki to Bombay, India. This, it said, was an offence under section 4(a) of the Human Trafficking (Control) Act 2043,[^name] and it asked for punishment under section 8(1). As Pasang had run away, it asked the court to issue a warrant (an order to arrest him) under Adalati Bandobasta No. 94 of the Muluki Ain.

**An absent accused.** A warrant was issued and duly served, but Pasang let the time run out without appearing. So an order of 2048/8/16 froze his share of the family property (अंश रोक्का). It also put the case on hold (multavi) under Adalati Bandobasta No. 190 until he appeared. A muchulka of 2048/12/11 recorded what was found. He had sold some land, and had mortgaged some to the Agricultural Development Bank for a loan. His house had collapsed. He had no movable property, and no other coparceners or family. No one knew where he had gone.

**Pasang's side.** The judgment does not say when or how Pasang came before the trial court (शुरु अदालत), the court that first hears a case. In his statement (बयान) there, he denied the charge. He said he was working at Gorkha Durbar from Mangsir 2041 to 2042/3/22, a period that includes 2042/1/1. He did not know who had lured the girls. In 2044, on the girls' complaint, he was held for three weeks at the district office in Rasuwa (जि.प्र.का., as abbreviated in the judgment), then released on a guarantee (जिम्मा जमानी). After that he lived in Kathmandu. When he was arrested in 2044, he said, he saw Pode, Lakki and three others at that office. His rivals, he said, had plotted and coaxed the girls into accusing him. The judgment does not say how this fits the women's account that they were in Bombay from 2042 to 2047. His witness said Pasang was not the kind of man to sell girls, and had heard that he had lived in Kathmandu since 2035.

**The witnesses.** In court, Lakki and Pode gave matching depositions (बकपत्र), that is, evidence given as witnesses. They said they trusted Pasang, and four of them went with him in 2042. He kept them in a room in Bombay and sold them, which they learned from people there. They came home after five years, in 2047. Two people from the local inquiry also gave evidence. One, whose daughter is Lakki, said Pasang had lured Lakki and Pode to Bombay and that the complaint was true. The other said Pasang had taken the girls to Bombay.

**Trial court.** On 2054/9/9 the Rasuwa District Court found that Pasang had taken Pode and Lakki to Bombay, India, and sold them. That was an offence under section 4(a). It sentenced him to 10 years' imprisonment under section 8(1) of the 2043 Act.

**Appellate Court.** Pasang appealed to the Appellate Court, Patan. He said the judgment misread the law, and asked to be cleared of a false charge. On 2055/3/2 that court spotted a problem. The jaheri described an incident of 2042/1/1, but Pasang had been convicted under an Act in force from 2043/7/24. As the judgment might need to change, the court called the other side under Adalati Bandobasta No. 202. On 2055/5/16 it upheld the District Court's judgment as just.

**Supreme Court.** Pasang appealed to the Supreme Court under section 9(1)(b) of the Administration of Justice Act 2048. On 2057/3/8 a Joint Bench noted the same problem. When the act happened, it was punishable under the Muluki Ain chapter on trafficking in persons (जीउ मास्ने बेच्नेको महल). Yet the courts had punished Pasang under the later 2043 Act. So the bench ordered notice to the Office of the Attorney General under No. 202.

On 2057/9/11 the Court received an application from Pasang, sent through the prison (application No. 3213). Under rule 29 of the Supreme Court Rules 2049, he asked to correct a typing error in his appeal. In two places, he said, "misinterpretation" (अपव्याख्या) should read "unclear interpretation" (अस्पष्ट व्याख्या). The judgment does not say what the Court did with this application. When the appeal was decided, Pasang was in custody at the prison branch in Sundhara, Kathmandu.

## Issues

The judgment does not number its issues. From the appeal and the Court's reasoning (paragraphs 23 to 25), there were two:

1. Could Pasang be charged and punished under the Human Trafficking (Control) Act 2043, in force from 2043/7/24, for acts that began on 2042/1/1? (paragraph 23)
2. Which law governs procedure, including the burden of proof (the duty to prove a fact)? Is it the law in force when the act was done, or when the case is heard? (paragraph 25)

Behind both lies the question Unit 1 asks. Substantive law (सारवान कानून) creates rights, duties and offences. Procedural law (कार्यविधि कानून) only says how a case is run. How does a court treat a change in each?

## Arguments

**For Pasang, the appellant** (Advocate Purna Chandra Paudel):

- The prosecution said the girls were sold in Baisakh 2042. The law in force then was the Muluki Ain chapter on trafficking, but Pasang was not charged under it.
- Instead he was charged under an Act "born" later, on Kartik 24, 2043.
- Unless a law expressly says so, substantive law cannot have retrospective effect (पश्चातदर्शी असर), that is, reach acts done before it was made. The 2043 Act says no such thing, so the charge itself should be thrown out.
- His written appeal also said that punishing him under the later Act was barred by the Constitution. It was also barred, he said, by the proviso to section 3(b) of the Nepal Law Interpretation Act 2010 (paragraph 23). A proviso (प्रतिबन्धात्मक वाक्यांश) is a clause that limits or makes an exception to the rule before it.
- The 2043 Act was a special Act (विशेष ऐन): a law on one subject that overrides the general law on it. It excluded section 25 of the Evidence Act 2031. Under the Muluki Ain chapter, section 25 would apply, and the prosecution would have to prove the case. Using the special Act to make Pasang prove his innocence went against the law and settled precedent.
- His written appeal added that dating the offence from 2042 to 2047 misread when it happened. There was also no solid proof that he sold the girls.
- He asked the Court to reverse both judgments and acquit him, that is, declare him not guilty.

**For His Majesty's Government, the respondent** (Joint Government Attorney (सह-न्यायाधिवक्ता) Narendra Kumar Pathak, a senior government lawyer):

- The jaheri shows that Pasang lured the women away on Baisakh 1, 2042, and took them to Bombay. They escaped from there in Bhadra 2047.
- So the offence continued from 2042 to 2047 (वारदातको निरन्तरता). It could therefore be treated as an offence under the 2043 Act, and this was not using that law retrospectively.
- The Muluki Ain chapter also made Pasang's act a crime.
- The Appellate Court rightly upheld his conviction under the special Act in force since 2043, and its judgment should stand.

## Decision

The Joint Bench dismissed the appeal. Justice Acharya agreed with Justice Aryal's judgment.

1. **The old law stayed in force** (paragraph 24). The 2043 Act did not repeal the Muluki Ain chapter on trafficking. Its section 11 said that on matters the Act covered, the Act applied whatever the chapter said. So the old chapter was "carried on", and the new Act ran alongside it "concurrently" (the Court's English words) as a special law. The proviso to section 3(b) of the Nepal Law Interpretation Act 2010 does not apply to such an arrangement. (Section 3 of that Act deals with when Acts come into force; see the table below.) Nor was Pasang punished under a repealed Act.
2. **No heavier punishment** (paragraph 24). Both laws made selling a person a crime. No. 1 of the Muluki Ain chapter gave 20 years' imprisonment for the act charged. Section 8(1) of the 2043 Act gave 10 to 20 years for selling a person. Pasang got 10 years: the new Act's minimum, and less than the old law's. So the new Act did not punish an act the old law left unpunished, and it did not harm him.
3. **Procedure follows the law in force when the case is heard** (paragraph 25). Filing a case, prosecution and deciding the burden of proof are procedural matters. Procedural law creates no rights; it only fixes and regulates procedure. So whenever a complaint was filed, the procedure in force when it is dealt with applies. The Court cited its earlier certiorari decision in Ghanashyam Basnet v High Level Committee, Dang (NKP 2049, D.N. 4449, p. 28).
4. **The 2043 Act's rules on proof applied** (paragraph 25). That Act was in force while the case was heard. Section 11 made it a special Act, whose procedure can make an exception to the general procedure. The victim had made a statement at the time, which was attested and backed by her deposition in court. Under section 7, Pasang had to give reasonable proof that her statement was false. He also had to prove that he had not taken her to sell her or put her into prostitution. He could not.
5. **Order** (paragraph 25). The Appellate Court, Patan's judgment of 2055/5/16 was correct and was upheld. Pasang's 10 years' imprisonment under section 8(1), for the offence under section 4(a), stood. The appeal failed.

The judgment does not decide whether the women were sold before or after the 2043 Act came into force. It does not say whether the Court accepted the government's argument that the offence continued until 2047.

## Ratio decidendi

Our translation of the official headnote, point by point:

1. This case does not show the present Act making punishable an act that the former Act did not. Both the Muluki Ain chapter on trafficking in persons and the Human Trafficking (Control) Act 2043 treat selling a person as a crime and punish it. The punishments differ somewhat. But the defendant got less than the imprisonment under No. 1 of the Muluki Ain chapter: the minimum that section 8(1) of the 2043 Act provides. So it does not appear that the 2043 Act alone affected the defendant adversely, that is, punished him. (प्रकरण नं. २४ र २५)
2. Procedural matters, including filing the case, prosecution and deciding the burden of proof, follow the law in force while the case is being heard. Procedural law does not create anyone's rights; it only fixes and regulates procedure. Because it regulates only procedure, whenever an application or complaint was filed, the legal procedure in force when it is acted on must be followed. (प्रकरण नं. २४ र २५)
3. The procedure in force while a case is under way must be followed in that case. Here, the Human Trafficking (Control) Act 2043 was already in force while the case was heard. Section 11 of that Act gives it the place of a special Act, and its procedure can make an exception to the general procedure. Under it, the informant (victim) said at the time that the defendant had lured her and sold her into a brothel in Bombay. That statement was attested, and her deposition in court further supported it. Section 7 of that Act required the defendant to give reasonable proof of two things. One was that the victim's statement was false. The other was that he had not taken her to sell her or put her into prostitution. He could not. On these grounds the Appellate Court, Patan's judgment of 2055/5/16 is correct and is upheld. That judgment had upheld the trial court's sentence on Pasang Dawa Tamang (Lopchan). He got 10 years' imprisonment under section 8(1), for the offence under section 4(a) of the 2043 Act. (प्रकरण नं. २४ र २५)

**In short (our summary):** the later Act did not treat the accused unfairly. His act was already a crime under the old law, and his sentence was lower than the old law's. Procedure, including who must prove what, follows the law in force when the case is heard.

## Why it matters

- **The line between substantive and procedural law (Unit 1.2).** For the substantive law, the Court asked two questions. Did the new Act punish an act the old law left unpunished? Did it punish Pasang more heavily? The answer to both was no. For procedure, the Court simply applied the law in force when the case was heard.
- **The burden of proof counts as procedure.** The Court listed filing a case, prosecution and the burden of proof as procedural matters. So the 2043 Act's rule, which made the accused disprove the victim's statement, applied to events that began before the Act. Our comment: this is the most debatable step, because who must prove a fact often decides who wins.
- **A special Act can run beside the general code.** Section 11 of the 2043 Act made the Act prevail on its subject, whatever the Muluki Ain chapter said. The Court read this as keeping the old chapter alive, not repealing it, so the rules on repealed laws did not apply.
- **The rule against retrospective punishment is now written down.** Article 20(4) of the Constitution of Nepal 2015 forbids punishing an act that was not punishable when done. It also forbids a heavier punishment than the law then allowed. Section 7 of the Muluki Criminal Code 2074 says the same. The Court checked both points here, though it named no article.
- **Trafficking law still puts the burden on the accused.** Section 9 of the Human Trafficking and Transportation (Control) Act 2064 makes the accused prove that he did not commit the offence. This is a reverse burden: the accused must prove his innocence, instead of the prosecution proving his guilt. Section 6 lets the court use the victim's statement once a judge has attested it.
- **A whole criminal case under the old law (Unit 4B).** The judgment shows each step. First come a jaheri, a local inquiry muchulka and a police report in court. Then come a warrant, multavi and a freeze on the absent accused's property. Last come the accused's statement, witnesses' depositions, the trial judgment and two appeals. ## Full 20-mark answer

??? example "Exam answer (20 marks)"
    **2077 Q5.** Shed light on the facts and principles laid down in the case of Pasang Dawa Tamang Vs. Government of Nepal (NKP 2057, No. 3-4, Page 188).

    **Introduction**

    Law is of two kinds. Substantive law creates rights, duties and offences: it says what is a crime and what the punishment is. Procedural law says how rights and duties are enforced: how a case is filed, who must prove what, and how a court hears it.

    The difference matters most when a law changes. A person cannot be punished for an act that was not a crime when he did it, or punished more than the law then allowed. Article 20(4) of the Constitution of Nepal 2015 now says this. Procedure is different: a court follows the procedure in force when it hears the case. In *Pasang Dawa Tamang (Lopchan) v His Majesty's Government*, the Supreme Court applied this difference to a human trafficking case.

    **Citation**

    Pasang Dawa Tamang (Lopchan) v His Majesty's Government, NKP 2058, No. 3, p. 188 (the judgment's own header says NKP 2057, No. 3-4), D.N. 6992. Supreme Court, Joint Bench: Justice Laxman Prasad Aryal, who wrote the judgment, and Justice Arvind Nath Acharya. Decided on 2057/10/19.

    **Facts**

    Pasang lived in Yarsa, Rasuwa District. Some women of his village said that from 2041 he lured them with talk of a better life abroad. On 2042/1/1, while they were cutting grass, he took four of them via Kathmandu to Bombay. They said he kept them in a room there and sold them. They came home in 2047, after five years, and a jaheri was filed on 2047/6/20.

    The police charged Pasang under section 4(a) of the Human Trafficking (Control) Act 2043, asking for punishment under section 8(1). That Act came into force only on 2043/7/24. Pasang could not be found, so the case was put on hold and his share of family property was frozen. In his later statement in court, he denied everything and blamed rivals.

    On 2054/9/9 the Rasuwa District Court sentenced him to 10 years' imprisonment. The Appellate Court, Patan upheld this on 2055/5/16.

    In the Supreme Court, Pasang argued that he was punished under a law made after the act. That, he said, broke the Constitution and the Nepal Law Interpretation Act 2010. He added that under the old law, section 25 of the Evidence Act 2031 would make the prosecution prove the case. The 2043 Act instead made him prove his innocence. The government replied that the offence continued until 2047, when the women came back, so the 2043 Act applied.

    **Issues**

    1. Could Pasang be punished under the 2043 Act for acts that began in 2042, before the Act came into force?
    2. Which law governs procedure, including the burden of proof: the law in force when the act was done, or when the case is heard?

    **Decision**

    The Supreme Court dismissed the appeal and upheld the 10-year sentence.

    On the first issue, the later Act had not harmed Pasang. The 2043 Act did not repeal the Muluki Ain chapter on trafficking. Its section 11 kept the old chapter in force and gave the new Act priority on its subject. Both laws made selling a person a crime. The Muluki Ain provided 20 years' imprisonment, the 2043 Act 10 to 20 years. Pasang got 10, less than the old law provided. So the new Act neither punished a lawful act nor punished him more heavily.

    On the second issue, filing a case, prosecution and the burden of proof are procedural matters. Procedural law creates no rights; it only regulates procedure. So the procedure in force when the case is heard applies, whenever the complaint was made. The Court followed its earlier decision in Ghanashyam Basnet v High Level Committee, Dang (NKP 2049, D.N. 4449). Under section 7 of the 2043 Act, Pasang had to prove the victim's statement false. He could not, so her statement at the time and her deposition in court stood.

    **Ratio decidendi**

    1. The old law already made the act a crime, and the accused got less than its punishment. So applying the later Act did not adversely affect him (paragraphs 24 and 25).
    2. Procedural matters, including filing the case, prosecution and the burden of proof, follow the law in force when the case is heard. Procedural law creates no rights; it only fixes and regulates procedure (paragraph 25).
    3. A special Act's procedure can override the general procedure. Under section 7 of the 2043 Act, the accused had to disprove the victim's attested statement. As he could not, the conviction stands (paragraph 25).

    **Comment**

    In my view, the decision is practical. The women said they were taken before the 2043 Act came into force, but the case was heard after it. The Court protected the accused by checking that he got less than the old law allowed.

    Two points are open to question. First, the Court treated the burden of proof as mere procedure. But who must prove a fact often decides who wins, so moving the burden onto the accused changed his position after the event. Second, the Court did not rule on the government's argument that the offence continued until 2047. That might have given a simpler answer.

    This reverse burden survives today. Section 9 of the Human Trafficking and Transportation (Control) Act 2064 still makes the accused prove that he did not commit the offence. That sits uneasily with Article 20(5) of the Constitution, which presumes an accused innocent until proved guilty.

    **Significance and conclusion**

    The case is prescribed in Unit 9.5. It is a clear example of the line between substantive and procedural law (Unit 1.2). It also traces a criminal case under the old system (Unit 4B), from the jaheri to the warrant, multavi, trial and two appeals.

    Its principles still apply. Article 20(4) of the Constitution and section 7 of the Muluki Criminal Code 2074 forbid punishing an act that was not a crime when done. They also forbid heavier punishment than the law then allowed. The 2064 Act repealed the 2043 Act (section 30) but kept the reverse burden (section 9).

    In conclusion, applying the later Act did not harm Pasang, because the old law already punished his act, and more heavily. And procedure, including the burden of proof, follows the law in force when the case is heard.
