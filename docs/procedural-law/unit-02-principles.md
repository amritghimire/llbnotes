## Start here: fair rules for a fair fight

Think of a football match.

- The **referee** must not support either team. If the referee's son plays for one side, people will not trust the result. That is the rule against bias.
- **Both teams** must be allowed onto the field. A team cannot lose a match it was never told about. That is the right to be heard.
- Only a player in the match can **complain** about a foul. A stranger in the crowd usually cannot. That is locus standi.
- A **complaint about a goal** must be made before the match ends, not a year later. That is limitation, and, where no fixed time is set, laches.
- Once the final whistle blows and the result is recorded, the **same match is not replayed**. That is res judicata.
- The league follows its **earlier rulings** on the same kind of foul, so every team knows the rules. That is stare decisis.
- Sometimes the league asks an **independent expert** to explain a hard rule. That expert plays for neither side. That is an amicus curiae.

These are the general principles of procedural law. Section 2.2 shows how Nepal's Civil Procedure Code writes many of them into law. Section 2.3 shows how Nepal's courts are changing the way they work.

## 2.1 General principles

Procedural law (कार्यविधि कानून) is the law on how a right is enforced in court (Unit 1). Its general principles are rules that apply in almost every kind of case. Some come from the Constitution and statutes. Others come from the courts and from long legal tradition. Article 126(1) of the Constitution of Nepal 2015 says the courts exercise their powers "in accordance with this Constitution, other laws and recognized principles of justice". The principles in this unit are among those "recognized principles of justice".

## 2.1.1 Principles of natural justice

### Meaning

Natural justice (प्राकृतिक न्याय) means the minimum rules of fair procedure that any judge or decision-maker must follow. It is not a written code. It is the common sense of fairness: hear both sides, and let someone neutral decide.

It is called "natural" because it is thought to come from reason and human nature, not from any one statute. The idea is very old. The Roman law maxims behind it are still used today. English courts developed it into a firm set of rules, and courts in India, Nepal and elsewhere adopted it.

Textbook definitions say the same thing in different words:

- Natural justice is the set of **minimum standards of fair decision-making** that the law requires of anyone who decides on another person's rights.
- It is **fair play in procedure**: the rules a reasonable person would expect of a fair decision.

!!! tip "Exam tip"
    Start with the plain meaning, then name the two Latin maxims: **nemo judex in causa sua** (no one should be a judge in his own cause) and **audi alteram partem** (hear the other side). Add the third rule, a reasoned decision, and you have the frame for the whole answer.

### Features

1. **Unwritten but binding.** It applies even where a statute is silent. Courts read it into the law.
2. **Procedural.** It is about how a decision is made, not whether the decision is right.
3. **Flexible.** What fairness requires depends on the facts, the subject and the law in question.
4. **Applies beyond courts.** It binds judges, tribunals, quasi-judicial officers and, in many cases, administrative authorities whose decision affects a person's rights.
5. **Can be excluded** in some cases, for example by clear words of a statute or in an emergency (see the exceptions below).

### Element 1: the rule against bias (nemo judex in causa sua)

The maxim **nemo judex in causa sua** means "no one should be a judge in his own cause". A decision-maker must be neutral. The test is not only whether the judge was actually biased. It is whether a fair-minded person, knowing the facts, would think there was a real chance of bias. Justice must not only be done but must be seen to be done.

The usual kinds of bias are:

| Kind of bias | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Pecuniary (money) bias** | The judge gains or loses money from the result | The judge owns shares in a company that is a party |
| **Personal bias** | A relationship, friendship or hostility with a party | The judge is a relative of the plaintiff |
| **Subject-matter or official bias** | The judge is connected with the issue itself | The officer who brought the charge also decides it |
| **Prior involvement** | The judge has already acted in the case | The judge was a lawyer or witness in the same case, or decided it in a lower court |
| **Departmental or policy bias** | The authority is so committed to a policy that it cannot decide fairly | A department decides a dispute about its own scheme |

### Element 2: the right to a fair hearing (audi alteram partem)

The maxim **audi alteram partem** means "hear the other side". No one should be condemned unheard. A fair hearing has several parts:

1. **Notice.** The person must be told that a case is being brought, what it is about and when to appear. In Nepal, a court notice is a summons or myad (म्याद).
2. **Knowing the case against you.** The person must know the charge and the evidence relied on.
3. **A real chance to answer.** Enough time to prepare, and a chance to give a written reply or statement.
4. **Chance to produce evidence and to question the other side's witnesses** (cross-examination), where the nature of the case requires it.
5. **Legal representation**, at least in courts and in serious matters.
6. **Hearing by the person who decides.** One person should not hear the case while another decides it.

### Element 3: a reasoned decision

Many writers add a third principle: the decision must give **reasons** (a "speaking order"). Reasons show that the judge considered the evidence and arguments. They also let the losing party appeal, and let the higher court check the decision. Some writers also list **good faith**: the decision must not be made for an improper purpose (mala fide).

### Exceptions to natural justice

Natural justice is the normal rule, but courts accept that it can be limited or excluded in some situations:

1. **Statutory exclusion.** A statute may expressly, or by clear implication, exclude a hearing. The statute must itself be constitutional.
2. **Emergency or urgency.** Where immediate action is needed to protect public health, safety or security, a hearing may be given after the action (a post-decisional hearing) or not at all.
3. **Necessity.** If the only competent authority is itself interested, it may still decide, because otherwise no one could. This is called the doctrine of necessity.
4. **Confidentiality and national security.** Some information may be withheld where disclosing it would harm the public interest.
5. **Legislative and policy acts.** Making a general rule for everyone (such as a tax rate) does not need an individual hearing.
6. **Purely administrative or preliminary steps.** An inquiry that only collects facts, or an interim step such as suspension pending inquiry, may not need a full hearing.
7. **Impracticability.** Where a decision affects a very large number of people, an individual hearing for each may be impossible.
8. **Waiver.** A person who knows of a defect (for example, a judge's interest) and raises no objection may be treated as having given up the objection.

!!! warning "Use the exceptions carefully"
    The exceptions are narrow. Courts apply them strictly, because natural justice protects basic rights. In Nepal, Article 20(9) of the Constitution makes a fair hearing by a court or judicial body a fundamental right. A statute cannot simply remove it.

### Effect of a breach

A decision made in breach of natural justice is open to challenge. Many textbooks call such a decision void. In Nepal, the Larger Full Bench in [Pradip Kumar Agrawal v Tax Office, Morang](cases/9-3-pradip-kumar-agrawal.md) said a decision is "as good as void in law" where the affected party was given no chance to defend himself, against natural justice or the law. Such a decision may be challenged by writ at once, even though an appeal exists (headnote point 11). This is an exception to the alternative remedy rule (Unit 7).

### Natural justice in Nepal's law

Nepal has no single "Natural Justice Act". The principles appear in the Constitution, the Codes and the cases.

| Principle | Where it appears in current law |
|---|---|
| Fair hearing by a neutral body | Constitution Art 20(9): every person has the right to a fair trial (स्वच्छ सुनुवाइ) by an independent, impartial and competent court or judicial body |
| Notice of proceedings | Constitution Art 20(8): every person has the right to be informed of any proceedings taken against him or her. Civil Procedure Code s 105 sets out how a myad is served |
| Right to a lawyer | Constitution Art 20(2): from the time of arrest. Criminal Procedure Code s 130(1): the accused may appoint a lawyer of his or her choice; s 130(2): for offences punishable with life imprisonment or ten years or more, the court may order a court-appointed lawyer to defend an accused who has none |
| Free legal aid | Constitution Art 20(10): an indigent party has the right to free legal aid as provided by law |
| Equality of parties | Civil Procedure Code s 9: parties have equal status and the court must treat each equally |
| Hearing in the parties' presence | Civil Procedure Code s 269: parties are to be present (रोहबर) at the hearing. A party who fails to appear on the date does not stop the court from deciding |
| Rule against bias | Civil Procedure Code s 271 and Criminal Procedure Code s 176: a judge must not hear a case of his own or a close relative's interest; a case in which he was an agent, lawyer or witness; a case he decided as a judge in any court; a case on which he gave an opinion whether it should proceed; or one where his or his household's interest otherwise conflicts. A party may apply for the judge to step aside (Civil Procedure Code s 271(3)) |
| Fair hearing in criminal cases | Muluki Criminal Code s 10: no person shall be deprived of a fair hearing in proceedings before a competent court or judicial body |
| Reasoned decision | Civil Procedure Code s 197(1): after the hearing, the court records its decision in a register (राय किताब), which, the explanation says, records the points decided and their grounds |
| Recognised principles of justice | Constitution Art 126(1); Civil Procedure Code s 12(2): where the law is unclear, decide on recognised principles of law and justice |

### Case: Parshuram Banjade v Durgadas Shrestha (NKP 2027)

[Parshuram Banjade v Durgadas Shrestha, Bagmati Special Court](cases/9-1-parshuram-banjade.md) is the prescribed case on natural justice.

- **Facts in brief.** An officer, given the government's power to punish under the National Guidance Act 2018, jailed a student for opening a student organisation. The hearing took place after office hours, at night. The accused had no time to find a lawyer.
- **Held.** An officer with power to fine and jail decides quasi-judicially, so he must act with a "judicial mind". Anyone who decides must hear every party and weigh the merits first (paragraph 21). It was not enough to say the accused "did not ask" for a lawyer (headnote point 6). A person cannot be a judge in his own cause; in the same way, the government cannot be a judge in its own cause (headnote point 7). The court issued habeas corpus, mainly because the officer lacked jurisdiction.
- **Use in the exam.** It shows both rules of natural justice. The court applied the rule against bias to the government itself, and the fair-hearing rule to a quasi-judicial officer. The judgment does not use the Latin names.

### Critical view

- Natural justice is flexible. This is a strength, because it fits many situations. It is also a weakness, because officers and parties cannot always know in advance what fairness requires.
- The exceptions, especially "emergency" and "national security", can be misused to deny a hearing. Courts must examine them strictly.
- In Nepal, many quasi-judicial bodies (land, revenue and local bodies) still decide quickly, with weak records and little chance to cross-examine. Banjade's warning about hurried hearings is still relevant.
- Since 2015, the Constitution's Article 20 has turned the main parts of natural justice into fundamental rights. This gives them stronger protection than in the past.

## 2.1.2 Locus standi

### Meaning

Locus standi (हकदैया) is Latin for "a place to stand". It means **the right of a person to bring a case and be heard by a court**. Before looking at the merits of a case, the court asks: is this person entitled to raise this matter?

### The traditional rule

Traditionally, only a **person aggrieved** has locus standi: someone whose own legal right has been infringed, or who has suffered a legal injury. A stranger to the dispute cannot sue. The reasons are:

- courts decide real disputes, not abstract questions;
- the person actually affected is best placed to present the case;
- it prevents a flood of cases by busybodies; and
- it protects the defendant from being sued by people with no real interest.

Even under the traditional rule there were exceptions. A **habeas corpus** petition could be filed by a relative or friend of the detained person. Representative suits allowed one person to sue for a group with the same interest.

### Liberal standing and public interest litigation

Modern courts relaxed the rule for **public interest litigation** (PIL). Where a public body breaks the law and the harm falls on the public at large, or on poor people who cannot come to court, a public-spirited person may file. The court then asks whether the petitioner acts in good faith and has a real connection with the issue, not whether his own right was harmed.

### Locus standi in Nepal's law

| Provision | What it says |
|---|---|
| Civil Procedure Code s 10 | A person who wants to file a plaint (फिरादपत्र) must state in it that he has a right to make the claim, or a lawful interest or concern (हित वा सरोकार) in it, and so show his locus standi |
| Civil Procedure Code s 91 | In a dispute involving the right, interest or concern of the federal, provincial or local government, or a public interest or concern, **anyone** may file a plaint with the court's permission. The permission is sought by a separate application filed with the plaint |
| Constitution Art 133(1) | **Any citizen of Nepal** may petition the Supreme Court to have a law declared void for conflict with the Constitution. No personal injury is needed |
| Constitution Art 133(2) | The Supreme Court's extraordinary power covers fundamental rights, other legal rights with no adequate remedy, and "any constitutional or legal question involved in any dispute of public interest or concern" |
| Supreme Court Rules 2074 r 32(1) | A writ under Art 133(2) for a right may be filed by the person whose right is affected (cl. (क)). A public interest petition may be filed by a person with a **meaningful relation and substantial interest** (सार्थक सम्बन्ध र तात्विक सरोकार) in the subject (cl. (ख)) |
| Supreme Court Rules r 33(1) | A habeas corpus petition is filed by the detainee. If the detainee cannot, a relative or a concerned person who knows of the matter may file for him |
| Supreme Court Rules r 40(2)(ज)-(झ), r 40(3) and r 42 | A public interest petition must show the petitioner's meaningful relation and substantial interest and his ability to represent the community. It should say if the people affected are poor, helpless, uneducated, children, workers, elderly or otherwise unable to come to court. The petitioner must declare he seeks no personal benefit (r 40(3)), and must attend and help the court at every stage (r 42) |

So Nepal now has three levels of standing: a **personal** right for ordinary suits and writs; **any citizen** for challenging a law under Art 133(1); and a **meaningful relation and substantial interest** for public interest disputes.

### Old course: Advocate Radheshyam Adhikari v HMG (NKP 2048, p. 810)

This case was asked in 2075 Q5 (old course). It is not on the new list of prescribed cases. It is summarised here from the saved Nepali judgment (our translation).

| | |
|---|---|
| Citation | Advocate Radheshyam Adhikari v His Majesty's Government (HMG), Council of Ministers Secretariat, Singha Durbar, et al., NKP 2048, No. 12, D.N. 4430, page 810. Writ No. 989 of 2047 |
| Decided | 2048/12/17 B.S. |
| Bench | Special Bench (विशेष इजलास): Chief Justice Bishwanath Upadhyay, who wrote the order, and Justices Surendra Prasad Singh and Gajendra Keshari Bastola, who agreed |
| Subject | Petition under Art 88(2) of the Constitution of the Kingdom of Nepal 2047 (1990) |

**Facts.**

- On 2047/8/27 the King appointed Kalyan Bikram Adhikari, already the royal Nepali ambassador to France, as ambassador to Spain and Israel too. The notice in the Gorkhapatra did not say the appointment was made on the advice and consent of the Council of Ministers.
- The weekly *Matribhumi* (2047/9/3) quoted the then Minister for Law and Justice, Nilambar Acharya, as saying the appointment did not follow the Constitution.
- The petitioner, an advocate of Kathmandu, filed a writ under Arts 23 and 88(2). Art 35(2) of that Constitution said all acts of the King were done on the Council of Ministers' advice and consent, unless the Constitution said otherwise. He said the appointment lacked that advice, and asked the Court to declare it invalid. He called it a dispute of public right or interest.
- The Chief Secretary replied for the Council of Ministers. Art 35(6) barred any court from asking whether advice was given, and Art 41(2) barred questions about compliance with the government's business rules. A single minister's remark to a newspaper had no official authority. The ambassador filed no reply.

**Issues** (paragraph 8). The Court noted that this was the first case to be decided under the new public-interest clause. It framed three questions:

1. What kind of dispute is a "dispute of public right or interest" under Art 88(2), why does the Constitution provide for it, and is this such a dispute?
2. Who has locus standi (हकदैया) to bring such a dispute, and does the petitioner have it?
3. Should the order asked for be issued?

**Reasoning on public interest** (paragraphs 9 to 11).

- A dispute of public right or interest is one about the **collective right or interest of the general public or of a community**, not the personal right of particular persons. The number of people actively interested does not matter.
- A "right" means something the law or Constitution gives. "Interest" is wider: security, governance, social peace, education, health, the economy, price rises and similar matters.
- But the dispute must rest on **law or the Constitution**, and must be real, not imaginary or hypothetical. Mere intellectual curiosity or general interest is not enough. The Court cannot correct every mistake of every public body.
- The purpose is to keep the court's door open when public bodies fail in their legal duties and no one's personal right is hurt. It is not for publicity, for testing the court's patience, or for intellectual whims. The Court should discourage such petitions.

**Reasoning on locus standi** (paragraphs 12 and 13).

- Ordinarily, only a person whose constitutional or legal right has been harmed can invoke the Court's extraordinary jurisdiction. Habeas corpus was the exception.
- Art 88(2) widened the Court's jurisdiction for public interest disputes, so the idea of standing must widen too. The petitioner's **own right need not be harmed**.
- But standing is not abolished. Not every member of the public may bring every public dispute. The petitioner must have a **meaningful relation** (Meaningful Relation) or **substantial interest** (Substantial interest) in the subject. He must also satisfy the Court that he can properly represent the public concerned. General interest or curiosity is not enough. Otherwise a flood of petitions could harm the Court's efficiency.
- Art 88(1), on challenging a law, removed all standing limits for citizens. Art 88(2) did not go that far. If it had meant to, the two clauses would not have been kept separate. People with no real interest should not be allowed to force a judicial solution on those who do.

**Application** (paragraph 14). As an educated citizen and lawyer, the petitioner's interest was natural. But the petition did not show why his interest in an ambassador's appointment was substantial or meaningful. It looked like "a general kind of curiosity". Still, the government raised no objection, and the case raised important constitutional questions. So the Court chose **not to decide** the case on standing.

**Decision** (paragraphs 15 to 18). Under Art 120(1) the King appointed ambassadors. No provision made this a power to be used by the King alone or on another body's recommendation. So the appointment needed the Council of Ministers' advice and consent under Art 35(2). But a minister's personal statement to a newspaper has no constitutional value: the Council of Ministers acts collectively. Art 35(6) bars courts from asking whether, or what, advice was given to the King. The right to information under Art 16 is not unlimited. So the dispute, built on a presumption from an unauthorised remark, was not one the Court could decide. **The writ was dismissed.**

**Ratio** (the official headnote has four points):

1. A dispute of public right or interest is one about the collective right or interest of the general public or a community, not one limited to the personal right of particular persons. Whether a dispute is public is decided on that basis (paragraph 9).
2. Art 120(1) does not expressly make ambassadors' appointments a matter for the King alone, at his discretion or on another body's recommendation. So they must be made with the Council of Ministers' advice and consent under Art 35(2) (paragraph 17).
3. The right under Art 16 to seek and receive information on matters of public importance is not unlimited (paragraph 18).
4. A dispute presumed from a minister's unauthorised remark is not one the Court can decide under the Constitution. As Art 35(6) bars inquiry, the Court cannot reach a firm conclusion on the procedure followed (paragraph 18).

!!! note "Is the standing test ratio or obiter?"
    The headnote does not list the standing test, and the Court said it did not need to decide standing (paragraph 14). Strictly, then, the "meaningful relation or substantial interest" test is a considered statement, not the ground of decision. But it is the part of the case on locus standi, and an exam answer on its "ratio" should explain it. Today's Supreme Court Rules r 32(1)(ख) and r 40(2)(ज) use the same words.

**Then and now.** Art 88(2) of the 1990 Constitution became Art 133(2) of the Constitution of Nepal 2015, which keeps the words "dispute of public interest or concern". The monarchy has been abolished, so the questions about the King's advice no longer arise.

### Critical view

- Liberal standing lets courts protect the environment, consumers and weak groups who could never sue alone.
- But it can be misused for publicity, political fights or private gain disguised as public interest. Radheshyam Adhikari's warnings and the self-declaration in r 40(3) aim to stop this.
- The test of "meaningful relation and substantial interest" is not precise. Different benches may apply it differently.

## 2.1.3 Limitation

### Meaning

Limitation (हदम्याद) means **the period fixed by law within which a case must be filed**. After that period, the court will not hear the claim. A claim filed late is called time-barred.

The Civil Procedure Code describes it simply. Where law fixes a definite period for filing a plaint in court, that period is the limitation (s 47(1)). The "statute of limitation" or "law of limitation" is the body of rules setting these periods.

### Why limitation exists

1. **Finality.** There must be an end to litigation: interest reipublicae ut sit finis litium ("it is in the interest of the state that there be an end to lawsuits").
2. **The law helps the vigilant.** The maxim vigilantibus non dormientibus jura subveniunt means "the law helps those who are watchful, not those who sleep on their rights".
3. **Evidence fades.** After many years, witnesses die, memories fail and documents are lost. A fair trial becomes hard.
4. **Certainty and peace.** People can plan their lives without fear of old claims.
5. **Prevents harassment** by stale claims brought only to pressure the other side.

### Features

- The period is **fixed by statute**, not left to the judge.
- It runs from a starting point set by law: usually when the cause of action arises, or when the person learns of it.
- The court applies it **of its own motion**; the defendant need not raise it.
- Generally, the court **cannot extend** it, except on grounds the law itself allows.
- It is a rule of procedure: it bars the remedy in court.

### Limitation in Nepal's law

The Muluki Civil Procedure Code 2074 has a whole chapter on limitation, Chapter 5 (ss 47-62). The periods themselves are mostly in the substantive law, for example in the Muluki Civil Code 2074.

| Section | Rule in plain words |
|---|---|
| s 47 | Where law fixes a period, the plaint must be filed within it. A late plaint is not registered; if registered by mistake, it is dismissed |
| s 48 | Where no law fixes a period, a plaint may be filed at any time |
| s 49 | Time starts as the law that fixes the period says. If that law is silent, it starts on the date the cause of action arose |
| s 50 | If time ran out because of fraud, forgery, conspiracy or secret acts, so that the person could not know, he may sue within 90 days of learning of it. For a government or an organisation, the date its office learned counts |
| s 51 | If the last day is a public holiday, the plaint may be filed on the first day the court opens after it |
| s 52 | For a minor, time starts when he comes of age; for a person of unsound mind, when he recovers. If he loses his mind after time has started, that period is left out, with at least 15 days left |
| s 53 | If such a person dies before coming of age or recovering, his heir may sue within the period from the date of death; there are also 35-day rules |
| s 54 | A guardian may sue for a minor or person of unsound mind within time |
| s 55 | Where several people must sue together, time does not stop for the adults, except the minor or person of unsound mind, who may later sue separately |
| s 56 | A transaction abroad that may be sued on in Nepal is treated as if made in Nepal |
| s 57 | A plaint filed in a court without jurisdiction: the time that was left when it was filed is kept, counted from when that court finishes with it, with at least seven days. A plaint returned at filing gets no extension under s 57. The separate 15-day rule in s 128(2) may apply if limitation had not expired on return |
| s 58 | Time is saved for causes beyond control, within set days: a death in the family requiring mourning, childbirth, roads blocked or curfew, abduction, earthquake or similar disaster, or hospital admission after an accident or sudden illness |
| s 59 | Proof of the cause of delay under s 50 or s 58 must be filed with an application |
| s 60 | Where permission is needed before suing, time starts from notice of the permission |
| s 61 | Where the government must name the authority to hear a case, time runs 35 days from when it is named |
| s 62 | How to count days and months |

Other key rules:

- **Civil Procedure Code s 90**: a plaint must be filed within the limitation set by law and must state under which law's limitation it is filed. A plaint that does not is returned.
- **Muluki Civil Code s 434** (an example of a period): for acts under the chapter on transfer and acquisition of property, a person harmed may sue within six months of learning of the act, unless the chapter sets another time.
- **Criminal cases.** The Muluki Criminal Code sets limitation for each kind of offence. For example, s 59(1) allows a complaint for offences such as treason (s 50) at any time, while s 59(2) bars complaints about the other offences of that chapter two years after the offence becomes known.

### Case: Padam Bahadur Karki v Man Kumari Basnet (NKP 2061)

In [Padam Bahadur Karki v Man Kumari Basnet](cases/9-7-padam-bahadur-karki.md), a family member sold joint family land without the others' consent. The seller's wife and sons learned of the sale and took a copy of the deed on 2043/5/29. The other coparceners (his father, mother and brothers) learned of it later and sued on 2043/7/9. The District Court first dismissed the plaint as time-barred, holding that everyone in the joint household must be taken to have known from 2043/5/29.

The Supreme Court disagreed. On a question of right, each person must make his own complaint. No one's right ends just because others knew, or let their own time pass. **The limitation period starts only when the person himself learns** (headnote points 3 and 4). The plaint was in time.

The lesson: limitation runs from **the claimant's own knowledge** where the law counts time from knowledge. Today the Civil Code still counts time from when the person learned (s 434).

### Critical view

- Short periods, such as six months, can defeat poor and rural people who learn of their rights late or cannot afford a lawyer quickly.
- The "date of knowledge" rule is fair but invites disputes about when a person really knew.
- The Code's s 58 list of excusable causes is closed. Other genuine reasons, such as serious illness without hospital admission, may not qualify.
- Still, without limitation, courts would be swamped with old claims that cannot be fairly tried.

## 2.1.4 Laches

### Meaning

Laches (विलम्ब) means **unreasonable delay in seeking a remedy, which leads the court to refuse relief**, even though no statutory time limit has expired. It is a doctrine of equity (fairness), expressed in the maxim "delay defeats equity". Equity helps the vigilant, not the indolent.

Laches applies mainly to **discretionary remedies**, where the court may grant or refuse relief: in Nepal, especially writs under Article 133(2), and remedies such as injunctions.

### Elements

1. The petitioner **knew, or should have known,** of his right and the wrong done to him.
2. He **delayed** in coming to court.
3. The delay was **unreasonable** and he gives no satisfactory explanation.
4. Usually, the delay has **harmed the other side**, or made it unfair to grant relief now (for example, others have acted on the decision, or rights have been settled).

### How courts judge delay

There is **no fixed period**. The court looks at:

- how long the delay was and why it happened;
- whether the petitioner was pursuing another genuine remedy in good faith;
- whether third parties have gained rights in the meantime;
- the seriousness of the wrong (a court may overlook delay where a fundamental right is gravely violated); and
- whether the petitioner is poor, unaware or otherwise unable to act sooner.

### Laches in Nepal

No Act or Rule sets a time limit for filing a writ petition under Articles 133 or 144 of the Constitution. Laches is a **court-made rule**. The leading prescribed case is [Kallu Chaudhary v Appellate Court, Dipayal](cases/9-6-kallu-chaudhary.md) (NKP 2060).

- **Facts in brief.** The Land Reform Office refused Kallu's claim to divide tenanted land on 2056/5/32. The Lands Act 2021 allowed no appeal against such decisions, but he appealed anyway. The Appellate Court dismissed the appeal on 2056/11/23. He filed a writ petition on 2057/7/3, more than a year after the Office's decision.
- **Held on laches.** Our system follows the principle that "delay defeats equity" (विलम्बले समन्यायलाई पराजित गर्छ) in writs. No fixed period makes a delay laches. The petitioner must give a satisfactory and reasonable reason for any delay. And **time spent chasing a remedy the law does not provide also counts as delay**. Kallu had gone to the wrong body, so laches applied, and the writ was dismissed.
- **Other holdings.** A writ is not a substitute for appeal or revision. A party who loses on one judicial route cannot attack the same decision again on another (judicial certainty).

### Critical view

- Laches is flexible: the court can excuse a good reason and punish a bad one.
- But it is uncertain. A petitioner cannot know in advance how long is "too long". Different benches may treat similar delays differently.
- Kallu Chaudhary is strict on a poor tenant who made a legal mistake. Some argue that, where the person is unaware of the law, courts should be slower to apply laches.

## Compare: limitation and laches

| Point | Limitation | Laches |
|---|---|---|
| Source | Statute (e.g. Civil Procedure Code ss 47-62; periods in each Code chapter) | Equity and court decisions |
| Nature | A fixed period of time | Unreasonable delay; no fixed period |
| Applies to | Mainly civil suits and criminal complaints, where law fixes a period | Mainly discretionary remedies, especially writs under Art 133 or 144 |
| Court's discretion | Little or none; the court must apply it (Code s 47(3)) | Wide; the court weighs the facts |
| Extension | Only on grounds the law allows (e.g. ss 50, 52, 57, 58) | Any reasonable explanation may excuse delay |
| Effect | Plaint not registered or dismissed | Relief refused |
| Question asked | "Has the fixed time expired?" | "Was the delay unreasonable in the circumstances?" |
| Maxim | Interest reipublicae ut sit finis litium; vigilantibus non dormientibus jura subveniunt | Delay defeats equity |
| Nepal case | [Padam Bahadur Karki](cases/9-7-padam-bahadur-karki.md): time runs from one's own knowledge | [Kallu Chaudhary](cases/9-6-kallu-chaudhary.md): time spent on a wrong remedy counts as delay |

**Common ground:** both protect finality, punish neglect and prevent stale claims.

## 2.1.5 Stare decisis

### Meaning

Stare decisis is short for *stare decisis et non quieta movere*: "stand by what has been decided and do not disturb settled points". It means **a court should follow earlier decisions on the same point of law**, especially those of higher courts. It is the doctrine that gives a precedent (नजिर) its binding force.

The theory of precedent is covered in Jurisprudence Unit 4: meaning, ratio decidendi and obiter dicta, kinds of precedent, and when a precedent loses its force. See [Jurisprudence Unit 4: Precedent](../jurisprudence/unit-04-sources.md). This section focuses on **how Nepal practises it**.

### The two directions of stare decisis

1. **Vertical:** lower courts must follow the decisions of higher courts. In Nepal this is absolute: High Courts, District Courts and every other body must follow the Supreme Court.
2. **Horizontal:** a court follows its own earlier decisions. In Nepal's Supreme Court, a bench normally follows an earlier bench of equal or larger size. A larger bench may depart from a smaller bench's ruling.

### Stare decisis in Nepal's practice

| Rule | Provision |
|---|---|
| Supreme Court's interpretations and principles bind all | Constitution Art 128(4): all shall abide by any interpretation of the Constitution or a law made by, or any legal principle laid down by, the Supreme Court in the course of hearing a lawsuit. Obstruction or disregard may be punished as contempt |
| Court of record | Art 128(2): the Supreme Court is a court of record, and has the final authority to interpret the Constitution and laws |
| Conflicting Joint Benches go to a Full Bench | Supreme Court Rules r 23(2)(ख): where two Joint Benches have interpreted a law or legal principle differently, a Joint Bench may refer the case to a Full Bench (three justices, r 23(1)) |
| Conflicting Full Benches go to a Larger Full Bench | r 22(2)(ख): where two Full Benches differ on a law or legal principle, a Full Bench may refer the case to a Larger Full Bench (five or more justices, r 22(1)) to keep interpretation uniform. A complex legal question may also be referred (r 22(2)(ग)). If the justices differ, the majority view is the Court's decision (r 22(3)) |
| Review if the Supreme Court ignores its own precedent | Administration of Justice Act 2073 (AJA) s 11(2)(ख): the Supreme Court may review its own judgment that goes against a precedent or legal principle it has laid down. A Larger Full Bench or Constitutional Bench decision cannot be reviewed (s 11(3)(ख)-(ग)) |
| Revision if a High Court ignores precedent | s 12(1)(ख): in cases where no appeal lies, the Supreme Court may revise a High Court judgment that did not follow, or wrongly interpreted, a Supreme Court precedent or legal principle |
| Reporting | Decisions are published in the Nepal Kanoon Patrika (NKP), with official headnotes |

### Case: Pradip Kumar Agrawal v Tax Office, Morang (NKP 2052)

In [Pradip Kumar Agrawal v Tax Office, Morang](cases/9-3-pradip-kumar-agrawal.md), two earlier Full Bench decisions pointed different ways: one granted a writ despite another remedy; the other refused it. A Larger Full Bench of seven justices was asked which to follow. By 5 to 2, it held:

- Not every decision is a precedent. A decision without an interpretation of law or a legal principle **binds only the parties** (headnote point 1).
- A court's interpretation of law, or legal principle, **on which the case is decided** becomes a precedent for later cases of the same kind (points 2 and 3). This is the idea of the ratio decidendi, though the judgment does not use the term.
- A few decisions that granted writs without explaining why the appeal was inadequate are **not authoritative precedents** (point 4).
- The Court did not overrule the earlier case. It **distinguished** it, showing that it turned on its own facts.

The dissent (Justice Trilok Pratap Rana) said "a precedent is just like law", and a litigant who followed the precedents of his time should not be turned away.

### Critical view

- **Strengths:** certainty, equality (like cases decided alike), speed, and control of lower courts' errors. Article 128(4) gives Nepal one of the strongest precedent rules in any codified system.
- **Problems:** the NKP holds many decisions, some in conflict. Lower courts sometimes do not follow the Supreme Court, which is why AJA s 12(1)(ख) exists. Headnotes are sometimes treated as the ratio without reading the judgment. And when the Supreme Court changes its view, people who relied on the old rule may suffer, which the dissent in Pradip Kumar Agrawal pointed out.

## 2.1.6 Res judicata

### Meaning

Res judicata (प्राङ्न्याय) means "a matter already judged". Once a competent court has finally decided a matter between parties, **the same parties cannot litigate the same matter again**. The decision is final between them.

It rests on two maxims:

- **nemo debet bis vexari pro una et eadem causa**: no one should be troubled twice for the same cause; and
- **interest reipublicae ut sit finis litium**: it is in the public interest that litigation should end.

### Essentials

For res judicata to apply:

1. **Same matter.** The matter in issue in the second case was directly and substantially in issue in the first.
2. **Same parties**, or people claiming through them.
3. **Same capacity** (title). A person who sued for himself is not barred from later suing as a guardian for someone else.
4. **Competent court.** The first court had jurisdiction to decide it.
5. **Heard and finally decided.** The first case was decided on its merits and the decision has become final.

### Kinds and related rules

- **Constructive res judicata.** A party must bring all related claims together. A claim that could and should have been raised in the first case cannot be raised later.
- **Criminal side: double jeopardy.** No one may be tried and punished twice for the same offence.
- **Judicial certainty.** Courts in Nepal also refuse to let a party who lost by one route attack the same decision by another ([Kallu Chaudhary](cases/9-6-kallu-chaudhary.md)).

### Res judicata in Nepal's law

| Provision | What it says |
|---|---|
| Civil Procedure Code s 14 | Once a court has decided a case, it must not hear and decide more than once a case on the **same subject between the same plaintiff and defendant**. The exceptions: an appeal, and a matter where a compromise has not been carried out |
| Civil Procedure Code s 94 | Once a matter is decided, the same party cannot file a plaint on the same subject against the same person without appealing. Such a plaint is dismissed, and any judgment on it is void |
| Civil Procedure Code s 87(2) | A person who could have sued on everything in one plaint, but waits for the result of his first case and then sues again, cannot proceed (constructive res judicata) |
| Constitution Art 20(6) | No person shall be tried and punished for the same offence in a court more than once (a fundamental right) |
| Muluki Criminal Code s 9 | No person shall be prosecuted and punished in court more than once for the same offence |
| Administration of Justice Act 2073 s 11(1) | A court does not review its own final judgment. Only the Supreme Court may review, on narrow grounds (s 11(2)), and only once (s 11(3)(क)) |

**Cases.** [Padam Bahadur Karki](cases/9-7-padam-bahadur-karki.md) shows the "same parties" element. The seller's wife and sons had lost their own case about the same deed. That did not bind his father, mother and brothers, who stood in a different legal position. The judgment does not use the term res judicata. [Kallu Chaudhary](cases/9-6-kallu-chaudhary.md) applied judicial certainty: one route, once.

### Res judicata, stare decisis and double jeopardy

| Point | Res judicata | Stare decisis | Double jeopardy |
|---|---|---|---|
| Binds | The parties to the case | Courts in later cases | The state, in prosecuting |
| Concerns | The decision (outcome) | The legal principle (ratio) | A criminal prosecution |
| Purpose | End this dispute | Consistency of law | Protect the accused from repeated trials |
| Nepal | Code ss 14, 94, 87(2) | Constitution Art 128(4) | Constitution Art 20(6); Criminal Code s 9 |

### Critical view

Res judicata saves time, protects people from harassment and makes judgments respected. But applied too rigidly, it can lock in an injustice, for example where the first judgment was obtained by fraud or collusion. The law gives limited escape routes: appeal, review and revision (Units 6 and 7).

## 2.1.7 Amicus curiae

### Meaning

Amicus curiae is Latin for "friend of the court" (अदालतको सहयोगी). It is a person, usually a senior lawyer or expert, whom the court asks (or allows) to **help it decide a difficult question**. The amicus is not a party and does not represent any party. His duty is to the court and to the law.

### Role and features

1. **Neutral.** He does not argue for either side, but presents the law and relevant facts fairly.
2. **Appointed by the court**, usually in cases of public importance or complex legal questions.
3. **Brings expertise**: comparative law, technical knowledge or a view the parties have not raised.
4. **Speaks for the absent.** In public interest cases, he can raise the interests of people who are not before the court.
5. **No stake in the result.** The judgment does not bind him as a party.
6. **The court decides.** His views are only assistance; the court may reject them.

### Amicus curiae in Nepal's law

| Rule | What it says |
|---|---|
| Supreme Court Rules 2074 r 147 | Considering the constitutional or legal question in a case, the bench may take the help of a lawyer, government attorney or expert as amicus curiae |
| Supreme Court Rules r 84(3) | If there is an amicus curiae, he argues after the respondent's lawyer has finished |
| High Court Rules 2073 r 157घ and r 87क(2) | The bench may take an amicus curiae's help considering a matter of public importance and a complex legal question. He argues after the lawyers for both parties |
| District Court Rules 2075 r 105 and r 61(3) | The same, for District Courts |

### Amicus curiae and intervenor

An **intervenor** joins a case because his own interest is affected and he wants a particular result. An **amicus curiae** has no interest of his own and helps the court reach the right answer.

### Critical view

An amicus adds expertise and balance, especially in public interest and constitutional cases. But if the amicus is not truly neutral, he can tilt the case. Courts should choose amici transparently and give the parties a chance to answer their submissions, so that natural justice is kept.

## 2.2 Principles adopted in the Muluki Civil Procedure Code 2074

### The Code and its Chapter 2

The Muluki Civil Procedure Code 2074 (मुलुकी देवानी कार्यविधि संहिता, २०७४) came into force on 1 Bhadra 2075 (s 1(2)). Chapter 2 is headed "General principles of civil procedural law" (देवानी कार्यविधि कानूनका सामान्य सिद्धान्तहरू). It contains ss 4-15.

Section 4 tells courts that, in proceeding, hearing and deciding cases, they "shall generally follow" the principles and provisions in that chapter. Section 3 adds that procedures in a separate special law apply to their own subject; the Code fills the gaps (s 3(2)).

### The principles, section by section

| Section | Principle | Nepali heading (from the Act) |
|---|---|---|
| s 4 | Courts must generally follow the principles of this chapter | देवानी मुद्दामा पालना गर्नु पर्ने सिद्धान्त |
| s 5 | Hearing in open court | सुनुवाई खुला इजलासमा हुने |
| s 6 | Hearing only by a competent court | अधिकार प्राप्त अदालतबाट सुनुवाई हुने |
| s 7 | Questions of title decided only by a regular court | हक बेहकको प्रश्न अदालतबाट मात्र निरुपण हुने |
| s 8 | Disputes on legal status decided only by a regular court | कानूनी हैसियत सम्बन्धी विवाद नियमित अदालतबाट मात्र निरुपण हुने |
| s 9 | Equal status of parties | पक्षहरूको हैसियत समान रहने |
| s 10 | Locus standi needed to file a plaint | फिराद दाबी गर्न हकदैया हुनु पर्ने |
| s 11 | Jurisdiction never in a vacuum | अधिकारक्षेत्र शून्य नहुने |
| s 12 | Judge must not avoid deciding | निर्णय गर्नबाट पन्छिन नहुने |
| s 13 | Opportunity for compromise or mediation | मिलापत्र वा मेलमिलाप गर्न मौका दिनु पर्ने |
| s 14 | Res judicata | प्राङ्न्यायको सिद्धान्त अवलम्बन गर्नु पर्ने |
| s 15 | Right to appeal | पुनरावेदनको अधिकार हुने |

**s 5: Open court.** Cases are proceeded, heard and decided in open court, unless law provides otherwise or the court orders otherwise on a party's application. Open justice lets the public see that justice is done, and keeps judges accountable. Section 268(2)-(3) gives the main exception: family, relationship, marriage and divorce cases may be heard in camera (see 2.3.3).

**s 6: Competent court.** Only a court empowered by law may proceed, hear and decide a case. A decision by a court without jurisdiction has no legal value (Banjade; Unit 3).

**s 7: Title only by a regular court.** If a question of who is entitled to something (हक बेहक) arises, only a regular court decides it. The explanation defines "regular court" as a District Court, High Court or Supreme Court. If such a question comes up in a case before another court or body (such as a quasi-judicial office), that body must send the parties to a regular court to settle the title first (s 7(2)).

**s 8: Legal status only by a regular court.** Disputes about establishing a relationship, divorce or a person's legal status are decided only by a regular court. These matters affect a person's whole life, so they are kept with the ordinary courts.

**s 9: Equality of parties.** Parties have equal status and the court must treat each equally (s 9(1)-(2)). The federal, provincial or local government, or any of their bodies, or any legal person, appearing as plaintiff or defendant cannot claim any separate status, facility, privilege, immunity or exemption, unless law or the court provides otherwise (s 9(3)). This applies natural justice and equality before law.

**s 10: Locus standi.** A person who wants to file a plaint must state that he has a right to the claim, or a lawful interest or concern in it, and so show his locus standi (हकदैया). Section 91 makes an exception for public interest disputes, with the court's permission (2.1.2).

**s 11: Jurisdiction never in a vacuum.** For filing, proceeding, hearing and deciding any case, law will have assigned jurisdiction to some court or authority. In other words, no dispute is left without a forum. This supports the maxim that where there is a right there is a remedy.

**s 12: No refusal to decide.** A judge may not avoid deciding a case because the law is inadequate or unclear (s 12(1)). In that situation, the judge decides on the recognised principles of law and justice (s 12(2)). This matches Article 126(1) of the Constitution.

**s 13: Compromise and mediation.** Before deciding any case, the court must give the parties an opportunity to compromise (milapatra), or to compromise through mediation (s 13(1)). If they agree, a compromise may be made at any level of the case (s 13(2)). This reflects the state policy on alternative dispute resolution in Article 51(k)(2) of the Constitution (Unit 5).

**s 14: Res judicata.** Once a court has decided a case, it must not hear and decide more than once a case on the same subject between the same plaintiff and defendant, except on appeal or where a compromise has not been carried out (2.1.6).

**s 15: Right to appeal.** A party dissatisfied with a judgment or final order of the court that first heard the case has the right to appeal, under law, to the court that hears appeals. Appeal protects against error and is part of a fair system (Unit 6).

### Other principles found elsewhere in the Code

Chapter 2 is not the full list. Other sections of the Code also express general principles:

- **Limitation** (Chapter 5, ss 47-62; s 90).
- **Rule against bias** (s 271).
- **Parties' presence at hearing** (s 269).
- **Reasoned and timely decisions** (ss 197-198: decide normally on the day the hearing ends; prepare the full judgment within 21 working days; certified judgments are posted on the court's website weekly).
- **Public interest standing** (s 91).

### Critical view

- Chapter 2 gathers, in one place, principles that were scattered in the Muluki Ain and in case law. This makes them easier to know and to enforce.
- The words "shall generally follow" (s 4) leave courts some room to depart in special cases, which may weaken them.
- Some important principles, such as the right to be heard and the rule against bias, are not in Chapter 2 itself. They appear later in the Code (ss 269, 271).
- Section 13's duty to offer compromise is good policy. But it can delay cases if parties use mediation only to buy time.

## 2.3 Development and recent trends of procedural law in Nepal

### Background

Nepal's procedural law for most of the last century was in the court-procedure chapter of the Muluki Ain (the Adalati Bandobasta), together with separate Acts. The 2074 B.S. Codes replaced its civil and criminal parts: the Muluki Civil Code, Civil Procedure Code, Criminal Code and Criminal Procedure Code. The Civil Procedure Code came into force on 1 Bhadra 2075 (s 1(2)). The Administration of Justice Act 2073 reorganised the courts into District Courts, High Courts (one in each province, with benches) and the Supreme Court, and dissolved the Appellate Courts (s 30). New court Rules followed: the Supreme Court Rules 2074, High Court Rules 2073, District Court Rules 2075, and the Civil and Criminal Procedure Rules 2075.

The judiciary also plans its own reform through five-year strategic plans. The Fourth Five-Year Strategic Plan of Judiciary started from fiscal year 2076/77 (2019/20) (Supreme Court, Nepal, 2020, p. 7). Its objectives include making adjudication speedy and qualitative, enhancing access to justice and strengthening court management.

The four trends in the syllabus follow.

## 2.3.1 Separate bench systems

### Meaning

A bench (इजलास) is a sitting of one or more judges hearing cases. A **separate bench system** means dividing a court's work among benches by size or by subject, so that each kind of case gets judges with the right number and expertise. It is a step toward specialisation without creating a new court.

### Benches by size: the Supreme Court

| Bench | Judges | Main work |
|---|---|---|
| Constitutional Bench (संवैधानिक इजलास) | Chief Justice and four other justices designated by the Chief Justice on the Judicial Council's recommendation (Constitution Art 137(1)) | Petitions under Art 133(1) to declare laws void; disputes on jurisdiction between federation, provinces and local levels; election disputes and disqualification of members of the Federal Parliament or Provincial Assemblies (Art 137(2)); and any case with a serious constitutional question that the Chief Justice assigns (Art 137(3)) |
| Larger Full Bench (बृहत् पूर्ण इजलास) | Five or more (SC Rules r 22(1)) | Conflicting Full Bench decisions; complex legal questions referred (r 22(2)) |
| Full Bench (पूर्ण इजलास) | Three (r 23(1)) | Split Joint Bench decisions; conflicting Joint Bench rulings (r 23(2)) |
| Joint Bench (संयुक्त इजलास) | Two (r 24(1)) | Appeals; writ petitions after a show-cause order; revision cases under AJA s 12; and other listed matters (r 24(2)) |
| Single-justice bench (एक न्यायाधीशको इजलास) | One (r 25(1)) | Preliminary hearing of writ petitions; habeas corpus while the court is closed for more than three days; and applications against interim steps in a case (r 25(2)) |

Rule 21(1) of the Supreme Court Rules lists these five benches.

### Benches by subject

| Bench | Provision | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| **Commercial bench** (वाणिज्य इजलास) in the High Court | High Court Rules 2073 r 66 (Chapter 7) | The Government of Nepal, with the Supreme Court's consent, may form a separate commercial bench in a High Court by notice in the Nepal Gazette. Judges are designated by the Chief Justice, and a Joint Bench of two, chosen by lottery, decides (r 66(1)-(3)). Related cases go to the same bench (r 66(7)), and certain insolvency cases start there (r 66(8)) |
| **Separate civil and criminal benches** in District Courts | District Court Rules 2075 r 20 | Where separate civil and criminal benches are formed, the Chief Justice may assign judges to them, and each case stays with the bench assigned to it |
| **Juvenile and family benches** in District Courts | District Court Rules r 40क(1) proviso | The rules refer to cases heard by the juvenile bench (बाल इजलास) and family bench (पारिवारिक इजलास). These go in the simple track of case management |

### The judiciary's plans (as of the Fourth Strategic Plan)

As of the Fourth Five-Year Strategic Plan of Judiciary (English summary, Supreme Court, Nepal, 2020), the judiciary planned to:

- form a **different bench** for disputes of national importance, development, tax and revenue, after counting such cases (printed p. 36);
- form **separate benches** to hear cases involving detainees, revision petitions and petitions for interim orders (p. 37);
- apply a **panel system**, grouping old cases into civil, criminal, commercial and writ categories with a designated panel of judges (p. 35); and
- study, and then try to establish, **specialised courts or benches**: civil, criminal, commercial, consumer, cyber, family and juvenile (pp. 47-48, timed for 2022-2024).

### Update checked 2026-11-13

The Supreme Court's [Annual Report 2081/082](https://supremecourt.gov.np/web/assets/downloads/annual/barsik-pratibedan-208182.pdf) confirms that the **Fifth Five-Year Strategic Plan** began on 1 Shrawan 2081. Its foreword reports automated hearing-date management in the Supreme Court and High Courts, video-conference proceedings, and the establishment of Consumer and Juvenile Courts (PDF page 13). These are reported developments, rather than promises from the Fourth Plan.

!!! note "Plans and practice"
    The Fourth Plan above is historical background. The annual report confirms selected developments, but does not establish that every bench proposed in that plan is operating everywhere.

### Merits and problems

- **Merits:** expertise, faster disposal, consistent decisions in one field, and less backlog of specialised cases.
- **Problems:** too few judges to staff many benches; uneven workload; and the risk that a "special" bench becomes a delay of its own if it sits rarely.

## 2.3.2 Continuous hearing

### Meaning

Continuous hearing (लगातार कारबाही र सुनुवाई) means that, once a case is ready, the court hears it **day after day without adjournments until it is decided**. The aim is to end the old practice of giving dates (tarikh) months apart, so that sensitive cases finish quickly.

### Nepal's law

| Rule | What it says |
|---|---|
| Muluki Civil Procedure Rules 2075 r 36 and Schedule 10 | Cases listed in Schedule 10 are heard continuously once the reply (प्रतिउत्तर) is filed or the time for it ends (r 36(1)). The listing follows the bench's order (r 36(2)). Parties must produce their evidence on the day the court fixes (r 36(3)), and the court may decide at once if no more evidence is needed (r 36(4)). Schedule 10 lists: maintenance (मानाचामल); establishing a relationship; partition cases where the plaintiff is a senior citizen or child; meeting or custody of a minor; meeting an adopted child; appointing a guardian; a child's care and upkeep; deciding maternity or paternity; parental authority; and cases the Supreme Court names by Gazette notice |
| Muluki Criminal Procedure Rules 2075 r 96 and Schedule 30 | In government-prosecuted cases listed in Schedule 30, hearing is continuous once all defendants are present or the time for their appearance ends (r 96(1)). The same rules on listing, evidence and immediate decision apply (r 96(2)-(4)). Schedule 30 lists: rape; discrimination and other degrading treatment; offences against the protection of pregnancy; enforced disappearance; abduction or hostage-taking; and offences the Supreme Court names by Gazette notice |
| High Court Rules 2073 r 79 | In the commercial bench, hearing may be continuous (r 79(1)). Applications for interim orders not completed on the fixed day must be heard continuously (r 79(2)) |
| District Court Rules r 44(4) | Continuously heard cases may be put before the bench even if not in the weekly cause list |

### Merits and problems

- **Merits:** speed for vulnerable people (children, women, the elderly); less chance for witnesses to be pressured; the judge decides while the evidence is fresh.
- **Problems:** it needs enough judges and courtrooms. If too many cases are "continuous", other cases wait longer. Lawyers with many cases may find daily hearings hard.

## 2.3.3 In-camera hearing

### Meaning

An in-camera hearing (बन्द इजलास, closed bench) is a hearing **from which the public and press are excluded**. Only the parties, their lawyers, court staff and people the court allows may attend. It is an exception to the principle of open court (Civil Procedure Code s 5).

It protects the **privacy and dignity** of victims, children and families, and protects **national security**.

### Nepal's law

| Provision | What it says |
|---|---|
| Administration of Justice Act 2073 s 25 | A case that must, under law, be heard in a closed bench shall be heard in a closed bench (s 25(1)); other matters as prescribed (s 25(2)) |
| Criminal Procedure Code s 129 | Trial is in open court, but cases of espionage, human trafficking, sexual offences, cases where a child is accused or victim, violence against women, rape, incest, cases between husband and wife, and any case the court orders, may be tried in a closed bench. The court may bar everyone except the parties, their lawyers, court staff and others it thinks necessary |
| Criminal Procedure Rules 2075 r 63 | Copies of papers in a closed-bench case go only to the plaintiff, defendant, victim and their lawyers (r 63(1)). No one may publish news revealing the victim's identity; with the court's permission, news may be published without it (r 63(2)) |
| Civil Procedure Code s 5 and s 268(2)-(4) | Hearing is normally open (ss 5, 268(1)). Family relationship, establishing a relationship, marriage or divorce cases may be heard in a closed bench on a party's reasoned application or where the court thinks it proper. Only the parties, their agents and lawyers, and people the judge allows may enter. For public security, the court may bar any person |
| Supreme Court Rules r 81 | Espionage, children, sexual offence (करणी), human trafficking, relationship and divorce cases, and others the law or the court specifies, are heard in a closed bench. Papers go only to the appellant, respondent and victim; facts may not be published, except without the victim's identity with permission |
| High Court Rules r 108 | Similar rule for High Courts |

### Merits and problems

- **Merits:** victims, especially of sexual violence, can testify without shame or fear; children are protected; family disputes stay private.
- **Problems:** secrecy can hide unfairness; the accused's right to a public trial is reduced; and the press cannot report on matters of public concern. That is why the law limits it to listed cases and requires the court's order in others.

## 2.3.4 Use of information technology

### Meaning

Courts in Nepal now use information technology (IT) to file cases, serve notices, take evidence, publish decisions and inform parties. This saves time and cost, and makes courts more open.

### Nepal's law

| Use | Provision |
|---|---|
| **Video-conference evidence (civil)** | Civil Procedure Code s 182: where a witness cannot attend because of old age, physical incapacity, illness or living abroad, the court may, on a party's application, examine him by audio-visual dialogue (श्रव्य–दृश्य सम्वाद, video conference). He is treated as if present in court, the record is written down, and the applicant pays the cost |
| **Video-conference evidence (criminal)** | Criminal Procedure Code s 109: where a witness is physically unable, a child, or cannot be brought to court for security reasons, a party may apply for examination by video conference, or the court may order it. The parties may question and cross-examine with the court's permission, and the court keeps a record (s 109(4)-(6)) |
| **Electronic service of notices** | Civil Procedure Code s 105(21): where the parties have agreed to give notice by email, fax or similar electronic address, the court may serve the myad there. Section 115(1): notices to another court's area may be sent electronically. Supreme Court Rules r 148(1)(क): if ordinary service fails, notice may be served by fax, email or other electronic address |
| **Electronic copies and filing** | Civil Procedure Rules r 11: an electronic copy of the plaint, reply and appeal must be filed; r 12(3): documents may be filed electronically where the court has arranged it. Supreme Court Rules r 17(7): same for the Supreme Court |
| **Questions sent electronically** | Civil Procedure Code s 189(2): written questions to a witness in another district may be sent electronically |
| **Websites and judgments online** | Civil Procedure Code s 198(3): each week the court posts the list of certified judgments on its notice board and website. Supreme Court Rules r 125: cause lists, case status, judgments and forms go on the court website |
| **SMS and email updates** | Supreme Court Rules r 126 and District Court Rules r 110: the court may tell parties by email or mobile when a case is registered, dates are fixed, or an order or judgment is made |
| **Online copies and payment** | District Court Rules r 76क (online request for copies) and r 119क (online payment of court fees, fines and deposits), both added by the fourth amendment of 2083 |

The Annual Report 2081/082 records automated hearing-date management and video-conference proceedings (PDF page 13). This gives a later example of implementation. **The Fourth Strategic Plan** (2020) had planned to make the Case Management System automated and linked to IT, and to study pleading by video conference (printed p. 37). It also planned software for online pleading and witness or expert examination, court-room technology and video-conference equipment (p. 89).

### Merits and problems

- **Merits:** speed, lower cost, less travel for witnesses abroad or ill, transparency (judgments online), and fewer chances for staff to demand money.
- **Problems:** poor internet in rural areas; many parties cannot use email; risks to data security and privacy; and the need to make sure a witness on video is not being coached off-screen.

### Other trends

- **Differentiated case management (फरक मुद्दा व्यवस्थापन):** cases are put in simple, general and special tracks, with time targets of six months, one year and one and a half years (District Court Rules r 40क; High Court Rules r 34क).
- **ADR inside the court:** s 13 of the Code requires an offer of compromise or mediation before judgment (Unit 5).
- **Time limits on judges:** decide normally on the day the hearing ends (Code s 197(1)) and prepare the full judgment within 21 working days (s 198(1)). In criminal cases, decide normally within a month of the end of evidence (Criminal Procedure Code s 131(1)).

??? example "Q1. What is the principle of natural justice? Explain its elements and exceptions. (2077 Q2, 2080 Q3; old course 2075 Q7(d), 20 marks)"
    **Introduction**

    Every legal system needs not only just laws but also a just way of applying them. A person may lose a case he should have won if the judge was biased, or if he was never heard. Natural justice (प्राकृतिक न्याय) is the name for the minimum rules of fair procedure that guard against this.

    Natural justice means the basic standards of fairness that any judge, tribunal or authority must follow when deciding on a person's rights. It is called "natural" because it rests on reason and conscience, not on any single statute. It is often summed up in two Latin maxims: **nemo judex in causa sua** (no one should be a judge in his own cause) and **audi alteram partem** (hear the other side). Many writers add a third: the duty to give a **reasoned decision**.

    **Nature of natural justice**

    1. It is unwritten but binding. Courts read it into every law unless the law clearly excludes it.
    2. It is procedural: it looks at how a decision was made, not whether it was correct.
    3. It is flexible. What it requires depends on the facts and the kind of decision.
    4. It applies to courts, to quasi-judicial bodies and, in many situations, to administrative authorities.
    5. Its breach makes a decision open to being set aside.

    **Element 1: rule against bias (nemo judex in causa sua)**

    A judge must be impartial. The test is whether a fair-minded observer would see a real chance of bias, because justice must be seen to be done. The main kinds of bias are:

    - **Pecuniary bias:** the judge has a money interest in the result. Even a small interest disqualifies.
    - **Personal bias:** friendship, family ties or hostility towards a party.
    - **Official or subject-matter bias:** the judge is connected with the issue, for example as the person who brought the charge.
    - **Prior involvement:** the judge earlier acted as lawyer, witness or lower-court judge in the same case.
    - **Departmental bias:** an authority decides a dispute about its own policy.

    **Element 2: right to a fair hearing (audi alteram partem)**

    No one should be condemned unheard. A fair hearing includes:

    1. **Notice** of the case, its subject and the date of hearing.
    2. **Disclosure** of the charge and the evidence against the person.
    3. A real **opportunity to reply**, with enough time to prepare.
    4. The chance to **produce evidence** and to **cross-examine** witnesses where needed.
    5. **Legal representation**, at least in courts and serious matters.
    6. **He who decides must hear**: the decision-maker must be the one who heard the case.

    **Element 3: reasoned decision**

    The decision should state its grounds. Reasons show that the judge applied his mind, help the losing party decide whether to appeal, and let a higher court check the decision. Good faith is also required: a decision made for an improper purpose (mala fide) is unfair.

    **Exceptions**

    Natural justice is not absolute. It may be limited or excluded in these situations:

    1. **Statutory exclusion**, by clear words, provided the statute is itself valid under the Constitution.
    2. **Emergency**, where urgent action is needed for public safety or health; a hearing may follow the action.
    3. **Necessity**, where the only competent authority is interested, so it must decide or no one can.
    4. **National security and confidentiality**, where disclosure would harm the public.
    5. **Legislative or policy acts** that apply to everyone, such as fixing a tax rate.
    6. **Preliminary or interim steps**, such as a fact-finding inquiry or suspension pending inquiry.
    7. **Impracticability**, where too many people are affected to hear each one.
    8. **Waiver**, where a party knows of a defect and does not object.

    These exceptions are narrow and courts read them strictly.

    **Nepal's position**

    Nepal has given natural justice constitutional and statutory form:

    - **Constitution of Nepal 2015, Art 20(9):** every person has the right to a fair trial by an independent, impartial and competent court or judicial body. **Art 20(8):** the right to be informed of proceedings against oneself. **Art 20(2):** the right to a lawyer from the time of arrest. **Art 20(10):** free legal aid for the indigent. **Art 126(1):** courts act under the Constitution, laws and recognised principles of justice.
    - **Muluki Civil Procedure Code 2074:** s 9 (equal status and equal treatment of parties); s 105 (service of notice); s 269 (parties present at hearing); s 271 (a judge must not hear a case of his own or a close relative's interest, one in which he was a lawyer or witness, or one he decided before; a party may apply for him to step aside); s 197 (decision with its grounds recorded).
    - **Muluki Criminal Procedure Code 2074:** s 176 (the same rule against bias); s 130 (right to a lawyer).
    - **Muluki Criminal Code 2074, s 10:** no one shall be deprived of a fair hearing.

    **Cases.** In *Parshuram Banjade v Durgadas Shrestha* (NKP 2027), an officer jailed a student after a hurried night hearing with no lawyer. The Supreme Court held that a quasi-judicial officer must decide with a judicial mind and hear every party. It said that, as a person cannot be a judge in his own cause, the government cannot be a judge in its own cause either. In *Pradip Kumar Agrawal v Tax Office, Morang* (NKP 2052), a Larger Full Bench held that a decision made without giving the affected party a chance to defend himself, against natural justice or law, is as good as void. It can be challenged by writ at once, even where an appeal exists.

    **Critical view**

    Natural justice has strengths: it protects individuals against arbitrary power, improves the quality of decisions and builds public trust. But it also has problems. Its flexibility makes it uncertain; officers may not know what is required. The exceptions, especially emergency and security, can be used as excuses. In Nepal, many quasi-judicial bodies still decide quickly, with poor records, and the poor often cannot afford lawyers despite Art 20(10). On the positive side, the 2015 Constitution and the 2074 Codes have written the core rules into law, so they no longer depend on court decisions alone.

    **Conclusion**

    Natural justice rests on two pillars, an unbiased judge and a fair hearing, supported by the duty to give reasons. It yields only in narrow exceptions. In Nepal it is now part of the fundamental right to a fair trial under Article 20, of the Codes, and of settled case law from Banjade onward. A decision that ignores it may be fast, but it is not justice.

??? example "Q2. What is meant by the doctrine of statute of limitation? Differentiate between limitation and the doctrine of laches. (2080 Q4; old course 2075 Q7(a), 20 marks)"
    **Introduction**

    Rights must be enforced within a reasonable time. A person who waits too long makes a fair trial difficult and leaves the other side in doubt for ever. The law deals with delay in two ways: by fixed time limits, called **limitation**, and by the equitable doctrine of **laches**.

    **Meaning of limitation**

    Limitation (हदम्याद) is the period fixed by law within which a case must be filed in court. The statute of limitation is the body of law that fixes these periods and says when they start and how they are counted. The Muluki Civil Procedure Code 2074 describes it this way: where law fixes a definite period for filing a plaint, that period is the limitation (s 47(1)).

    **Rationale**

    1. **Finality:** interest reipublicae ut sit finis litium; litigation must end.
    2. **Vigilance:** vigilantibus non dormientibus jura subveniunt; the law helps the watchful, not those who sleep on their rights.
    3. **Evidence:** old claims are hard to prove or disprove fairly.
    4. **Peace and certainty** for defendants and third parties.
    5. **Prevention of harassment** by stale claims.

    **Features**

    - Fixed by statute, not by the judge.
    - Runs from a starting point set by law.
    - Applied by the court on its own; a late plaint is not registered.
    - Extended only on grounds the law itself allows.

    **Nepal's law of limitation**

    Chapter 5 of the Civil Procedure Code (ss 47-62) sets the general rules:

    - **s 47:** a plaint must be filed within the period; a late plaint is not registered, and if registered by mistake is dismissed.
    - **s 48:** where no period is fixed, a plaint may be filed at any time.
    - **s 49:** time starts as the relevant law says, or else from the date the cause of action arose.
    - **s 50:** where fraud, forgery, conspiracy or secret acts prevented knowledge, the person may sue within 90 days of learning.
    - **s 51:** if the last day is a public holiday, filing on the next open day is in time.
    - **ss 52-54:** for minors and persons of unsound mind, time starts when they come of age or recover; heirs and guardians may sue.
    - **s 57:** time spent in a court without jurisdiction is saved, in part.
    - **s 58:** causes beyond control save time for set days: mourning, childbirth, blocked roads or curfew, abduction, disaster, hospital admission.
    - **s 90:** the plaint must state the law under whose limitation it is filed.

    The periods themselves are in the substantive law. For example, Muluki Civil Code s 434 gives six months from knowledge for acts under the chapter on transfer of property. In criminal law, the Muluki Criminal Code sets periods by offence; for example, s 59(2) bars complaints for certain offences two years after they become known.

    **Case.** In *Padam Bahadur Karki v Man Kumari Basnet* (NKP 2061), the seller's wife and sons knew of a sale earlier than his father, mother and brothers. The Supreme Court held that each person must complain for himself, and limitation starts only when the person himself learns. The plaint was in time.

    **Meaning of laches**

    Laches (विलम्ब) is unreasonable delay in seeking a remedy, which leads the court to refuse relief though no statutory period has expired. It comes from equity: "delay defeats equity". It applies mainly to discretionary remedies, especially writs. Its elements are knowledge of the right, delay, no satisfactory explanation, and usually prejudice to the other side.

    **Laches in Nepal.** No law sets a time for writ petitions under Arts 133 or 144 of the Constitution. In *Kallu Chaudhary v Appellate Court, Dipayal* (NKP 2060), the Supreme Court said our system follows "delay defeats equity" in writs. No fixed period makes a delay laches; the petitioner must give a satisfactory and reasonable reason. Time spent chasing a remedy the law does not provide also counts as delay. The writ, filed more than a year after the decision, was dismissed.

    **Differences between limitation and laches**

    | Point | Limitation | Laches |
    |---|---|---|
    | Source | Statute | Equity, court decisions |
    | Period | Fixed | Not fixed |
    | Court's role | Mandatory; little discretion | Discretionary |
    | Applies to | Suits and complaints where a period is fixed | Writs and other discretionary relief |
    | Extension | Only on statutory grounds | Any reasonable explanation |
    | Test | Has time expired? | Was the delay unreasonable and harmful? |
    | Effect | Plaint not registered or dismissed | Relief refused |
    | Nepal | Code ss 47-62, s 90 | Kallu Chaudhary (court-made) |

    **Similarities.** Both rest on finality, both punish neglect, and both protect the other side from stale claims.

    **Critical view**

    Limitation gives certainty but can be harsh. Short periods such as six months may defeat rural or poor people who learn of their rights late. The date-of-knowledge rule, applied in Padam Bahadur Karki, softens this, but leads to disputes about when a person really knew. Laches is fairer in principle because it looks at reasons, but it is uncertain; similar delays may be treated differently by different benches. Kallu Chaudhary's rule that time spent on a wrong remedy counts as delay is strict on parties who make honest legal mistakes. A balance is needed: clear periods for ordinary suits, and in writs a flexible test that is applied with sympathy for the weak.

    **Conclusion**

    Limitation is a fixed statutory period after which a case cannot be filed. Laches is unreasonable delay that defeats discretionary relief where no period is fixed. Nepal's Civil Procedure Code sets detailed limitation rules in ss 47-62, while laches remains a court-made rule for writs. Both express the same idea: the law helps those who are vigilant about their rights.

??? example "Q3. Explain the doctrine of stare decisis. How is it followed in Nepal? (likely long question; 2080 Q7(a) and 2075 Q7(b)-(c) as short notes, 20 marks)"
    **Introduction**

    Courts decide disputes one at a time. But if each judge decided similar cases differently, the law would be uncertain and unequal. The doctrine of stare decisis solves this. It comes from the maxim *stare decisis et non quieta movere*: "stand by what has been decided and do not disturb settled points". It means that a court follows earlier decisions on the same point of law, especially those of higher courts. An earlier decision that is followed in this way is a precedent (नजिर).

    **What binds: ratio and obiter**

    Only the **ratio decidendi**, the legal principle necessary to decide the case on its material facts, binds. **Obiter dicta**, remarks made by the way, only persuade. (For the theory of precedent, see Jurisprudence Unit 4.)

    **Vertical and horizontal stare decisis**

    - **Vertical:** a lower court must follow a higher court's decision even if it disagrees.
    - **Horizontal:** a court generally follows its own earlier decisions. A larger bench may depart from a smaller bench's ruling.

    **When a precedent loses force**

    When it is reversed or overruled; given per incuriam (in ignorance of a statute or binding decision); decided sub silentio (without argument on the point); distinguished on the facts; or changed by statute.

    **Merits**

    1. Certainty and predictability: people and lawyers know the law.
    2. Equality: like cases are decided alike.
    3. Efficiency: settled points need not be argued again.
    4. Control: lower courts' errors are corrected.
    5. Growth: the law develops case by case.

    **Demerits**

    1. Rigidity: a wrong decision can bind for years.
    2. Volume: many reported cases, some conflicting.
    3. Difficulty in finding the ratio in long judgments.
    4. Retrospective effect: a new rule applies to past acts.

    **Stare decisis in Nepal**

    Nepal is mainly a codified system, but it has adopted binding precedent firmly.

    1. **Constitution of Nepal 2015, Art 128(4):** all shall abide by any interpretation of the Constitution or a law, or any legal principle, laid down by the Supreme Court in hearing a lawsuit. Obstruction or disregard can be punished as contempt.
    2. **Art 128(2):** the Supreme Court is a court of record with final authority to interpret the Constitution and laws.
    3. **Bench structure (Supreme Court Rules 2074):** where two Joint Benches differ on a law or legal principle, the matter may go to a Full Bench of three (r 23(2)(ख)). Where two Full Benches differ, a Full Bench may send it to a Larger Full Bench of five or more to keep interpretation uniform (r 22(2)(ख)); the majority view is the decision (r 22(3)). So the Supreme Court can resolve its own conflicts and depart from smaller benches' rulings.
    4. **Administration of Justice Act 2073:** the Supreme Court may review its own judgment that goes against its established precedent or legal principle (s 11(2)(ख)), though not a decision of a Larger Full Bench or Constitutional Bench (s 11(3)). It may revise a High Court judgment that failed to follow, or misinterpreted, a Supreme Court precedent (s 12(1)(ख)).
    5. **Reporting:** decisions are published in the Nepal Kanoon Patrika (NKP), with official headnotes.

    **Case: Pradip Kumar Agrawal v Tax Office, Morang (NKP 2052).** Two Full Bench decisions conflicted on whether a writ lies when an appeal is available. A Larger Full Bench of seven justices, by 5 to 2, held that not every decision is a precedent. A decision without an interpretation of law or legal principle binds only the parties. Only the interpretation or principle on which a case is decided becomes a precedent for similar cases. A few unreasoned decisions that granted writs despite an appeal were not authoritative precedents. The Court distinguished the earlier case instead of overruling it. The dissent warned that a precedent is like law, and a litigant who relied on the precedents of his day should not suffer.

    **Persuasive authority.** Nepali courts also cite foreign and international decisions, which persuade but do not bind.

    **Critical view**

    Article 128(4) makes Nepal's precedent rule stronger than in many common-law countries: it binds "all", not only courts. This has helped protect rights and fill gaps in legislation. But problems remain. The NKP contains conflicting decisions, and lower courts and offices do not always follow the Supreme Court; that is why AJA s 12(1)(ख) exists. Students and lawyers often rely on headnotes without reading the reasoning, though Pradip Kumar Agrawal shows that only the reasoned principle binds. Changes in the Court's view can also be unfair to those who relied on the old rule, a point the dissent made, which suggests a need for prospective overruling in suitable cases.

    **Conclusion**

    Stare decisis means standing by decided points of law. In Nepal it rests on Article 128(4) of the Constitution, is enforced through the Supreme Court's bench structure and the review and revision powers in the Administration of Justice Act, and is explained in Pradip Kumar Agrawal. It gives Nepal's codified system certainty and equality, provided the courts keep their precedents clear and consistent.

??? example "Q4. What are the general principles of civil procedural law? Explain the major principles in detail. (2077 Q4; old course 2075 Q1, 20 marks)"
    **Introduction**

    Civil procedural law is the law that governs how civil disputes, about rights, property, contracts and family matters, are brought to court, heard and decided. Its general principles are the basic rules that apply to every civil case, whatever its subject. In Nepal, the Muluki Civil Procedure Code 2074 (मुलुकी देवानी कार्यविधि संहिता, २०७४), in force from 1 Bhadra 2075, sets them out in Chapter 2, headed "General principles of civil procedural law" (ss 4-15). Section 4 requires courts generally to follow these principles in proceeding, hearing and deciding cases.

    **Major principles in the Code**

    1. **Open court (s 5).** Cases are heard and decided in open court, unless law provides otherwise or the court orders otherwise on a party's application. Open justice keeps judges accountable and builds public trust. The exception for family matters is in s 268(2).

    2. **Competent court (s 6).** Only a court empowered by law may hear and decide a case. A decision without jurisdiction has no legal value.

    3. **Title only by a regular court (s 7).** A question of entitlement (हक बेहक) is settled only by a regular court: a District Court, High Court or Supreme Court. If it arises before another body, that body must send the parties to a regular court.

    4. **Legal status only by a regular court (s 8).** Disputes on establishing a relationship, divorce or legal status are decided only by a regular court.

    5. **Equality of parties (s 9).** Parties have equal status and the court must treat them equally. Governments and legal persons cannot claim special status, privilege or immunity in the proceedings, unless law or the court allows.

    6. **Locus standi (s 10).** A plaintiff must show in the plaint his right to the claim, or a lawful interest or concern in it. Section 91 lets anyone file, with the court's permission, in a public interest dispute.

    7. **No vacuum of jurisdiction (s 11).** Law assigns every case to some court or authority. No dispute is left without a forum.

    8. **Duty to decide (s 12).** A judge cannot refuse to decide because the law is inadequate or unclear. He must decide on the recognised principles of law and justice, as Article 126(1) of the Constitution also requires.

    9. **Compromise and mediation (s 13).** Before judgment, the court must give the parties a chance to settle by compromise (milapatra) or through mediation; a compromise may be made at any level of the case.

    10. **Res judicata (s 14).** A case on the same subject between the same plaintiff and defendant is not heard and decided more than once, except on appeal or where a compromise has not been carried out. Section 94 adds that a plaint on a decided matter is dismissed.

    11. **Right to appeal (s 15).** A dissatisfied party has the right to appeal, under law, against a judgment or final order of the first court.

    **Other principles in the Code and the Constitution**

    12. **Natural justice.** Parties must be present at the hearing (s 269), and a judge must not hear a case in which he or his relatives have an interest, or in which he acted before (s 271). Article 20(8)-(9) of the Constitution guarantees notice and a fair trial.

    13. **Limitation (ss 47-62, s 90).** A plaint must be filed within the period fixed by law; a late plaint is not registered.

    14. **Reasoned and timely decision (ss 197-198).** The court decides normally on the day the hearing ends, records its grounds, and prepares the full judgment within 21 working days.

    15. **Special law prevails (s 3).** Where a special law sets its own procedure, it applies; the Code fills the gaps.

    16. **Binding precedent.** Courts follow the Supreme Court's interpretations and principles (Constitution Art 128(4)).

    **Importance of these principles**

    - They make civil justice **fair**: equality, hearing, no bias.
    - They make it **orderly**: jurisdiction, standing, limitation.
    - They make it **final**: res judicata, limitation.
    - They make it **accessible and efficient**: open court, compromise, time limits.
    - They make it **correctable**: appeal.

    **Critical view**

    Collecting the principles in one chapter is a real improvement on the scattered rules of the old Muluki Ain. But some weaknesses remain. The words "shall generally follow" in s 4 leave room to depart from them. Some core principles, such as the right to be heard and the rule against bias, sit outside Chapter 2, so the chapter is not complete. In practice, delays, frequent adjournments and the cost of lawyers still prevent many people from enjoying these principles. Section 13 can be misused to buy time. The principles must be backed by enough judges, staff and technology to work.

    **Conclusion**

    The general principles of civil procedural law, mainly in ss 4-15 of the Muluki Civil Procedure Code 2074, ensure that civil cases are heard openly by a competent and impartial court, between equal parties with standing, within time, decided once with reasons, and open to appeal. They turn the constitutional promise of a fair trial into the daily practice of the civil courts.

??? example "Q5. What is res judicata? Explain it on the basis of the constitutional and legal provisions of Nepal. (old course 2078 Q3, 20 marks)"
    **Introduction**

    A lawsuit must end somewhere. If a losing party could bring the same case again and again, no judgment would be final and the winning party would never be at peace. The doctrine of res judicata (प्राङ्न्याय) prevents this. Res judicata means "a matter already judged". Once a competent court has finally decided a matter between parties, the same parties cannot litigate the same matter again.

    **Basis: two maxims**

    1. **Nemo debet bis vexari pro una et eadem causa:** no one should be troubled twice for the same cause. This protects the individual.
    2. **Interest reipublicae ut sit finis litium:** it is in the public interest that litigation should end. This protects the courts and society.

    A third reason is respect for judgments: if the same matter could be decided twice, two courts might give opposite results.

    **Essentials**

    1. **Same matter in issue.** The matter in the second case was directly and substantially in issue in the first.
    2. **Same parties** or those claiming through them.
    3. **Same title** (capacity) in which they litigated.
    4. **Competent court.** The first court had jurisdiction.
    5. **Heard and finally decided** on the merits, and the decision is final.

    **Kinds**

    - **Actual res judicata:** the matter was actually raised and decided.
    - **Constructive res judicata:** a matter that could and should have been raised in the first case is treated as decided.
    - **Criminal side:** the rule against double jeopardy; no one is tried and punished twice for the same offence.

    **Constitutional provisions in Nepal**

    - **Art 20(6), Constitution of Nepal 2015:** no person shall be tried and punished for the same offence in a court of law more than once. This is a fundamental right, enforceable under Arts 46, 133 and 144.
    - **Art 126(2):** all shall abide by the orders and decisions of courts. This supports the finality of judgments.

    **Legal provisions**

    - **Muluki Civil Procedure Code 2074, s 14:** once a court has decided a case, it shall not hear and decide more than once a case on the same subject between the same plaintiff and defendant, except on appeal or in a matter where a compromise has not been carried out. This section is in Chapter 2, among the general principles of civil procedure.
    - **s 94:** once a matter is decided, the same party cannot file a plaint on the same subject against the same person without appealing. Such a plaint is dismissed, and a judgment on it is void.
    - **s 87(2):** a person who could have included all claims in one plaint but waits for the result of the first case and then sues again cannot proceed. This is constructive res judicata.
    - **Muluki Criminal Code 2074, s 9:** no person shall be prosecuted and punished in court more than once for the same offence.
    - **Administration of Justice Act 2073, s 11:** a court does not review its own final judgment. Only the Supreme Court may review, on narrow grounds such as new evidence or conflict with precedent (s 11(2)), and only once (s 11(3)(क)).

    **Exceptions and limits**

    - **Appeal, review and revision** are not barred; they are part of the same case (Code s 14; AJA ss 11-12).
    - **A compromise not carried out** may be litigated again (s 14).
    - **Different parties or different title:** the bar does not apply.
    - **A judgment without jurisdiction** does not bar a fresh case.
    - **Fraud or collusion** in obtaining the first judgment is a ground for challenge.

    **Cases**

    - In *Padam Bahadur Karki v Man Kumari Basnet* (NKP 2061), the seller's wife and sons had lost their own case about a deed. That result did not bind his father, mother and brothers, whose position in law was different. This fits the "same parties" requirement, though the judgment does not use the term res judicata.
    - In *Kallu Chaudhary v Appellate Court, Dipayal* (NKP 2060), the petitioner lost his appeal and then attacked the same decision by writ. The Court held that it is not our judicial tradition to review the same decision again and again, and the principle of judicial certainty does not let a party who failed by one route take another.

    **Res judicata distinguished**

    - **From stare decisis:** res judicata binds the parties to the decision; stare decisis binds courts to the legal principle.
    - **From double jeopardy:** double jeopardy is the criminal form, and protects the accused from repeated prosecution.

    **Critical view**

    Res judicata is essential for finality, saves court time and protects people from harassment. Nepal has given it both constitutional (Art 20(6)) and statutory (Code ss 14, 94, 87(2)) force. But applied too rigidly it can protect a wrong judgment, for example one obtained by fraud. The law balances this with appeal, review and revision. In practice, parties still try to reopen decided matters under new names or through writs; the courts' insistence on judicial certainty, as in Kallu Chaudhary, is the answer.

    **Conclusion**

    Res judicata means that a matter finally decided between the same parties by a competent court cannot be tried again. In Nepal it is a constitutional right in criminal matters (Art 20(6)) and a general principle of civil procedure (Code s 14), supported by ss 94 and 87(2), the Criminal Code s 9 and the limits on review in the Administration of Justice Act. It serves both the individual and the public: no one should be vexed twice, and every dispute must end.

??? example "Q6. Explain the ratio enunciated in Advocate Radheshyam Adhikari v HMG, Council of Ministers Secretariat and others, NKP 2048, p. 810. (old course 2075 Q5, 20 marks)"
    **Introduction**

    *Advocate Radheshyam Adhikari v His Majesty's Government, Council of Ministers Secretariat, et al.* (NKP 2048, No. 12, p. 810, D.N. 4430) was decided by a Special Bench of the Supreme Court on 2048/12/17. The Chief Justice, Bishwanath Upadhyay, wrote the order, and Justices Surendra Prasad Singh and Gajendra Keshari Bastola agreed. The Court said it was the first case to be decided under the public interest clause of Article 88(2) of the Constitution of the Kingdom of Nepal 2047. It is known for defining a "dispute of public interest" and for its test of locus standi (हकदैया) in such disputes.

    **Facts**

    - On 2047/8/27 the King appointed Kalyan Bikram Adhikari, ambassador to France, as ambassador to Spain and Israel too. The Gorkhapatra notice did not say this was on the advice and consent of the Council of Ministers.
    - The weekly *Matribhumi* quoted the then Law and Justice Minister as saying the appointment did not follow the Constitution.
    - Article 35(2) required all acts of the King to be done on the Council of Ministers' advice and consent, unless the Constitution provided otherwise.
    - The petitioner, an advocate, filed a writ under Arts 23 and 88(2), calling it a dispute of public right or interest. He asked the Court to declare the appointment invalid.
    - The government replied that Art 35(6) barred courts from asking whether advice was given, Art 41(2) barred questions about its business rules, and a minister's remark to a newspaper was not official.

    **Issues**

    1. What is a dispute of public right or interest under Art 88(2), and is this one?
    2. Who has locus standi to bring such a dispute, and does the petitioner?
    3. Should the order sought be issued?

    **Decision**

    The writ was dismissed. The appointment did need the Council of Ministers' advice under Art 35(2). But the claim that no advice was given rested only on a minister's unauthorised newspaper remark. Art 35(6) barred the Court from inquiring into the advice. So the dispute was not one the Court could decide.

    **Ratio and principles**

    **1. Meaning of public interest dispute.** A dispute of public right or interest is one about the collective right or interest of the general public or of a community, not the personal right of particular persons. Whether a dispute is public depends on this, not on how many people take an active interest (headnote point 1; paragraph 9).

    **2. Limits of public interest.** "Right" means a right given by law or the Constitution; "interest" is wider, covering matters such as security, governance, social peace, education, health and the economy. But the dispute must be based on law or the Constitution, must be real and not hypothetical, and must be capable of judicial decision. Intellectual curiosity, publicity or testing the Court's patience are not proper purposes (paragraphs 10 and 11).

    **3. Locus standi widened but not abolished** (paragraphs 12 and 13). Ordinarily, only a person whose own right is harmed can invoke the extraordinary jurisdiction, habeas corpus apart. Art 88(2) widened the Court's jurisdiction, so standing widens too: the petitioner's personal right need not be harmed. But not everyone may bring every public dispute. The petitioner must show a **meaningful relation or substantial interest** in the subject, and must satisfy the Court that he can properly represent the public concerned. General interest or curiosity is not enough. Art 88(1) removed all standing limits for challenging laws; Art 88(2) did not go so far.

    **4. Application.** The petitioner's interest looked like general curiosity, but the government raised no objection and the constitutional questions were important. So the Court did not decide the case on standing (paragraph 14). Strictly, the standing test is therefore a considered statement, not the ground of decision; the headnote does not list it.

    **5. Advice to the King** (headnote point 2). Art 120(1) did not make ambassadors' appointments a matter for the King alone, so the Council of Ministers' advice under Art 35(2) was needed.

    **6. Right to information** (headnote point 3). The Art 16 right to information is not unlimited.

    **7. Justiciability** (headnote point 4). A dispute presumed from a minister's unauthorised remark is not one the Court can decide, because Art 35(6) bars inquiry into the advice given to the King.

    **Significance**

    - It was the first major statement of public interest litigation under the 1990 Constitution.
    - Its "meaningful relation and substantial interest" test is now written into the Supreme Court Rules 2074, r 32(1)(ख) and r 40(2)(ज).
    - Article 133(2) of the Constitution of Nepal 2015 keeps the public interest jurisdiction.

    **Critical view**

    The case opened the door to public interest litigation while guarding against its abuse. That balance has served Nepal well. But the test is vague: "meaningful relation" can be applied strictly or loosely by different benches. And the Court's choice not to decide standing makes it hard to say what the binding ratio on standing is. Its main holdings on the King's advice are no longer relevant after the end of the monarchy.

    **Conclusion**

    Radheshyam Adhikari defined a dispute of public interest as one about the collective right or interest of the public, rooted in law and capable of judicial decision. It held that standing in such disputes is wider than in private disputes, but requires a meaningful relation or substantial interest and the ability to represent the public. Though the writ failed, these principles remain the foundation of public interest standing in Nepal.
