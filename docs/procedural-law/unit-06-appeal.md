# Unit 6: Appellate Proceedings

!!! abstract "In one line"
    How a party who loses in a lower court asks a higher court to correct the judgment: filing the appeal, the first hearing on the appellant's side alone, calling and hearing the other side, the respondent's written reply, the final hearing and judgment, and the special routes to the Supreme Court that need its permission first.

## Why it matters for the exam

- **Asked as a full 20-mark question:** 2080 Q6. "Unsatisfied party can appeal to higher court. If a party to a case files an appeal when the other party of the case has not obtained the copy of the judgement and also has not been issued notice of appeal, and if the court gives an ex parte judgement, will the judgement be legitimate or not? Explain in reference to the procedure set in the case of Shivanath Shah Kanu v Bishwonath Shah Kanu."
- **Asked as a short note:** 2080 Q7(e) "rejoinder".
- Two prescribed cases belong here: [Shivanath Shah Kanu v Bishwanath Shah Kanu](cases/9-10-shivanath-shah-kanu.md) (9.10) and [Pradip Kumar Prasad Shah Kalwar v Ram Bhajan Raya Yadav](cases/9-9-pradip-kumar-prasad-shah-kalwar.md) (9.9). The case question comes every year, so a Unit 6 question can arrive through either case.
- Learn the civil procedure (Muluki Civil Procedure Code 2074, Chapter 19, ss 205-218) in order, step by step. Then learn how the criminal Code (ss 134-147) differs. Three full answers are written out below.

## Start here: a second look by a higher court

Think of a school exam. A teacher marks your paper, and you think a question was marked wrongly. You ask for a re-check by the head of department. The head does not set a new exam. The head looks at your answer sheet and the marking, and decides whether the mark was right.

An appeal works the same way.

- A **District Court** decides a case. One side loses, or wins only part of what it asked for.
- That side, now called the **appellant**, files an appeal in the **High Court** within the time the law allows.
- The High Court gets the **case file** from the District Court. It first hears the appellant alone. If the judgment looks right, it can uphold it at once.
- If the judgment may change, the High Court **calls the other side**, now called the **respondent**. The respondent may file a written reply.
- The High Court then hears both sides and gives its **judgment**: it upholds, reverses, or changes the lower decision.
- In some serious cases, a further appeal lies to the **Supreme Court**. In others, the Supreme Court can only look again if it first gives **permission** (revision).

The two prescribed cases show what goes wrong when these steps are skipped. In *Shivanath*, an appeal court decided one brother's appeal before the other brother had even been sent his notice of time to appeal. In *Pradip*, a party said he never got his notice, but the court found it had been served the way the law requires.

## 6.1 Filing of appeal

### Meaning of appeal

An **appeal** is a proceeding in which a higher court re-examines the decision of a lower court, on facts, law or both, at the request of a party who is not satisfied. The Code defines the appeal petition (पुनरावेदनपत्र) as the application a party gives to a higher court, with his arguments, to correct a lower court's decision he is not satisfied with (Civil Procedure Code s 2(च)).

Standard textbook points about appeals:

1. **Appeal is a creature of statute.** No one has an inborn right to appeal. The right exists only where a law gives it, and only within the limits that law sets.
2. **It is a right, not a favour, where the law gives it.** In Nepal, s 15 of the Civil Procedure Code says a party not satisfied with a judgment or final order of a first-instance court has the right to appeal to the appellate court "according to law" (कानून बमोजिम).
3. **It continues the same case.** The appeal court looks at the same dispute and the same record. It can take more evidence where the law allows, but it does not start a fresh case.
4. **It goes up one level.** An appeal lies to the next higher court named by law. Section 27 of the Code gives the appellate court jurisdiction over orders and judgments of the court below it.
5. **It must be filed in time and in form.** A late or defective appeal is not registered.

!!! info "Appeal, revision and review"
    These three are often confused. An **appeal** is a right where the law gives it. **Revision** (मुद्दा दोहोर्‍याउने) is the Supreme Court looking again at a High Court judgment where no appeal lies, and only after it first gives permission. **Review** (पुनरावलोकन) is the Supreme Court looking again at its own judgment, on narrow grounds, also after permission. Revision and review are covered fully in Unit 7. They come up here under 6.6.

### Which court hears the appeal

Nepal has three tiers of courts. The Constitution and the Administration of Justice Act 2073 decide where an appeal goes.

| From | To | Law |
|---|---|---|
| Quasi-judicial body or official under federal law; judicial body of a local level | District Court | Administration of Justice Act 2073 s 7(4) |
| District Court (first-instance cases) | High Court | Act s 8(3)(क); Constitution Art 144(3) |
| District Court deciding on appeal, in some cases: where it quashed a judicial or quasi-judicial body's decision in whole or part; or where the case involves government or public property, prison, a fine over Rs 1 lakh, or a claim over Rs 5 lakh | High Court | Act s 8(3)(ख)-(ग) |
| Judicial bodies within the province, unless federal law says otherwise | High Court | Act s 8(3)(घ) |
| High Court, in the cases listed in s 9 (below) | Supreme Court | Act s 9; Constitution Art 133(4)-(5) |

**Appeal to the Supreme Court (Administration of Justice Act 2073 s 9(1)).** The Supreme Court hears appeals only in these cases:

- (क) cases a High Court decided at first instance under s 8(2);
- (ख) cases where a High Court's judgment imposes imprisonment of ten years or more;
- (ग) cases involving more than three years' prison, a fine over Rs 5 lakh, or a claim over Rs 25 lakh, where the High Court, on appeal, set aside the lower decision in whole or in part;
- (घ) other cases where a federal law gives an appeal to the Supreme Court.

Section 9(2) adds cases sent to the Supreme Court for confirmation (साधक). So a High Court judgment that simply **upholds** the District Court, in an ordinary case, is not appealable to the Supreme Court. The only route left is revision, which needs permission (6.6).

!!! tip "Exam tip"
    In *Shivanath*, the brief records that the appeal to the Supreme Court lay under s 9(1)(c) of the old Administration of Justice Act 2048, because the Appellate Court had partly reversed the District Court. Today the matching rule is s 9(1)(ग) of the 2073 Act, which also has money and prison thresholds.

### When no appeal lies

Section 206 of the Civil Procedure Code says no appeal can be filed in these cases, whatever s 205 says:

- (क) the defendant let the notice (myad) lapse, after it was lawfully received or served, without filing a written defence (pratiuttar) or contesting;
- (ख) the parties settled the case by mediation or compromise;
- (ग) the claim was withdrawn;
- (घ) the plaint was dismissed (डिसमिस). But if the plaint was dismissed improperly, an appeal can be filed.

Rule (क) is important for ex parte judgments. A defendant who was properly served but stayed away cannot later appeal. But a defendant who lost ex parte because the notice was **not properly served** has a special route under s 118 (see 6.2).

### Time limit: civil cases

The time to appeal is the heart of both prescribed cases. Read ss 200 and 205 of the Civil Procedure Code together.

**Section 200: telling the parties of the judgment.**

- If a party is present when judgment is given, the court reads it out and has him sign a paper saying he heard it (फैसला सुनिपाएको कागज) (s 200(1)). If he is absent but his agent (वारिस) is present, the agent signs (s 200(2)).
- If a party is absent, the deciding court must send him a notice within **three days** of the judgment being prepared. It uses the form in Schedule 20 and follows the service procedure of Chapter 8. It may serve it itself or through a lower court. If an appeal lies, the notice gives the judgment **with the time to appeal** (पुनरावेदनको म्याद). If no appeal lies, it gives notice of the judgment only (s 200(3)).
- A party counts as present if he, his agent, or his lawyer who argued the case was present (s 200(4)).

**Section 205: the thirty days.**

- A party not satisfied with a first-instance judgment may appeal within **30 days** of learning of the judgment, unless the Code says otherwise (s 205(1)).
- The 30 days run from the day he signed the paper saying he heard the judgment. If he was absent and a notice was issued, they run from the day the notice was **served** (s 205(2)).
- If the party or his agent was present on the date fixed for hearing, and judgment was given that day, he is treated as knowing of it (s 205(3)). The explanation to s 205 says the day of knowing is counted from the day the judge authenticated (signed) the judgment under s 198.
- If no paper was signed and no notice was served, the party may appeal within 30 days of taking a copy of the judgment. The copy must be taken within six months of the judgment being authenticated (s 205(4)).

Failure to serve notice prevents the ordinary service-based appeal period from starting. But today s 205(4) also requires a copy within six months of authentication, followed by an appeal within 30 days of that copy. Apply those limits separately from Shivanath, which was decided under the old law.

**Lapsed time.** Section 223 is the Code's general rule on a lapsed time limit (म्याद). A party kept away by circumstances beyond his control may have it revived once, by up to 15 days. In *Pradip*, by contrast, the party first complained about the notice more than a year and a half after it was served.

### Time limit: criminal cases

The Muluki Criminal Procedure Code 2074 has its own rules.

- **Time.** A party not satisfied with a judgment or final order may appeal within **70 days** of learning of it in a criminal case where the Government of Nepal is the plaintiff, and within **30 days** in other cases (s 134(1)).
- **Extension.** If the party shows why he could not appeal in time, and the reason is reasonable, the appellate court may extend the time by up to 30 days (proviso to s 134(1)).
- **Deemed knowledge.** A party is treated as knowing of the judgment if he, his agent, or his lawyer who argued was present at the hearing and judgment was given that day (s 134(3)). For the government side, time runs from when the government attorney's office receives the information under s 132(4) (proviso to s 134(3)). After one year from the judgment, every party is treated as knowing of it (s 134(4)).
- **Notice of judgment.** The court must send a notice of judgment (Schedule 44) to an accused who was not part of the case from the start, an accused in custody, and an accused tried ex parte (s 132(1)). It is served like a summons. If it cannot be served, it is published in a national daily owned by the Government (s 132(2)).
- **The judgment must say whether an appeal lies, and to which court** (s 131(3)(च)).

### Form, fee and documents

**Civil (Code ss 73, 207, 208).**

- The appeal is written in the form in Schedule 21, in polite language (शिष्ट भाषा), with the documents that support it (s 208(1)-(2)).
- An appeal fee (पुनरावेदन दस्तुर) of Rs 300 is charged (s 208(3)).
- Proof of paying the court fee (अदालती शुल्क) must go with the appeal (s 207(1)). Where the claim has a value, the fee is 15 percent of the plaint fee, to the extent the appellant is dissatisfied (s 73(1)). In a partition case with no stated value, it is the same as the plaint fee (s 73(2)). If the court had allowed the fee to be paid later, the appeal must be registered even without proof of payment (s 207(2)).
- The Civil Procedure Rules 2075 r 44 list what the appeal must state. These include the date of judgment and of knowing it, the points of dissatisfaction and their legal basis, the time to appeal, and **whether the other party has also appealed**, if known (r 44(ज)).

**Criminal (Code ss 136-137).**

- The appeal follows the form in Schedule 45. It gives the parties' names and addresses, a short account of the case, the points of dissatisfaction and why, what the appeal court should decide, and the law relied on (s 136(1)-(2)).
- It attaches a copy of the judgment and proof that the appellant has served or paid any sentence, or is in custody, or has given bail or a deposit (s 136(3)).
- **Custody for some appellants.** Some convicted persons must be in custody to appeal: those sentenced to life imprisonment, to more than ten years, and some others listed in s 137(1). The appellate court may give **leave to appeal without going into custody** (थुनामा नबसी पुनरावेदन दिने अनुमति), with a deposit or guarantee, in the cases in s 137(2). Examples are a person over 75, or one too ill to stay in custody. No one may be kept in prison longer than his sentence just because he appealed (s 137(8)(ख)).

### Where the appeal is filed and registered

**Civil.**

- The appeal is registered in the appellate court of the court whose judgment is challenged (s 209(1)).
- The court checks three things: was it filed within the s 205 time, was the fee paid, and is it in proper form. If so, it registers the appeal and gives a receipt (Schedule 3) (s 209(2)).
- If the appeal is not in proper form, the court does not register it. It writes a refusal endorsement (दरपीठ) giving the reason (s 209(3)).
- An appeal may also be filed **through the court that decided the case**. That court follows the s 209 checks, registers it, and sends it with the file to the appellate court within three days (s 210).
- A government employee who cannot attend because of official work may file through the court where he is posted (s 211).
- If a defect can be cured, the Rules allow the appeal to be registered on deposit (धरौटीमा दर्ता) on condition that the defect is cured within seven days, excluding travel time (Civil Procedure Rules 2075 r 45(2)).

**Criminal.** The appeal is filed in the appellate court (s 134(2)). It may also be filed through the deciding court, or through the prison where a convicted appellant is held. These send it on as soon as possible, with the file (s 135).

**Supreme Court.** The Supreme Court Rules 2074 r 60 set out the registration of an appeal under s 9 of the Administration of Justice Act. The officer registers it if it states what it must, is within time, attaches the needed documents and has paid the fee. Rule 61 allows filing through the deciding court or the prison.

### Calling for the case file

The appellate court cannot judge without the record. Under the civil Code, it writes to the lower court for the case file (मिसिल) within three days of registering the appeal. The lower court sends it within three days of getting the letter (s 212). The criminal Code is the same: the appellate court orders the file as soon as possible, and the lower court sends it within three days (s 138).

The Rules add a safeguard that matters for *Shivanath*. When the lower court sends the file, it must attach the paper showing the party heard the judgment, or the served notice of time to appeal, or the notice of judgment (Civil Procedure Rules r 47). And the file must not go to the records room until the appeal notice under s 200(3) has been sent and the proof attached (r 41).

### Effect of an appeal on execution

A judgment is not carried out while the time to appeal is running. Under s 231 of the Civil Procedure Code, the court must stay execution:

- (क) until the time to appeal has run out, where an appeal lies; and
- (ख) where an appeal has been filed, until the appeal is finally decided.

Execution itself is Unit 8. *Pradip* shows the other side: once the notice was properly served and the time passed, the judgment became final and recovery went ahead.

## 6.2 Ex parte hearing

### Two meanings

Ex parte means "from one side". In appellate proceedings, the phrase covers two different hearings.

1. **The first hearing on the appellant's side alone.** The appeal court first hears only the appellant. It decides whether the lower judgment may change. If not, it can uphold the judgment without ever calling the respondent.
2. **The hearing after the respondent fails to appear.** The respondent has been called and served, but does not come. The court hears the appeal anyway.

There is also the **ex parte judgment of the first-instance court**, where the defendant never answered the plaint. The appeal is then the defendant's remedy, as explained below.

### The first hearing on the appellant's side alone

**Civil: Code s 213.**

- Within 15 days of receiving the file, the appellate court holds a hearing on whether, as the appellant asks, the respondent must be called to the hearing (s 213(1)).
- If the lower court's decision does not appear likely to change, the appellate court decides as it finds (s 213(2)). In effect this means the appeal fails and the lower judgment stands.
- If the lower decision may change, the appellate court records its reasons and orders the respondent to be called for the hearing (s 213(3)).

**Criminal: Code s 140.**

- The appellate court must finish hearing the appeal within six months of receiving the file (s 140(1)).
- If the lower decision is reasonable and no material difference appears, the appellate court may reject the appeal, giving reasons (s 140(2)).
- If the decision may change, it records why and orders the respondent to be called (s 140(3)).

**High Court Rules 2073.**

- An order calling the respondent under Civil Procedure Code s 213 or Criminal Procedure Code s 140 must state its reasons (r 112).
- Where the appeal court upholds the trial judgment without calling the respondent, because the trial court's grounds look reasonable, the bench may write a short judgment in the form in Schedule 7 (r 114(1)). Schedule 7 records the appellant's main grounds and then says the lower judgment is upheld.
- When the final judgment is written after the respondent was called, it must say why, if the bench disagrees with the points in the order that called him (r 111(झ)).

**Why this step exists.** Appeal courts receive many appeals that have no real chance. Calling every respondent would waste his time and money and delay every case. The first hearing filters these out. And it does not harm the respondent: if the court rules for him without hearing him, he has lost nothing.

!!! note "Is this against natural justice?"
    The rule of natural justice audi alteram partem ("hear the other side") protects a party who may be **harmed** by a decision. At the first hearing, the court can only uphold the judgment the respondent already won. It cannot reverse it without calling him (s 213(3), criminal s 140(3)). So the person not heard is never the person harmed. The danger arises when the "respondent" is a party with his **own** grievance against the same judgment, as in *Shivanath*.

### The hearing when the respondent does not appear

**Civil.** Once the notice under s 214 has been served, the appellate court must hear the appeal even if the respondent does not appear (s 215(2)). It hears the appeal on the fixed date even if one or both parties are not on tarikh (s 215(1)). Section 143 also allows an appeal-stage case to proceed without keeping the parties on tarikh, unless the court orders otherwise.

**Criminal.** Section 141 is headed "ex parte hearing of the appeal if the respondent is absent" (प्रत्यर्थी अनुपस्थित भएमा पुनरावेदनको एकतर्फी सुनुवाई हुने).

- If the respondent does not appear within the time in the notice, the appellate court fixes a date at least 15 days later. On that date it may hear and decide the appeal (s 141(1)).
- But if the respondent shows a reasonable cause for not appearing, and applies before that date, the court may extend his time. It then orders him to appear on the fixed date and hears him too (s 141(2)).

The key condition in both Codes is **proper service**. An ex parte hearing is fair only because the respondent was given a real chance to come and chose not to. *Pradip* shows the court's approach: a notice served the way the law requires counts, and a party who says otherwise must prove which step was missed.

### Ex parte judgment at first instance: the appeal "as a defence"

Suppose the District Court decided a civil case ex parte because the defendant never filed a written defence. If he was properly served, s 206(क) bars his appeal. But if the notice was served improperly, s 118 of the Civil Procedure Code gives him a special appeal.

- A defendant may say that, because the notice was served improperly, he could not defend and an ex parte judgment was given against him. He may file an **appeal that counts as a written defence** (प्रतिउत्तरपत्र सरहको पुनरावेदन) in the appellate court. He must file it within 35 days of learning of the judgment, if he learns within six months of it (s 118(1)).
- Before hearing it, the appellate court decides whether the notice was properly served (s 118(2)).
- If it finds the notice was not properly served, it quashes the ex parte judgment. It treats the appeal itself as the written defence and sends the case back to the lower court for a fresh hearing (s 118(3)).
- The lower court then serves a seven-day notice on the plaintiff, excluding travel time, and proceeds by law (s 118(4)-(5)).

This connects with *Pradip*. There, the party's complaint was about the notice of time to appeal, not the first notice, and the court held it had been served properly.

## 6.3 Notice to respondent

### Why notice matters

A court may not decide against a person it has not heard. The Constitution of Nepal 2015 protects every person's right to be informed of proceedings taken against him (Art 20(8)) and to a fair trial by an independent, impartial and competent court (Art 20(9)). In an appeal, this right is met by a **notice** (myad) to the respondent, served in the way the law requires.

There are really two notices in appellate proceedings:

1. **Before the appeal:** the notice of judgment and of time to appeal, sent to a party who was absent at judgment (Civil Procedure Code s 200(3); Criminal Procedure Code s 132). This tells the losing party he can appeal, and starts his time.
2. **During the appeal:** the notice calling the respondent to the appeal hearing, once the court orders him to be called (Civil Procedure Code s 214; Criminal Procedure Code s 140(4)).

*Shivanath* is about the first notice. Its lesson also governs the second: an appeal court must know who has a live right to appeal before it decides.

### Civil: Code s 214

- Once the court orders the respondent to be called under s 213(3), it must serve a notice in his name to appear within **15 days, not counting travel time** (बाटोको म्याद). The notice follows the form in Schedule 22 (s 214(1)).
- The appellate court may serve the notice itself or through the court that decided the case at first instance, following the procedure of Chapter 8 (s 214(2)).
- After service, the respondent may file a written defence in the appellate court if he wishes (s 214(3)).
- If he wishes, the court keeps him on tarikh and proceeds with the case (s 214(4)).
- The notice must go with a copy of the order calling him and a copy of the appeal (Civil Procedure Rules 2075 r 49).

**How service works (Chapter 8).** Chapter 8 of the Code (ss 99-118) sets out how a notice is served. The [Pradip brief](cases/9-9-pradip-kumar-prasad-shah-kalwar.md#then-and-now) summarises s 105: the server hands the notice to the person, or at his home to an adult of his household, before local witnesses. If no one is found or they refuse it, he pastes it on the door before the same witnesses. A court officer must check every service (s 110). A notice not served properly can be cancelled and served again (ss 111, 117). Unit 4A covers this in full.

### Criminal: Code ss 140 and 143

- When the court orders the respondent to be called, it issues a notice in his name, with a copy of the appeal, to appear within **15 days, not counting travel time** (s 140(4)).
- The appellate court may serve it itself or through the trial court, following the Code's procedure (s 140(4क)).
- Notices and orders to the appellant or respondent are served like a summons under Chapter 6 (s 143).
- The Criminal Procedure Rules 2075 r 75(1) add that the notice goes with a copy of the order, in the form in Schedule 23 of the Rules.

### Supreme Court: Rules r 67

When the Supreme Court orders the respondent in an appeal to be called, it serves a notice to appear within **15 days, not counting travel time**, in the form in Schedule 9 (Supreme Court Rules 2074 r 67(1)). The same applies after permission is given in a review, revision or leave-to-appeal application. A copy of the petition and the order goes with it, to every respondent (r 67(2)-(3)).

### The notice of time to appeal: protecting the other party

What if one party appeals while the other has not yet been told of the judgment? The law now has several safeguards.

- The deciding court must send the absent party the notice of judgment and time to appeal within three days of preparing the judgment (Civil Procedure Code s 200(3)).
- The court's registry must issue that notice and attach proof before sending the file to the records room (Civil Procedure Rules r 41).
- The file sent to the appellate court must carry the record of the party hearing the judgment, or the served notice (Civil Procedure Rules r 47).
- The appeal itself must state whether the other party has appealed, if known (Civil Procedure Rules r 44(ज); Criminal Procedure Rules r 71(ज)).
- **Supreme Court Rules r 61(4).** Where an appeal to the Supreme Court is filed through the deciding court, and the case has several plaintiffs or defendants, some may have appealed while others still need to be sent their notice of time to appeal. The deciding court must then issue notices to the rest. It sends the file, with all the service records, within seven days of getting them back.
- **No decision while time is running, in a sadhak.** In a case sent for confirmation, the Supreme Court must not decide the sadhak while a party's time to appeal is still running (Supreme Court Rules r 66(7)). If a party was never sent the notice, or it was sent improperly, and he appeals after the sadhak is decided, the appeal is registered and heard by a Full Bench (r 66(10); r 23(2)(छ)).

### The cases

**[Shivanath Shah Kanu v Bishwanath Shah Kanu](cases/9-10-shivanath-shah-kanu.md)** (NKP 2069, No. 5, D.N. 8820, Full Bench). Both brothers appealed against one District Court judgment. The Appellate Court, Hetauda decided Bishwanath's appeal on 2058/3/25. It did so before Shivanath had been sent his notice of time to appeal, and without calling him under Adalati Bandobasta No. 202. Later it heard Shivanath's appeal with both sides present and partly reversed the District Court. The Full Bench held that the first judgment could not narrow, restrict or cancel Shivanath's right to appeal. One court cannot give two judgments in one dispute. Where it does so by mistake, the later judgment, given after hearing both sides and weighing the evidence, is the authoritative one.

**[Pradip Kumar Prasad Shah Kalwar v Ram Bhajan Raya Yadav](cases/9-9-pradip-kumar-prasad-shah-kalwar.md)** (NKP 2073, No. 10, D.N. 9690, Joint Bench). The notice of time to appeal was pasted on Pradip's door before two witnesses, after his household refused it, and the VDC secretary signed. Pradip did not appeal. Long after, he said he never knew of the notice. The Supreme Court held that the notice had been served by the rules (old Adalati Bandobasta No. 110). The burden was on Pradip to show which step was missed and that he did not learn of it. The law does not mean that a properly served notice must be cancelled without reason to give a fresh chance.

!!! tip "Read the two cases together"
    The case briefs draw the line this way. In *Shivanath*, the notice of time to appeal had **not even been issued**, so the right to appeal survived. In *Pradip*, the notice had been **properly served**, so the right ended when the time ran out. The question in each case is whether the party's time to appeal had properly started.

## 6.4 Written statements of respondents (rejoinder)

### Meaning

In this syllabus, a **rejoinder** is the respondent's written reply to an appeal or to a writ petition. It answers the appellant's grounds, defends the lower judgment, and raises any points of law or fact in the respondent's favour.

The word comes from old common-law pleading. There, the plaintiff's declaration was met by the defendant's plea, the plaintiff's replication, and then the defendant's **rejoinder**. In Nepal, the word is used more loosely for the reply of the party on the other side.

The Nepali laws use two names:

- **लिखित प्रतिवाद (written defence)** for the respondent's reply in an **appeal**: Civil Procedure Code s 214(3), Criminal Procedure Code s 140(5), Civil Procedure Rules r 50, Supreme Court Rules r 68.
- **लिखित जवाफ (written reply)** for the respondent's reply in a **writ petition**: for example, High Court Rules r 11(ख) speaks of an order asking the respondent for a written reply. Unit 7 covers writs.

### When and how it is filed

**Civil.** Once the notice under s 214 is served, the respondent "may, if he wishes," file a written defence in the appellate court (s 214(3)). The Civil Procedure Rules r 50 add that a respondent called under s 213 may contest in writing both the appellant's grounds (पुनरावेदन जिकिर) and the court's order calling him, and put his own case.

**Criminal.** A person who receives the notice under s 140(4) may file a written defence within the time the court sets, rebutting the grounds of appeal (s 140(5)). It follows the form in Schedule 46 (s 140(6)).

**Supreme Court.** A respondent who receives the notice under r 67, or who was called under Civil Procedure Code s 213(3) or Criminal Procedure Code s 140(3), may appear and contest in writing. He may contest the appeal, a review, revision or leave-to-appeal application, and the court's order, paying the fee in Schedule 3 (Supreme Court Rules r 68). A party who wants to submit evidence must get the bench's permission, and the bench may accept or refuse it (r 69).

### It is optional, but wise

The Codes say the respondent "may" file a written defence. Not filing it does not stop the hearing: the court must hear the appeal even if the respondent does not appear (Civil Procedure Code s 215(2); Criminal Procedure Code s 141). But a respondent who stays silent leaves the appellant's grounds unanswered. The court has already found, by calling him, that the decision may change.

### What a good rejoinder contains

1. The case number, the parties, and the notice or order being answered.
2. A short account of the case and of the lower judgment.
3. A point-by-point answer to each ground of appeal.
4. A reply to the reasons in the order calling the respondent (Civil Procedure Rules r 50 lets him contest that order too).
5. Points of law: time limit, form, whether an appeal lies at all (s 206; Administration of Justice Act s 9), precedents.
6. The relief sought, usually that the appeal be dismissed and the lower judgment upheld.
7. The documents relied on, and the respondent's signature.

### Example: the rejoinders in Pradip

In *Pradip*, a writ case, the Supreme Court asked each respondent to explain within 15 days why the order sought should not be issued. Three written replies came in: from the Appellate Court, Hetauda; from Ram Bhajan; and from the Bara District Court and its tahasildar. The District Court's reply pointed to the 35-day execution notice and the lawyer's copy of the judgment. Ram Bhajan's reply said the notice of time to appeal was properly served on 2065/7/29 and the judgment had become final. All three asked for the petition to be dismissed. The [brief](cases/9-9-pradip-kumar-prasad-shah-kalwar.md#arguments) sets them out.

## 6.5 Final hearing and judgment

### Which bench hears the appeal

- **High Court:** appeals are heard by a two-judge bench (संयुक्त इजलास) (High Court Rules 2073 r 10(क)). If the two judges disagree, the case goes to a third judge, and the majority view is final. If there is still no majority, the file goes to the Supreme Court for decision (r 12).
- **Supreme Court:** appeals and sadhak cases are heard by a Joint Bench (Supreme Court Rules 2074 r 24(2)(क)). A case on which the two justices of a Joint Bench disagree goes to a Full Bench of three (r 23(1), r 23(2)(क)). That is how *Shivanath* reached the Full Bench.

### The hearing

**Civil (Code s 215).**

- Once the notice is served and the respondent appears, if both sides are on tarikh, the court fixes a hearing date (पेशी तारिख). Even if one or both are not on tarikh, it fixes a date and hears the appeal (s 215(1)).
- It hears the appeal even if the served respondent does not appear (s 215(2)).
- After hearing, it decides as it finds (s 215(3)). The judgment follows the form in Schedule 23 (s 215(4)).

**Criminal (Code ss 139-142).**

- After getting the file, the court fixes the hearing date (s 139). The hearing must finish within six months of receiving the file (s 140(1)). After hearing, the court decides (s 140(7)).
- Appellant and respondent need not stay on tarikh if they apply. They may still appear on the hearing date. The court may call them if it needs to clarify something or have evidence produced (s 142).

**Order of argument.** The appellant's lawyer argues first, then the respondent's. If both sides have appealed, the bench decides which lawyer argues first (High Court Rules r 87क(1), (3)).

### All appeals against one decision are heard together

Section 218 of the Civil Procedure Code is the present-day form of the *Shivanath* principle.

- Where different parties have filed separate appeals against one lower court decision in the same case, all of them must be heard together and decided as the court finds (s 218(1)).
- If for some reason one appeal has already been decided, the remaining appeals must still be decided on their merits (s 218(2)).

The Shivanath brief notes that the 2017 English translation of s 218(2) ends "as if such an appeal were not adjudged previously". The 2075 amendment struck words out of this subsection, and the current Nepali text has no such phrase.

The Full Bench in *Shivanath* put the duty this way. Where the law gives a right to appeal, the appeal court must find out whether a party's time to appeal is still running and whether he has appealed. It must then decide the matter at its level **once, all together**. Section 14 of the Code makes the same point from the side of res judicata: a court must not hear and decide the same case between the same parties more than once, except on appeal or where a compromise is not carried out.

### Powers of the appeal court

**Civil Procedure Code s 216.** When hearing an appeal, the appellate court may:

- (क) uphold (सदर) or quash (बदर) the lower court's judgment;
- (ख) reverse the lower judgment in part or in full;
- (ग) where the judgment was given without taking evidence that should have been taken, take that evidence itself or order the lower court to take it;
- (घ) where an issue that had to be decided was not addressed, decide it itself or send it to the lower court to decide;
- (ङ) make incidental or related changes to the lower judgment.

Under s 217, the appellate court may also use the powers of the trial court. Section 174 adds that, where a document's genuineness should have been tested and the lower court did not do so, the appellate court must call for the original and take the party's statement.

**Criminal Procedure Code s 144.** The powers are similar, with one more: the court may reduce, increase or change the sentence (s 144(1)(ख)). And if only one accused appealed, but the judgment should change for an accused who did not appeal, the court may give its decision for him too, giving reasons (s 144(2)).

**Administration of Justice Act 2073 s 14.** This general section gives every appellate court and confirming court the power to uphold or quash a lower decision in part or in full, and to make any judgment the lower body could make. It may also take missing evidence itself, or send the file down for it while keeping the case on its register. Where the lower body decided some questions but not others, it may send the file back with a time limit to decide the rest (s 14(1)). But District Courts and High Courts may themselves decide a question the lower body left undecided (s 14(2)).

!!! info "Deciding instead of sending back"
    In *Shivanath*, the Full Bench held that the Joint Bench could weigh the evidence and decide the land question itself, so it should have done so. It rejected the view that the case should go back to the Appellate Court. The Supreme Court Rules r 29(2) take a similar line: a Full Bench that gets a case referred on a complex legal question may also decide other questions and finally dispose of the case.

### The judgment

The appeal judgment must set out, in separate paragraphs, the claims, the lower decision, the grounds of appeal, the issues, the arguments, whether any precedent cited is relevant, and the reasoned decision (High Court Rules r 111). If the bench disagrees with the order that called the respondent, it must say why (r 111(झ)).

**Finality.** Unless the law says otherwise, a decision of a court in a case is final and binds all parties (Administration of Justice Act s 18(1)). The same court cannot reopen it or change it in a way that affects the justice of the case (s 18(2)). Only clerical slips may be corrected, by a separate recorded order (s 18(3)).

### Reference for confirmation (sadhak)

Some heavy sentences go to the higher court even without an appeal. Under the Criminal Procedure Code s 145(1), a court that sentences an accused to twenty years or more, or to life imprisonment, must send the case for confirmation to the appellate court within 35 days. If a party appeals in such a case, the appeal takes over from the sadhak (Criminal Procedure Code s 147(2); Administration of Justice Act s 10(3)). A sadhak must not be decided while the time to appeal is still running (Criminal Procedure Code s 147(1)).

## 6.6 Application for special leave to appeal

### Meaning

An ordinary appeal is a **right**: if the law gives it and the appeal is in time and in form, the court must register and hear it. **Leave to appeal** is different. The party must first ask the higher court for permission (अनुमति). The court looks at the application at a preliminary hearing. It grants leave only if the grounds the law sets are shown. Only then is the case heard like an appeal.

The best-known example in South Asia is the "special leave petition" to the Supreme Court of India under Article 136 of the Indian Constitution. There, the Court has a wide discretion to grant leave against almost any judgment.

!!! note "Nepal's position in one line"
    We found no general "special leave to appeal" in Nepal's Civil Procedure Code or the Administration of Justice Act 2073. An appeal to the Supreme Court is a right in the cases listed in s 9. Outside them, the main route is revision with permission. Leave to appeal in the strict sense survives only for some specialised bodies, under the Supreme Court Rules r 65.

### Leave to appeal from specialised tribunals: Supreme Court Rules r 65

- A person not satisfied with a judgment of the Revenue Tribunal (राजस्व न्यायाधिकरण) may apply for leave to appeal (पुनरावेदनको अनुमति). The Rule names s 8 of the Revenue Tribunal Act 2031 and s 16 of the Administrative Court Act 2076 (r 65(1)). The application uses the form in Schedule 8, with a certified copy of the decision and the fee in Schedule 3.
- It must state clear grounds for getting leave (r 65(2)).
- At a preliminary hearing (प्रारम्भिक सुनुवाई), if the bench finds that leave should be given, it grants leave with reasons (r 65(3)).
- The applicant then pays the appeal fee and the fee for notices to respondents (r 65(4)). The application is then **registered as an appeal**, with a separate number showing it came through leave (r 65(5)).
- A Joint Bench hears leave applications under s 8 of the Revenue Tribunal Act and the cases where leave is granted (r 24(2)(घ)).

!!! warning "Unverified"
    We did not check the Revenue Tribunal Act 2031 s 8 or the Administrative Court Act 2076 s 16 themselves; we describe them only as Supreme Court Rules r 65 names them.

### Revision: the general route that needs permission

Where no appeal lies to the Supreme Court, a party may ask it to **revise** a High Court judgment or final order (Administration of Justice Act 2073 s 12; Civil Procedure Code s 219). It works much like leave to appeal.

- **Grounds** (Act s 12(1); Code s 219(1)): (क) a serious constitutional or legal error in the High Court's judgment; (ख) failure to follow, or wrong interpretation of, a legal principle or precedent set by the Supreme Court; (ग) in a dispute over government or public property, misuse or loss of that property because the evidence was not properly weighed; (घ) a child, woman, person with disability, person with mental illness, or person aged 75 or more was not properly represented, and this materially affected the justice of the case.
- **Exclusions** (Act s 12(2)): a case already revised once by the Supreme Court; and a case the High Court decided on appeal under s 8(3)(ख).
- **Time:** within 45 days of learning of the High Court judgment, through that High Court or in the Supreme Court (Act s 12(3); Code s 219(2)).
- **Permission:** a Joint Bench decides whether to grant permission (Act s 12(5)). If granted, judges other than those who granted it and those who gave the first judgment hear the case (s 12(6)).
- **Procedure after permission:** the Supreme Court orders the other parties to be called, hears and decides. Their absence does not stop the decision (Code s 219(5)-(6)). The Court decides only within the grounds on which it gave permission (Code s 221). Under the Supreme Court Rules r 64, the application is registered **as an appeal** once permission is given and the fees are paid.

In *Shivanath*, the Supreme Court refused permission to revise the first Appellate Court judgment, but on the same day called the respondent in Shivanath's appeal. The Full Bench held that refusing permission to revise did not end a valid appeal against the later judgment.

**Review** of the Supreme Court's own judgments (Act s 11; Code s 220; Rules r 63) is also by permission, granted by judges other than those who gave the judgment. It is covered in Unit 7.

### Leave to appeal without going into custody

The word "leave" (अनुमति) also appears in criminal appeals in a narrower sense. An appellant who would normally have to be in custody to appeal may ask the appellate court for leave to appeal without going into custody, on a deposit or guarantee (Criminal Procedure Code s 137(2)). The court must record its reasons (s 137(3)). This is leave about **how** to appeal, not about **whether** an appeal lies.

## Model answers

??? question "2080 Q6: Unsatisfied party can appeal to higher court. If a party to a case files an appeal when the other party of the case has not obtained the copy of the judgement and also has not been issued notice of appeal, and if the court gives an ex parte judgement, will the judgement be legitimate or not? Explain in reference to the procedure set in the case of Shivanath Shah Kanu v Bishwonath Shah Kanu. (20 marks)"
    The case brief has this answer written out in full: [Shivanath Shah Kanu: exam answer](cases/9-10-shivanath-shah-kanu.md#full-20-mark-answer). Learn it from there. Below is the skeleton, with the Unit 6 law to add.

    **1. Introduction.** Appeal is a legal right (Civil Procedure Code s 15). The 30 days run from knowing the judgment (s 205). So a party must be told of the judgment and of his time to appeal (s 200(3)).

    **2. Citation.** NKP 2069, No. 5, D.N. 8820; Full Bench (Khil Raj Regmi CJ, Baidya Nath Upadhyay and Kamal Narayan Das JJ); decided 2068/12/23.

    **3. Facts.** Partition suit by Bishwanath against his younger brother Shivanath in the Bara District Court. Both appealed. The Appellate Court, Hetauda decided Bishwanath's appeal on 2058/3/25, before Shivanath had been sent his notice of time to appeal and without calling him. Later, after hearing both, it partly reversed the District Court (2060/5/30). The Supreme Court refused permission to revise the first judgment. The Joint Bench split on Shivanath's appeal, and the case went to the Full Bench.

    **4. Issues.** Did refusing permission to revise end the dispute? Who decides the land question? Behind both: what is the effect of an appeal judgment given while the other side's time to appeal is running?

    **5. Decision and ratio.** (a) An appeal court must check whether a party's time to appeal is still running and whether he has appealed, then decide once, all together. (b) A judgment on one party's appeal, given before the other was sent his notice, cannot narrow, restrict or cancel the other's right to appeal. (c) One court cannot give two judgments in one dispute; the later one, given after hearing both sides, is authoritative.

    **6. Answer to the question.** No, the ex parte appeal judgment is not a legitimate final answer to the dispute. It cannot bind the party who was never told, and his right to appeal stays intact. Today, service starts the ordinary period under s 205(2). If no notice was served, s 205(4) requires a copy within six months of authentication and an appeal within 30 days of that copy. Apply these limits separately from the old-law Shivanath decision.

    **7. Today's law.** The court must send the notice within three days (s 200(3)). The appeal court may decide without the respondent only if the decision is not likely to change (s 213). All appeals against one decision are heard together, and later ones are still decided on merits (s 218). The file must carry proof of the served notice (Civil Procedure Rules rr 41, 47). In the Supreme Court, the deciding court must issue notices to the remaining parties before sending the file (Supreme Court Rules r 61(4)).

    **8. Comment.** The judgment protects the right to appeal from the court's own mistake and fits audi alteram partem. Contrast *Pradip* (9.9): there the notice was properly served, so the right ended when the time ran out. One gap: the Full Bench did not say what happens to steps already taken under the first judgment.

    **9. Conclusion.** A court's slip cannot take away a party's right to appeal. When both appeals are heard, the later judgment prevails.

??? question "2080 Q7(e): Rejoinder (short note)"
    **Meaning.** A rejoinder is the respondent's written reply to an appeal or writ petition. It answers the appellant's or petitioner's grounds and defends the decision under challenge. The word comes from common-law pleading, where the defendant's rejoinder answered the plaintiff's replication.

    **Nepali terms.** In an appeal: लिखित प्रतिवाद (written defence). In a writ petition: लिखित जवाफ (written reply).

    **Law.**

    - Civil: after the notice under s 214 is served, the respondent may file a written defence (Civil Procedure Code s 214(3)). He may contest both the grounds of appeal and the order calling him (Civil Procedure Rules 2075 r 50).
    - Criminal: the respondent may file a written defence within the time the court sets, in the form in Schedule 46 (Criminal Procedure Code s 140(5)-(6)).
    - Supreme Court: a respondent may contest in writing an appeal, review, revision or leave application and the court's order, paying the fee (Supreme Court Rules 2074 r 68). Evidence needs the bench's permission (r 69).
    - Writ: the court asks the respondent for a written reply after a show-cause order (Unit 7).

    **Features.** Filed only after the court calls the respondent; optional, but the hearing goes on without it (s 215(2)); answers each ground point by point; may raise time limit, form and whether an appeal lies.

    **Example.** In *Pradip Kumar Prasad Shah Kalwar v Ram Bhajan Raya Yadav*, three rejoinders came in: from the Appellate Court, Hetauda, from Ram Bhajan, and from the District Court and its tahasildar. All said the notice of time to appeal had been properly served, and asked for the petition to be dismissed.

    **Importance.** It puts the respondent's side on record, so the court hears both sides (audi alteram partem) and decides on full arguments.

??? question "Likely short note: Ex parte hearing in an appeal"
    **Meaning.** A hearing with only one side present. In an appeal it has two senses.

    1. **First hearing on the appellant's side alone.** Within 15 days of getting the file, the appeal court hears whether the respondent must be called. If the lower decision is not likely to change, it decides (upholds). If it may change, it orders the respondent called, with reasons (Civil Procedure Code s 213). Criminal: s 140(2)-(3). A short-form upholding judgment may use Schedule 7 (High Court Rules r 114).
    2. **Hearing after the respondent fails to appear.** Civil: the court must hear the appeal even if the served respondent does not come (s 215(2)). Criminal: the court fixes a date at least 15 days later and decides; the respondent can get his time extended for reasonable cause (s 141).

    **Safeguards.** The respondent cannot lose at the first hearing without being called. The second kind needs proper service (*Pradip*).

    **Ex parte judgment at first instance.** A defendant who lost ex parte because of improper service can file an appeal that counts as his written defence, within 35 days of learning of the judgment, provided he learns within six months of judgment (s 118).

    **Limit.** An appeal court must not decide one party's appeal ex parte while another party's time to appeal is running (*Shivanath*; s 218).

??? question "Likely short note: Hearing all appeals together (Civil Procedure Code s 218)"
    Where different parties file separate appeals against one decision in the same case, all must be heard together and decided (s 218(1)). If one was decided earlier for some reason, the rest must still be decided on their merits (s 218(2)). This puts into the Code the duty the Full Bench stated in *Shivanath*: decide the matter at one level once, all together. Where a court has given two judgments, the later one, given after hearing both sides, prevails. The rule avoids conflicting judgments and protects the right to appeal of the party whose time was still running.

## Full 20-mark answers

These are written the way you would write them in the exam. Each takes about 35 minutes by hand. Learn the structure and the key sentences; you do not need to memorise every word.

??? example "Q1. Describe the appellate procedure in a civil case in Nepal, from filing the appeal to the final judgment. (likely question, 20 marks)"
    **Introduction**

    No court is free from error. A party who believes a lower court has decided wrongly needs a way to have a higher court correct the decision. That way is the **appeal** (पुनरावेदन): a proceeding in which a higher court re-examines a lower court's decision, on facts, law or both, at the request of a dissatisfied party. The Muluki Civil Procedure Code 2074 defines the appeal petition as an application, with arguments, to a higher court to correct a lower court's decision (s 2(च)).

    Two textbook points frame the topic. First, an appeal is a creature of statute: it exists only where a law gives it. Second, where the law gives it, it is a right. Section 15 of the Code gives a party dissatisfied with a first-instance judgment or final order the right to appeal according to law. The procedure is in Chapter 19 (ss 205-218), supported by the Administration of Justice Act 2073 and the court Rules.

    **1. Which court hears the appeal**

    Appeals go one level up. Under the Administration of Justice Act 2073, District Courts hear appeals from quasi-judicial bodies and local judicial committees (s 7(4)). High Courts hear appeals from District Court judgments (s 8(3); Constitution Art 144(3)). The Supreme Court hears appeals only in the cases listed in s 9: cases a High Court decided at first instance; sentences of ten years or more; and serious cases where the High Court reversed the lower decision in whole or part.

    **2. When an appeal does not lie**

    Section 206 bars an appeal where the defendant let a properly served notice lapse without defending, where the case was settled by mediation or compromise, where the claim was withdrawn, or where the plaint was dismissed. An improper dismissal can still be appealed.

    **3. Time limit**

    The appeal must be filed within **30 days** of learning of the judgment (s 205(1)). If the party heard the judgment in court, the days run from the paper he signs (s 200(1), s 205(2)). If he was absent, the court must send him a notice of judgment with time to appeal within three days of preparing the judgment (s 200(3)). The days then run from the date of service (s 205(2)). If neither happened, he may appeal within 30 days of taking a copy, if he takes it within six months of the judgment being authenticated (s 205(4)). A lapsed time limit may be revived once, by up to 15 days, for causes beyond his control (s 223).

    **4. Form, fee and documents**

    The appeal follows the form in Schedule 21, in polite language, with supporting documents (s 208(1)-(2)). The appeal fee is Rs 300 (s 208(3)). Proof of the court fee must be attached (s 207); the fee is 15 percent of the plaint fee to the extent of dissatisfaction (s 73(1)). Under the Civil Procedure Rules 2075 r 44, the appeal states the dates, the points of dissatisfaction and their legal basis, and whether the other party has appealed.

    **5. Registration**

    The appeal is filed in the appellate court of the deciding court (s 209(1)). The court checks the time, the fee and the form. If all is in order, it registers the appeal and gives a receipt (s 209(2)). If not, it writes a refusal endorsement (दरपीठ) with reasons (s 209(3)). A curable defect may be fixed within seven days (Rules r 45(2)). The appeal may also be filed through the deciding court, which sends it on within three days (s 210). A government employee may file through the court where he is posted (s 211).

    **6. Calling the case file**

    Within three days of registration, the appellate court asks the lower court for the case file (मिसिल); the lower court sends it within three days (s 212). The file must carry proof that the parties heard the judgment or were served the notice (Rules r 47).

    **7. First hearing on the appellant's side alone**

    Within 15 days of getting the file, the court hears whether the respondent must be called (s 213(1)). If the lower decision is not likely to change, it decides as it finds, which means upholding the judgment (s 213(2)). The High Court may write a short upholding judgment in the form in Schedule 7 (High Court Rules r 114). If the decision may change, the court records its reasons and orders the respondent to be called (s 213(3); High Court Rules r 112).

    **8. Notice to the respondent**

    The court serves a notice on the respondent to appear within 15 days, not counting travel time, in the form in Schedule 22 (s 214(1)). It may serve it itself or through the trial court, following Chapter 8 (s 214(2)). A copy of the order and the appeal go with it (Rules r 49).

    **9. Respondent's written defence (rejoinder)**

    After service, the respondent may file a written defence (लिखित प्रतिवाद) (s 214(3)). He may contest both the grounds of appeal and the order calling him (Rules r 50). If he wishes, he is kept on tarikh (s 214(4)).

    **10. Final hearing**

    The court fixes a hearing date and hears the appeal, whether or not the parties are on tarikh (s 215(1)). It hears it even if the respondent does not appear (s 215(2)). In the High Court, a two-judge bench hears appeals (High Court Rules r 10). Where several parties have appealed against one decision, all appeals are heard together. If one was decided earlier, the rest must still be decided on merits (s 218).

    **11. Powers and judgment**

    The court may uphold or quash the judgment, reverse it in part or full, take missing evidence or order it taken, decide unaddressed issues or send them down, and make incidental changes (s 216). It may use the trial court's powers (s 217). It decides as it finds, in the form in Schedule 23 (s 215(3)-(4)). The judgment states the claims, the lower decision, the grounds, the issues, the arguments, the precedents, and the reasoned decision (High Court Rules r 111).

    **12. Execution and further steps**

    Execution of the lower judgment is stayed while the appeal is pending (s 231). A further appeal to the Supreme Court lies only under s 9 of the Act. Otherwise, a party may seek revision with the Supreme Court's permission (Act s 12; Code s 219).

    **Critical view**

    The Code's procedure is clear and time-bound: three days for the notice, three days each way for the file, fifteen days for the first hearing. The first hearing on the appellant's side alone saves time and spares respondents in weak appeals. But Chapter 19 states no consequence if the court itself misses these deadlines.

    The prescribed cases show two risks. In *Shivanath Shah Kanu v Bishwanath Shah Kanu*, an appeal court decided one party's appeal before the other had been sent his notice of time to appeal. The Full Bench held that this could not cut down the other party's right to appeal. It also held that the later judgment, given after hearing both sides, prevails. Section 218 now writes this into the Code. In *Pradip Kumar Prasad Shah Kalwar v Ram Bhajan Raya Yadav*, the Court held that once the notice is served properly, a party who says otherwise must prove which step was missed. Both rules depend on careful service of notices, which remains the weak point of the system.

    **Conclusion**

    An appeal in a civil case moves through clear stages: filing within 30 days, registration, calling the file, a first hearing on the appellant's side, notice to the respondent, his written defence, the final hearing and the judgment. The Code balances speed with fairness: a respondent is not troubled where the judgment will stand, but he must be heard before it is changed against him. As *Shivanath* shows, the appeal court's duty is to make sure every party with a right to appeal has been heard, and to decide once, all together.

??? example "Q2. What is an ex parte hearing in appellate proceedings? Explain the provisions on notice to the respondent and the consequences of his absence. (likely question, 20 marks)"
    **Introduction**

    Ex parte means "from one side". An ex parte hearing is one held with only one party present. At first sight, it seems to conflict with natural justice, whose rule audi alteram partem says no one should be judged without a fair chance to be heard. The law reconciles the two through **notice**. A party who is given proper notice and stays away cannot complain. A party who was never given notice can.

    The Constitution of Nepal 2015 protects the right of every person to be informed of proceedings taken against him (Art 20(8)) and to a fair trial (Art 20(9)). In appellate proceedings, the Muluki Civil Procedure Code 2074 and the Muluki Criminal Procedure Code 2074 turn these rights into concrete steps.

    **1. Two kinds of ex parte hearing in an appeal**

    (a) The **first hearing on the appellant's side alone**, held before the respondent is called.

    (b) The **hearing after the respondent, though served, fails to appear**.

    **2. The first hearing on the appellant's side**

    Under s 213 of the Civil Procedure Code, within 15 days of getting the case file, the appellate court hears whether the respondent must be called, as the appellant asks. If the lower decision is not likely to change, the court decides as it finds (s 213(2)). This means the appeal fails and the judgment stands. If the decision may change, the court records its reasons and orders the respondent to be called (s 213(3)).

    The Criminal Procedure Code s 140 is similar. The court may reject the appeal with reasons if the lower decision is reasonable and no material difference appears (s 140(2)). If the decision may change, it orders the respondent called (s 140(3)). The whole hearing must finish within six months of getting the file (s 140(1)).

    The High Court Rules 2073 require the order calling the respondent to give reasons (r 112). A short upholding judgment may use the form in Schedule 7 (r 114).

    This hearing does not breach natural justice. The respondent is the party who won below. The court can only uphold his judgment without hearing him. It cannot reverse it against him without first calling him.

    **3. Notice to the respondent**

    **Civil.** When the respondent is to be called, the court serves a notice to appear within 15 days, excluding travel time, in the form in Schedule 22 (s 214(1)). It may serve it itself or through the trial court, following the procedure of Chapter 8 (s 214(2)). Chapter 8 requires the notice to be handed to the person or an adult of his household before local witnesses, or pasted on the door if no one takes it. The service is then checked by a court officer, and a defective service is cancelled and repeated (ss 105, 110, 111, 117). The notice goes with copies of the order and the appeal (Civil Procedure Rules 2075 r 49).

    **Criminal.** The notice, with a copy of the appeal, gives 15 days excluding travel time (s 140(4)). The court may serve it itself or through the trial court (s 140(4क)). It is served like a summons (s 143), in the form in Schedule 23 of the Criminal Procedure Rules 2075 (r 75).

    **Supreme Court.** The Supreme Court Rules 2074 r 67 give the same 15 days, with the Schedule 9 form and copies of the petition and order to every respondent.

    **4. The respondent's reply**

    After service, the respondent may file a written defence (लिखित प्रतिवाद), the rejoinder (Civil Procedure Code s 214(3); Criminal Procedure Code s 140(5)-(6); Civil Procedure Rules r 50; Supreme Court Rules r 68). He may also stay on tarikh (s 214(4)).

    **5. Consequences of the respondent's absence**

    **Civil.** Once the notice has been served, the court must hear the appeal even if the respondent does not appear (s 215(2)). It fixes a date and hears the appeal even if a party is not on tarikh (s 215(1)). It then decides as it finds (s 215(3)).

    **Criminal.** Section 141 is headed "ex parte hearing of the appeal if the respondent is absent". If the respondent does not appear within the notice period, the court fixes a date at least 15 days later and may hear and decide the appeal then (s 141(1)). If, before that date, the respondent shows a reasonable cause for his absence, the court may extend his time and hear him (s 141(2)).

    **6. The other notice: notice of judgment and time to appeal**

    In a civil case, notice matters even before the appeal. A party absent at judgment must be sent the notice of judgment, with time to appeal, within three days (Civil Procedure Code s 200(3)). His 30 days run only from its service (Civil Procedure Code s 205(2)). If it was never served, he may appeal within 30 days of taking a copy, taken within six months (s 205(4)).

    *Shivanath Shah Kanu v Bishwanath Shah Kanu* (NKP 2069, No. 5, D.N. 8820) shows why. The Appellate Court decided one brother's appeal before the other had even been sent his notice of time to appeal. The Full Bench held that such a judgment cannot narrow, restrict or cancel the other party's right to appeal. The appeal court must check whether any party's time to appeal is running, and decide all appeals together. Section 218 of the Code now requires this.

    *Pradip Kumar Prasad Shah Kalwar v Ram Bhajan Raya Yadav* (NKP 2073, No. 10, D.N. 9690) shows the other side. The notice of time to appeal was pasted on the door before witnesses after the household refused it. The Court held it validly served. The burden was on the party to show which step was missed, and a properly served notice is not cancelled without reason.

    **7. Ex parte judgment at first instance**

    A defendant who lost ex parte in the trial court because the notice was served improperly may file an appeal that counts as his written defence, within 35 days of knowing, if he learns within six months (Civil Procedure Code s 118(1)). If the appellate court finds the service improper, it quashes the ex parte judgment and sends the case back (s 118(3)). A properly served defendant who stayed away has no appeal (s 206(क)).

    **Critical view**

    The system is sound in design. The first hearing filters out weak appeals without troubling respondents. Ex parte hearings after proper service prevent a party from blocking justice by staying away. The criminal Code's extra 15 days and the chance to show cause (s 141) add fairness.

    But everything depends on the quality of service. Pasting on a door before local witnesses can be abused, and the burden of proving a defect falls on the absent party (*Pradip*). Chapter 19 of the civil Code has no rule like criminal s 141(2) to excuse a respondent's absence; he must rely on the general extension rule in s 223. And, as *Shivanath* shows, a court that does not check whether all parties have been served the notice of time to appeal can give two judgments in one dispute.

    **Conclusion**

    An ex parte hearing in an appeal is lawful in two settings: the first hearing, where the respondent cannot be harmed, and the hearing after he ignores a properly served notice. Proper notice is the bridge between speed and natural justice. Where notice is given as the law requires, the absent party bears the result. Where it is not, any judgment against him gives way, as the Full Bench held in *Shivanath*.

??? example "Q3. When does an appeal lie to the Supreme Court of Nepal? Explain the application for leave to appeal and the revision procedure. (likely question, 20 marks)"
    **Introduction**

    The Supreme Court is the highest court in Nepal. Under Art 133(4) of the Constitution of Nepal 2015, it has power, as federal law provides, to hear appeals, test cases referred for confirmation, revise cases and review its own judgments. Art 133(5) adds appeals from cases a High Court decided at first instance. Because it is the final court, access to it is limited. Some cases reach it as of right, by **appeal**. Others reach it only with its **permission**: leave to appeal, revision and review.

    **1. Appeal as of right: Administration of Justice Act 2073 s 9**

    The Supreme Court hears appeals in:

    - (क) cases a High Court decided at first instance under s 8(2);
    - (ख) cases where the High Court imposed ten years' imprisonment or more;
    - (ग) cases involving more than three years' prison, a fine over Rs 5 lakh, or a claim over Rs 25 lakh, where the High Court on appeal set aside the lower decision in whole or in part;
    - (घ) other cases where federal law gives an appeal.

    It also hears appeals in cases sent for confirmation (s 9(2)).

    So an ordinary High Court judgment that upholds the District Court is not appealable. This keeps the Supreme Court for serious cases and cases where the two lower courts disagreed.

    **2. Procedure of a Supreme Court appeal**

    The appeal is filed in the Schedule 4 form, stating the points of dissatisfaction and relief (Supreme Court Rules 2074 r 60). It may be filed through the deciding court or the prison (r 61). Where the case has several parties and some have not yet been sent their notice of time to appeal, the deciding court must issue those notices first and send the file with the service records (r 61(4)). A Joint Bench hears appeals (r 24(2)(क)). The respondent, if called, gets 15 days plus travel time (r 67) and may contest in writing (r 68). If the two justices disagree, a Full Bench decides (r 23(2)(क)).

    **3. Leave to appeal**

    Leave to appeal means the party must first get the court's permission before the appeal can proceed. India's special leave petition under Art 136 of its Constitution is the well-known example. Nepal has no general procedure of this kind in the Civil Procedure Code or the Administration of Justice Act. The Supreme Court Rules r 65 keep it for decisions of the Revenue Tribunal, naming s 8 of the Revenue Tribunal Act 2031 and s 16 of the Administrative Court Act 2076:

    - the application is in the Schedule 8 form, with a certified copy and the fee (r 65(1));
    - it must state clear grounds for getting leave (r 65(2));
    - at a preliminary hearing, if leave should be given, the bench grants it with reasons (r 65(3));
    - after fees are paid, it is registered as an appeal with a separate number (r 65(4)-(5));
    - a Joint Bench hears these applications (r 24(2)(घ)).

    **4. Revision: Administration of Justice Act s 12 and Code s 219**

    Where no appeal lies to the Supreme Court, a party may apply for revision of a High Court judgment or final order. The grounds are:

    - (क) a serious constitutional or legal error;
    - (ख) not following, or wrongly interpreting, a Supreme Court precedent or legal principle;
    - (ग) loss or misuse of government or public property because evidence was not properly weighed;
    - (घ) a child, woman, person with disability, person with mental illness or person aged 75 or more was not properly represented, materially affecting justice.

    A case already revised once, or decided by the High Court on appeal under s 8(3)(ख), cannot be revised (s 12(2)). The application is due within 45 days of learning of the judgment (s 12(3); Code s 219(2)). A Joint Bench decides whether to grant permission (s 12(5)). If granted, other judges hear it (s 12(6)). The Court calls the other parties and decides, even if they do not appear (Code s 219(5)-(6)). It is confined to the grounds on which permission was given (Code s 221). Once permission is given, the application is registered as an appeal (Rules r 64).

    **5. Review**

    The Supreme Court may review its own judgment on new evidence or because it went against an established precedent, if the application is made within 60 days of authentication (Act s 11). Judges other than those who decided first give permission and hear it (s 11(5)-(6); Constitution Art 133(4)).

    **6. Shivanath and the permission routes**

    In *Shivanath Shah Kanu v Bishwanath Shah Kanu*, the Supreme Court refused permission to revise the Appellate Court's first judgment. The Full Bench held that this refusal did not end Shivanath's valid appeal against the later judgment, which had partly reversed the District Court. Refusing permission in one route does not close a right of appeal in another.

    **Critical view**

    Limiting appeals saves the Supreme Court's time for important cases. The money thresholds in s 9(1)(ग) favour finality. But they also mean that small claimants usually have only one appeal, while wealthier ones may have two. Revision fills the gap only for serious legal error, not for ordinary mistakes of fact. And Nepal's leave procedure is narrow: it covers only some tribunals, unlike the wide special leave in India.

    **Conclusion**

    An appeal lies to the Supreme Court as of right only in the cases in s 9 of the Administration of Justice Act 2073. Leave to appeal survives for Revenue Tribunal decisions under the Supreme Court Rules r 65. Revision under s 12 is the main permission route for other High Court judgments. For leave and revision, the Court first decides whether the statutory grounds for permission are met. An ordinary s 9 appeal does not need this permission.

    Within 15 days of getting the file, the court hears whether to call the respondent. If the decision is not likely to change, it decides; if it may change, it orders the respondent called, with reasons (s 213).

??? question "4. How long does the respondent get to appear?"
    Fifteen days, not counting travel time (Civil Procedure Code s 214(1); Criminal Procedure Code s 140(4); Supreme Court Rules r 67).

??? question "5. What does s 218 of the Civil Procedure Code require?"
    Separate appeals by different parties against one decision must be heard together. If one was decided earlier, the rest must still be decided on merits.

    Seventy days where the Government of Nepal is plaintiff, 30 days in other cases, from knowing of the judgment. The appellate court may extend by up to 30 days for reasonable cause (Criminal Procedure Code s 134(1)).

??? question "7. What is the ratio of Shivanath Shah Kanu v Bishwanath Shah Kanu on appeals?"

## One-minute revision

- **Appeal:** a higher court re-examines a lower decision. A creature of statute; a right under Code s 15. DC → HC (Act s 8(3)) → SC only in Act s 9 cases.
- **Filing:** 30 days from knowing (Code s 205); notice of judgment within 3 days (s 200(3)); no appeal in s 206 cases; Schedule 21, Rs 300 fee, court fee (ss 207-208, 73); registration or दरपीठ (s 209); via deciding court (s 210); file called in 3 + 3 days (s 212). Criminal: 70/30 days, +30 for cause (s 134); custody or deposit (s 137).
- **Ex parte hearing:** first hearing on appellant's side within 15 days of file; uphold or call respondent with reasons (Code s 213; Crim s 140(2)-(3); HC Rules rr 112, 114). Respondent absent after service: hearing goes on (Code s 215(2); Crim s 141, at least 15 days, extension for cause). Ex parte first-instance judgment after improper service: appeal as a defence within 35 days of learning of the judgment, provided he learns within six months of judgment (s 118).
- **Notice to respondent:** 15 days plus travel, Schedule 22, by itself or through trial court under Ch 8 (Code s 214); Crim s 140(4), s 143; SC Rules r 67.
- **Rejoinder:** लिखित प्रतिवाद in appeals (Code s 214(3); Crim s 140(5)-(6); CPR r 50; SC Rules r 68); लिखित जवाफ in writs. Optional; hearing goes on without it.
- **Final hearing and judgment:** s 215; all appeals together (s 218); powers s 216-217, Act s 14, Crim s 144; Schedule 23; HC joint bench (r 10), third judge (r 12); SC Joint Bench, Full Bench if split (rr 23-24).
- **Leave and revision:** no general special leave; Revenue Tribunal leave under SC Rules r 65; revision with permission (Act s 12; Code s 219; 45 days; Joint Bench); review (Act s 11).
- **Cases:** *Shivanath* (9.10): no notice issued → right to appeal intact; later judgment after hearing both prevails. *Pradip* (9.9): notice properly served → time ran; burden on the party alleging defect.
