# Unit 7: Writ, Review and Revision

!!! abstract "In one line"
    This unit covers three special ways to reach the higher courts: a writ petition, which asks the Supreme Court or a High Court to protect a right or stop an unlawful act; review, where the Supreme Court looks again at its own judgment; and revision, where the Supreme Court looks again at a High Court judgment that cannot be appealed.

## Start here: three ways back to a higher court

Imagine three people.

- **Sita** is a farmer. A government office refuses her a licence without hearing her side. No law gives her an appeal. She goes straight to the High Court with a **writ petition**. She asks it to cancel the refusal and order the office to decide again fairly.
- **Ram** lost a land case in the Supreme Court. After the judgment, he finds an old deed that proves his ownership. He could not have found it earlier. He asks the Supreme Court to **review** its own judgment.
- **Hari** lost a case in the High Court. The law gives no appeal to the Supreme Court in his kind of case. But the High Court ignored a clear Supreme Court precedent. He asks the Supreme Court to **revise** (look again at) the High Court's judgment.

All three are asking a court to correct a decision. But each route has its own conditions, time limits and steps. A writ is not an appeal, and neither review nor revision is a second appeal. This unit explains each route and the procedure for it.

## 7.1 Writ proceedings

### What a writ is

A writ is a written order of a superior court. It commands a person or authority to do something, or not to do something. In Nepal only the Supreme Court and the High Courts issue writs. They do so under their **extraordinary jurisdiction** (असाधारण अधिकारक्षेत्र).

It is "extraordinary" because it is not the normal way to correct a decision. The normal ways are appeal and revision, which statutes provide. The writ jurisdiction comes from the Constitution itself. It is meant for cases where a right has been invaded and the ordinary law gives no proper remedy.

A writ case is not a suit between two private people over money or land. It is usually a challenge to the act of a public body: a ministry, an office, a local government, a quasi-judicial officer, or a lower court. The person who files is the **petitioner** (निवेदक). The body or person whose act is challenged is the **respondent** (प्रत्यर्थी, also विपक्षी).

### The constitutional basis

**Article 46** of the Constitution of Nepal 2015 makes the remedy itself a fundamental right. There is a right to obtain constitutional remedies "in the manner set forth in Article 133 or 144" to enforce the rights in Part 3. This is the right to constitutional remedy (संवैधानिक उपचारको हक).

**Article 133(2)** gives the Supreme Court extraordinary power for three purposes:

1. to enforce the **fundamental rights** in the Constitution;
2. to enforce **any other legal right** for which no other remedy has been provided, or for which the remedy provided "appears to be inadequate or ineffective"; and
3. to settle any **constitutional or legal question** involved in a dispute of public interest or concern.

For these purposes it may issue necessary and appropriate orders, give appropriate remedies, enforce the right or settle the dispute.

**Article 133(3)** says that under this jurisdiction the Supreme Court may issue appropriate orders and writs. These include habeas corpus, mandamus, certiorari, prohibition and quo warranto. Its proviso keeps the court out of the internal proceedings of the Federal Parliament or a Provincial Assembly, and their privilege proceedings, except where they lack jurisdiction.

**Article 144(1)-(2)** gives each High Court the same kind of power, with the same five writs and the same proviso. There is one difference. The High Court's public-interest power covers any **legal** question. The Supreme Court's covers any **constitutional or legal** question.

Section 8(1) of the **Administration of Justice Act 2073** says the High Court may hear petitions filed under Art 144(1) and issue necessary and appropriate orders.

!!! info "A different power: Article 133(1)"
    Article 133(1) lets any citizen ask the Supreme Court to declare a law void. Grounds include conflict with the Constitution, such as an unreasonable restriction on a fundamental right. A provincial law that conflicts with a federal law can also be declared void. So can a local law that conflicts with a federal or provincial law. The court may declare the law void from the start or from the date of its decision. The Constitutional Bench (संवैधानिक इजलास) hears these petitions (Art 137(2)). This is judicial review of legislation. The High Court has no such power under Article 144.

### Kinds of writ (in brief)

Constitutional Law covers the writs in depth. For this paper, know what each one does.

| Writ | Nepali | Meaning | What it does |
|---|---|---|---|
| habeas corpus | बन्दीप्रत्यक्षीकरण | "You shall have the body" | Orders that a detained person be brought before the court, and frees him if the detention is unlawful |
| mandamus | परमादेश | "We command" | Orders a public authority to perform a legal duty it has failed to perform |
| certiorari | उत्प्रेषण | "To be informed" | Calls up the record and quashes (cancels) an unlawful decision of a lower court or quasi-judicial authority |
| writ of prohibition | प्रतिषेध | Forbidding | Orders a lower court or body not to act beyond its jurisdiction, while the matter is still before it |
| quo warranto | अधिकारपृच्छा | "By what authority" | Asks a person holding a public office to show the legal right to hold it; removes him if he has none |

Articles 133(3) and 144(2) say "including", so the list is not closed. The courts also issue other appropriate orders, such as **directive orders** (निर्देशनात्मक आदेश), which tell the government to take steps. The Supreme Court Rules 2074 refer to directive orders issued in writ petitions (r 127(4)).

Two writs are often combined. In [Kallu Chaudhary](cases/9-6-kallu-chaudhary.md) the petitioner asked for certiorari with mandamus (उत्प्रेषणयुक्त परमादेश). Certiorari would cancel the decisions against him, and mandamus would order the office to decide again in his favour.

### Who may file

Rule 32(1) of the **Supreme Court Rules 2074** says who may file a petition under Article 133(2):

- **(क)** to enforce a fundamental right, or a legal right with no remedy or an inadequate or ineffective one: **the person whose right is affected**;
- **(ख)** to settle a constitutional or legal question in a dispute of public interest: **a person who has a meaningful relation and substantial interest** in the matter (सार्थक सम्बन्ध र तात्विक सरोकार).

This is the rule of locus standi for writs (Unit 2). Limb (ख) allows public interest litigation (PIL). Under rule 32(2), people who rely on different facts, grounds or legal questions must file separate petitions, even against the same decision.

For **habeas corpus**, the detainee files. If he cannot, a relative or any concerned person who knows the facts may file for him, giving the reason (r 33(1) and its proviso).

### Writ jurisdiction and the alternative remedy

This is the most-asked point in the unit (2077 Q3).

**The rule.** For a legal right that is not a fundamental right, the writ lies only where no other remedy exists, or the remedy "appears to be inadequate or ineffective" (Arts 133(2) and 144(1)). The Supreme Court Rules build this in at three points:

1. **At registration.** The registry may refuse to register a writ petition (दरपीठ) where, at first sight, an appeal or a revision application lies. It may also refuse where the matter can go to an appeal court under certain sections on orders made during a case. These are ss 156(4) and 157 of the Civil Procedure Code, s 73 of the Criminal Procedure Code and s 15 of the Administration of Justice Act (r 18(2)).
2. **In the petition.** The petition must show, as needed, that no alternative remedy exists, or that it is inadequate or ineffective (r 40(1)(घ)).
3. **At the hearing.** The court refuses the writ if an effective remedy was available and not used.

**Why the rule exists.**

- The legislature has created appeals and revision for a purpose. If parties could skip them, those routes would become meaningless.
- Appeal courts can weigh evidence and facts. The writ court mainly checks legality.
- It keeps each route in its place and gives finality to decisions (judicial certainty).
- It stops the higher courts being flooded with petitions that belong elsewhere.

**The cases.**

- **[Kallu Chaudhary v Appellate Court, Dipayal](cases/9-6-kallu-chaudhary.md)** (NKP 2060, Joint Bench). Kallu asked by writ to quash an Appellate Court judgment. The Supreme Court held that the route against such a judgment was appeal (s 9) or revision (s 12) under the Administration of Justice Act 2048. "Apart from that, it is not a matter for the writ jurisdiction at all." His claim was "meaningless from the outset". The court added two points. A party who loses by one judicial route cannot try another against the same decision (judicial certainty). And time spent on a remedy the law does not give counts as delay (laches).
- **[Pradip Kumar Agrawal v Tax Office, Morang](cases/9-3-pradip-kumar-agrawal.md)** (NKP 2052, Larger Full Bench, 5 to 2). The petitioner skipped the remedies in the Income Tax Act 2031 and came by writ. The majority dismissed the writ. A required deposit does not make an appeal ineffective. Otherwise every taxpayer could bypass the Act. But the court gave the exceptions. A party may come straight by writ if the decision was made without jurisdiction, under an unconstitutional law, by a body with no power, or without a hearing against natural justice. Such a decision is void, so an appeal against it has no meaning.
- **[Pradip Kumar Prasad Shah Kalwar v Ram Bhajan Raya Yadav](cases/9-9-pradip-kumar-prasad-shah-kalwar.md)** (NKP 2073, Joint Bench). The law gave a remedy for improper service of a notice (Adalati Bandobasta No. 208). A court had already examined the complaint under it and acted on that. The writ jurisdiction could not then be used to seek a remedy.

**The exceptions.** From the Constitution's words and the cases, a writ may still lie despite another remedy:

1. where the remedy is **inadequate or ineffective** (Arts 133(2), 144(1));
2. where a **fundamental right** is directly violated. The "no other remedy" words in Article 133(2) attach to "any other legal right", not to fundamental rights. But Pradip Kumar Agrawal warns that a decision within jurisdiction does not violate a fundamental right just because it affects property;
3. where the decision is **void**: made without jurisdiction, under an unconstitutional law, by a body with no power, or in breach of natural justice ([Pradip Kumar Agrawal](cases/9-3-pradip-kumar-agrawal.md), paragraph 16);
4. where **personal liberty** is at stake. In [Samir Khan v Kathmandu District Court](cases/9-8-samir-khan.md) the government argued that another remedy was available. The Supreme Court issued habeas corpus without dealing with that argument. In [Parshuram Banjade v Durgadas Shrestha](cases/9-1-parshuram-banjade.md) the court held that habeas corpus and certiorari do different jobs, so neither replaces the other.

!!! tip "Exam tip"
    Say it in one line: a writ is not a substitute for appeal or revision, but it is available where no remedy exists, where the remedy is inadequate or ineffective, or where the decision is void. Then cite Article 133(2), rule 40(1)(घ), Kallu Chaudhary and Pradip Kumar Agrawal.

### Judicial review

**Judicial review** (न्यायिक पुनरावलोकन) is the power of the courts to examine the acts of the legislature, the executive and administrative bodies. The court asks whether the act is consistent with the Constitution and the law. If it is not, the court declares it void or quashes it. Judicial review rests on the supremacy of the Constitution. Article 1(1) of the Constitution makes it the fundamental law and says any inconsistent law is void to the extent of the inconsistency.

Do not confuse judicial review with **review** (पुनरावलोकन) of a judgment under s 11 of the Administration of Justice Act (section 7.2 below). Review is a court looking again at its own decision. Judicial review is a court testing the acts of other organs of the state.

**Kinds of judicial review in Nepal.**

1. **Review of legislation.** The Supreme Court may declare a law void under Article 133(1). The Constitutional Bench hears these petitions (Art 137(2)).
2. **Review of administrative and quasi-judicial action.** The Supreme Court (Art 133(2)-(3)) and the High Courts (Art 144) quash unlawful decisions and order authorities to act lawfully, through writs.
3. **Review of judicial decisions.** Certiorari can reach a lower court's decision. But [Kallu Chaudhary](cases/9-6-kallu-chaudhary.md) holds that a court's judgment must normally be challenged by appeal or revision, not by writ.

**Grounds.** Textbooks group the grounds of judicial review of administrative action as illegality, irrationality and procedural impropriety. In Nepali writ practice they include:

- lack or excess of jurisdiction (the act was ultra vires);
- error of law;
- breach of natural justice, such as deciding without a hearing;
- mala fide (bad faith) or improper purpose;
- unreasonable or arbitrary use of discretion;
- acting under an unconstitutional law.

**Courts that do judicial review.**

| Court | Power | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Supreme Court, Constitutional Bench | Declare federal, provincial or local laws void | Constitution Arts 133(1), 137(2) |
| Supreme Court, other benches | Writs and orders for fundamental rights, other legal rights, and constitutional or legal questions of public interest | Arts 133(2)-(3) |
| High Court | Writs and orders for fundamental rights, other legal rights, and legal questions of public interest | Art 144(1)-(2); Administration of Justice Act s 8(1) |
| District Court | Habeas corpus for anyone unlawfully detained in the district; injunction (निषेधाज्ञा) where a legal right is harmed or threatened. These come from statute, not the writ articles | Administration of Justice Act s 7(2)-(3) |

### The writ procedure, step by step

The Supreme Court's procedure is in Chapter 6 of the Supreme Court Rules 2074 (rr 32-57), "Provisions relating to petitions under Article 133(2) of the Constitution". The High Court's is in Chapter 5 of the High Court Rules 2073 (rr 35-50क), on petitions under s 8(1) of the Administration of Justice Act. The steps below follow the Supreme Court Rules, with High Court differences noted.

```
Petition filed with the registry (fee)
        ↓
Registry checks it: registers it, or refuses (दरपीठ)
        ↓
Preliminary hearing by a single justice
        ↓   (if the claim does not look proper, it ends here)
Show-cause order to respondent
(+ interim order, if asked and justified)
        ↓
Notice served on respondent
        ↓
Written reply (rejoinder) within the set time
        ↓
Final hearing by a Joint Bench (two justices)
        ↓
Decision: writ issued, or petition dismissed
        ↓
Execution: order sent to the body; Directorate monitors
```

#### Step 1: Filing the writ petition

The petition is filed before the Registrar with the fee in Schedule 3 (r 32(1)). That fee is Rs 500, and a habeas corpus petition is free (Schedule 3, item 1). The High Court Rules set the same amounts (r 35).

Every document filed in the Supreme Court must meet the general form in rule 13. Examples are A4 paper, set margins, full names and addresses of the parties, and a signed declaration that its contents are true. A writ petition must also state, as needed (r 40(1)):

- (क) the reason for the petition and the facts;
- (ख) the court's jurisdiction;
- (ग) which fundamental or legal right of the petitioner has been infringed, and how;
- (घ) that no alternative remedy exists, or that it is inadequate or ineffective;
- (ङ) the remedy the petitioner asks for; and
- (च) the grounds for that remedy.

**Other filing rules:**

- **Parallel jurisdiction.** The petitioner must state clearly that he has not filed a petition or started legal action on the same matter in a High Court or District Court with parallel jurisdiction (r 43(1)). Without a good reason for choosing the Supreme Court, the petition is not registered (r 43(2)). If the statement proves false, the petition becomes ineffective (r 43(3)). The High Court Rules have a matching rule (r 31).
- **Summary.** If the petition or the written reply is over three pages, a summary of at most two pages must be filed (r 48).
- **PIL petitions** must also show the constitutional or legal question, how the respondent's act affects a class or community, and the petitioner's link to the issue (r 40(2)). The petitioner must declare that he has no personal gain or private interest (r 40(3)).
- **No time limit.** We found no time limit for filing a writ petition in Articles 133 or 144, or in Chapter 6 of the Supreme Court Rules. Delay is judged by the court-made doctrine of laches ([Kallu Chaudhary](cases/9-6-kallu-chaudhary.md)).

#### Step 2: Registration or refusal (दरपीठ)

The registry checks the petition. Rule 18(1) lets it refuse registration for general defects, such as being out of time or outside the court's jurisdiction. Rule 18(2) adds grounds aimed at writs. The registry may refuse a petition on:

- a point the Supreme Court has already clearly settled;
- a matter already pending in another writ petition;
- a matter that, at first sight, can go on appeal or revision, or to an appeal court under the sections on orders made during a case; or
- a vague petition that raises no constitutional or legal question.

The officer must give the grounds and reasons in writing (r 18(1)-(2)).

#### Step 3: Preliminary hearing and the show-cause order

The petition is listed for its first hearing, normally within three days of registration (r 74(6)). In the High Court, a habeas corpus petition is heard the day it is registered, and others the next day (High Court Rules r 82(3)).

A **single justice** hears this preliminary stage (Supreme Court Rules r 25(2)(क); High Court Rules r 11(ख)). The question is whether the petition shows a case worth answering.

If the claim looks proper and lawful, the court must issue a **show-cause order** (कारण देखाउ आदेश) in the respondent's name (r 46(1)). It asks the respondent to explain why the order sought should not be issued. He must send a written reply within a fixed time, appearing himself or through a representative. If the petitioner has asked for an interim order, the court must decide that request at the same time (r 46(1)).

The High Court Rules use the language of a prima facie case. If the petition shows that the respondent appears to have done the wrong complained of, the court issues the show-cause order (r 41(2)).

The rule requires a show-cause order only where the claim looks proper and lawful. If it does not, no show-cause order issues and the petition goes no further.

The court need not issue a show-cause order to a respondent who has no connection with the issue, or whom it is not practical to keep as a party (r 46(2)). It may add a respondent later if the written replies show one is needed.

#### Step 4: Stay order (interim order)

A writ case can take months. Meanwhile the respondent might demolish the house, hold the auction or fill the post. The final order would then be useless. So the petitioner may ask for an **interim order** (अन्तरिम आदेश), often called a **stay order**. He must show that, unless the matter is kept as it is (यथास्थिति) until the final decision, his claim will become meaningless (r 49(1)). An order to keep the status quo is the common form.

**Grounds.** The court may grant an interim order if it finds any of these (r 49(2)):

- the party would suffer **irreparable loss** (अपूरणीय क्षति);
- there is a **serious violation** of a fundamental right or a legal provision; or
- it is proper and necessary on the **balance of convenience** (सुविधा सन्तुलन).

**Forms.** The court may (r 49(2)(क)-(ङ)):

- issue it for a fixed period, or until the petition is finally decided;
- issue it until a discussion with the other side, once that side is summoned;
- summon the other side first, hear both, and then decide;
- summon the other side to discuss whether an ex parte interim order should continue; or
- issue a conditional interim order. The party who benefits gives a written undertaking to pay compensation if the other side suffers loss after the final decision.

**Limits.**

- A fixed-period order lapses when the period ends (r 49(5)).
- If the discussion on continuing the order cannot take place because of the petitioner, the order becomes ineffective (r 49(6)).
- The court may cancel the order at any time, on application or on its own (r 49(7)).
- Every interim order lapses when the petition is finally decided (r 49(8)).
- The court must give reasons both for granting and for refusing an interim order (r 49(9)).
- No extension of time or adjournment is allowed for an interim-order discussion (r 55(3)).
- A respondent may apply to cancel an interim order made ex parte, or made without giving him a chance to be heard. The court may hear both sides and cancel or change it (r 50).

The High Court Rules have the same scheme (r 42). Rule 42(1)(घ) adds that the bench may decide the petition itself, even if it was listed only for an interim order.

**Example.** In [Pradip Kumar Prasad Shah Kalwar](cases/9-9-pradip-kumar-prasad-shah-kalwar.md), the petitioner asked for an interim order to stop the execution of a judgment. The Supreme Court refused it "for now". The petition itself said that the judgment was already being carried out.

#### Step 5: Notice to the respondent

The show-cause order is the notice to the respondent. It is served on each respondent named in it (High Court Rules r 47: served "according to law"). Service of notices follows the Civil Procedure Code s 105 and the Criminal Procedure Code ss 61 and 63. If that fails, the Supreme Court may serve by fax or email, by notice in a national daily or on radio or television, or through a government office or local level (Supreme Court Rules r 148(1)). A party may also appoint an agent or lawyer to receive notices (r 149).

The time given depends on the case. In [Pradip Kumar Prasad Shah Kalwar](cases/9-9-pradip-kumar-prasad-shah-kalwar.md) the court gave 15 days, not counting travel time. In [Samir Khan](cases/9-8-samir-khan.md), a habeas corpus case, it gave 7 days.

**Joining other parties.** A person who learns that he is a respondent, or that he has an interest, may ask to join the hearing (r 47(1)). The court may allow anyone whose right or interest is linked with the petition to join and file a written reply (r 47(2)). It may also call and examine any other person or body (r 47(3)).

#### Step 6: Written reply (rejoinder)

The respondent must file a **written reply** (लिखित जवाफ) within the set time, with the fee in Schedule 3 (r 46(3)). The fee is Rs 50 (Schedule 3, item 2). The syllabus calls this reply a **rejoinder**.

The reply answers the petition point by point. A government respondent usually says that it acted within its powers, that the petitioner has another remedy, or that the petition is late. In [Kallu Chaudhary](cases/9-6-kallu-chaudhary.md), for example, the replies argued that the decision could be challenged only by appeal or revision, and that Kallu had come too late.

If the time cannot be met for a good reason, it may be extended once: up to 7 days in a habeas corpus case and up to 15 days in other writ cases (r 55(1)). Special grounds such as a death in the family, childbirth, blocked roads or natural disaster have their own periods (r 55(2)).

#### Step 7: Final hearing

Once the replies are in, or the time for them has passed, the file is complete (अङ्ग पुगेको). The case is listed for final hearing.

- **Bench.** A **Joint Bench** of two justices hears Article 133(2) petitions once a show-cause order has been issued (Supreme Court Rules r 24(2)(ख)). In the High Court, a joint bench hears writ petitions after the show-cause order (High Court Rules r 10(ग)). If the justices disagree, the case goes to a Full Bench (r 23(2)(क)).
- **Early final hearing.** If the replies are in and the file is complete, the court may go straight to the final hearing, even when the case was listed only for an interim order (r 49(4)).
- **Open court.** Hearings are in open court (r 80), except cases that must be heard in camera (r 81).
- **Priority.** The court may give priority to cases of public importance or urgent nature (r 73(1)).
- **Missed dates.** If the petitioner misses a date and the time to restore it passes, the petition is put aside as dormant (तामेली) (r 82(क)). This does not apply to PIL. A public-interest case continues even if the petitioner misses a date or dies (r 56(1)).
- **Hearing.** Both sides argue. A party may submit evidence with the bench's permission (r 69). In PIL cases the court may order a study by experts (r 52).

#### Step 8: Decision

After the hearing and arguments, the bench decides the case that day unless it orders otherwise (r 88). If the case raises complex questions, the bench may take up to 15 days to announce its decision (r 90(1)). The full written judgment is normally prepared within 21 days of announcing the decision (r 91(1)).

The decision in a writ case is called an **order** (आदेश). The court may:

- **issue the writ** asked for, in whole or in part. Examples: quash the decision by certiorari, order the duty to be done by mandamus, or order release by habeas corpus;
- issue another appropriate order, such as a directive order;
- **dismiss the petition** (खारेज), as in [Kallu Chaudhary](cases/9-6-kallu-chaudhary.md), [Pradip Kumar Agrawal](cases/9-3-pradip-kumar-agrawal.md) and [Pradip Kumar Prasad Shah Kalwar](cases/9-9-pradip-kumar-prasad-shah-kalwar.md);
- award costs and compensation (r 54); or
- order action against a person who filed a petition for personal gain, from bias or to harass others (r 53).

Any interim order lapses with the final decision (r 49(8)).

#### Step 9: Execution of the order

A writ order is useless if the authority ignores it.

- **Supreme Court.** Where a directive order is issued in a writ petition, or something must be done under the judgment, the branch sends a copy to the **Judgment Execution Directorate** (फैसला कार्यान्वयन निर्देशनालय) (r 127(4), r 129(1)). The Directorate classifies the directions. It writes to each body, through the Office of the Attorney General, saying what must be done and by when (r 130(घ)). It monitors compliance. If an order is not carried out in time and the reason is not satisfactory, it places the matter before the bench (r 130(ङ)). A body that needs more time, for example because a law must be made, may apply through the Attorney General (r 134).
- **High Court.** The court must write to the person or body to carry out the order within three days of the order being prepared. On the petitioner's request, it must follow up and monitor (High Court Rules r 50क).
- **Contempt.** Disobeying a court order is contempt. The Supreme Court may punish anyone who disregards its orders or those of its subordinate courts (Constitution Art 128(4); Administration of Justice Act s 17(1)). The High Court has the same power for its own orders and those of courts below it (s 17(2)).

Execution of judgments in general is Unit 8.

### Special rules for habeas corpus

Habeas corpus protects personal liberty, so its procedure is faster.

| Point | Rule |
|---|---|
| Fee | None (Supreme Court Rules Schedule 3, item 1; High Court Rules r 35(1)) |
| Who files | The detainee, or a relative or concerned person if he cannot (SC r 33(1)) |
| Contents | Who detained him, since when and on what charge; where he is held; whether he is denied food and water or tortured; any earlier petition (SC r 33(2)) |
| First order | If the detention appears unlawful, made with a fixed motive or in bad faith: a show-cause order to reply within at most three days, not counting travel time, and to bring the detainee to court (SC r 34(1)) |
| Without producing the detainee | Allowed if he is unwell, too far away, or for his safety or public order (SC r 34(2)) |
| Meanwhile | Release on personal or money bail, or move him to the court's custody (SC r 35) |
| Search warrant | The court may issue one to find a hidden detainee (r 36; Administration of Justice Act s 27) |
| No transfer | Once the show-cause order is known, the detainee may not be moved without the court's order (SC r 38) |
| Release | If detained in bad faith or against law, or the respondent cannot give a proper reason (SC r 37) |
| False reply | The court frees the detainee and may punish for contempt (SC r 39) |
| Decision | Normally placed for decision within seven days of the written reply (SC r 74(7)) |
| Court holidays | A judge is assigned to hear habeas corpus when courts close for three days or more (Administration of Justice Act s 26) |

[Samir Khan](cases/9-8-samir-khan.md) and [Parshuram Banjade](cases/9-1-parshuram-banjade.md) both followed this pattern: show-cause order, written replies, hearing, and release.

### Public interest petitions

PIL petitions (limb (ख) of r 32(1)) have extra rules:

- The single bench first checks three points (r 45(2)). Is there a real public or community concern? Does the petitioner have a meaningful relation and substantial interest? Is there a constitutional or legal question to settle? Only then does it issue a show-cause order.
- A letter, request or report about serious public harm may be registered as a PIL if the Chief Justice so orders (r 45(3)).
- The petitioner must attend every stage and help the court with continuing research (rr 42, 51(1)).
- A PIL petition cannot be withdrawn (r 51(2)).

## 7.2 Review and revision proceedings

### Why the law allows a second look, and why it limits it

Two ideas pull against each other. The first is **justice**: a judgment that rests on a serious error should not stand. The second is **finality**: a case must end somewhere, or no one could rely on a judgment. The maxim is *interest reipublicae ut sit finis litium*: it is in the public interest that litigation should end.

Section 18 of the Administration of Justice Act states the rule of finality. A decision made by a court, body or officer is final, and every party must obey it, unless the Act or another law says otherwise (s 18(1)). No court may reopen or amend its own decided case in a way that affects the justice of it (s 18(2)). Only minor slips in writing or typing may be corrected, by a separate note giving reasons, and only if they do not affect the decision (s 18(3)).

Review and revision are the narrow exceptions to finality. Both lie only to the Supreme Court. Both need the court's **leave** (अनुमति) before the case is heard again. And both are limited to the grounds the law lists.

### Where the law is

| Source | Review (पुनरावलोकन) | Revision (मुद्दा दोहोर्‍याउने) |
|---|---|---|
| Constitution | Art 133(4): the Supreme Court may review its judgments or final orders; justices other than those who gave the judgment must do so | Art 133(4): the Supreme Court may revise cases, as federal law provides |
| Administration of Justice Act 2073 | s 11 | s 12 |
| Muluki Civil Procedure Code 2074 (Chapter 20) | s 220 | s 219 |
| Both | s 221 (Code): decide only on the grounds admitted; s 13 (Act): compromise allowed; s 74 (Code): extra court fee | |
| Supreme Court Rules 2074 | rr 27, 30, 63 | rr 24(2)(ग), 64 |

The Act covers all cases, civil and criminal. The Code repeats the same rules for civil cases. We found no separate review or revision sections in the Criminal Procedure Code; criminal cases use ss 11-12 of the Act.

The High Court has no power of review or revision. Article 144(3) gives it original, appeal and confirmation powers only. Section 8 of the Act lists its jurisdiction and includes neither.

### Review (पुनरावलोकन)

**Meaning.** Review is the Supreme Court looking again at its **own** judgment or final order, to correct a serious error.

**The general bar.** No court may review a judgment or final order it has already made (Act s 11(1); Code s 220(1)).

**The exception: two grounds.** The Supreme Court may review its own judgment or final order only in two situations (Act s 11(2); Code s 220(2)):

- **(क) New evidence.** Evidence of a fact that would materially affect the justice of the case came to the party's knowledge only after the case was decided. The Code adds: or was discovered in some other way.
- **(ख) Conflict with precedent.** The judgment or final order is contrary to a precedent or legal principle established by the Supreme Court.

**When review is barred.** The Supreme Court will not review a judgment or final order (Act s 11(3); Code s 220(5)):

- (क) that has already been reviewed once;
- (ख) given by a **Larger Full Bench** (बृहत पूर्ण इजलास);
- (ग) given by the **Constitutional Bench**;
- (घ) in a case already decided on revision under the law in force before the Act (or the Code) began; or
- (ङ) in a case looked at under s 12 (Code: s 219), where the Supreme Court upheld the High Court's decision or refused leave for revision.

**Time limit.** The petition must be filed within **60 days** of the date on which the Supreme Court justice signed and authenticated the judgment or final order (Act s 11(4); Code s 220(3)).

**Different justices.** The order granting or refusing leave must be made by justices other than those who gave the first judgment (Act s 11(5); Code s 220(4)). If leave is granted, the review is heard by a bench without the original justices and without those who granted leave (Act s 11(6)). This follows the natural justice rule that no one should judge his own cause. It is also in the Constitution (Art 133(4)).

**Review procedure (Supreme Court Rules).**

1. **Petition.** The party files a petition with a certified copy of the judgment and the supporting evidence. It uses the form in Schedule 5 for ground (क), or Schedule 6 for ground (ख), with the fee (r 63(1)). The fee is Rs 100 (Schedule 3, item 4). The petition must say which ground applies (r 63(2)).
2. **No tarikh.** The petitioner is not kept on tarikh (r 63(3)).
3. **Leave hearing.** The petition goes to justices other than those who gave the judgment (r 63(4)). For ground (क), the bench has the same number of different justices. For ground (ख), it goes up a level: a Joint Bench judgment goes to a Full Bench, and a Full Bench judgment to a Larger Full Bench (r 27(1)-(2)). It may be heard in chamber (r 30). There is no oral argument by the petitioner or his lawyer unless the bench orders it (r 63(5)).
4. **Leave granted.** If review should be granted, the bench gives its reasons and orders leave (r 63(6)). The petitioner is told to come to court (r 63(7)). He pays the court fee, the appeal fee and a notice fee for each respondent (r 63(8)). The petition is then registered like an appeal, with a separate number showing that it came through review (r 63(9)).
5. **Notice.** The respondent is summoned to appear within 15 days, not counting travel time (r 67(1)). He may file a written counter-statement (लिखित प्रतिवाद) (r 68).
6. **Hearing.** A bench without the original justices and the leave-granting justices hears the case (r 27(3); r 63(10)). It decides only on the ground on which review was granted (Code s 221).

### Revision (मुद्दा दोहोर्‍याउने)

**Meaning.** Revision is the Supreme Court looking again at a **High Court's** judgment or final order in a case where **no appeal lies** to the Supreme Court. It exists because appeals to the Supreme Court are limited. Section 9 of the Act allows them only in listed cases, such as cases the High Court tried first or judgments of ten years' prison or more. In all other cases, the High Court's decision would be final. Revision is the safety valve for serious errors in those cases.

**Conditions for revision.** The Supreme Court may revise a High Court judgment or final order, in a case not appealable to it, in four situations (Act s 12(1); Code s 219(1)):

- **(क) Serious error.** The High Court's judgment or final order has a serious constitutional or legal error (गम्भीर संवैधानिक वा कानूनी त्रुटि).
- **(ख) Precedent ignored.** The High Court did not follow a legal principle or precedent established by the Supreme Court, or interpreted and applied it wrongly.
- **(ग) Public property harmed.** In a case involving government or public property, the evidence on the file was not properly evaluated. As a result the property was misappropriated or damaged.
- **(घ) Vulnerable party unrepresented.** A child, a woman, a person with disability, a person suffering from mental illness, or a person aged 75 or more was not properly represented. This materially affected the justice of the case.

**When revision is barred** (Act s 12(2)):

- (क) the Supreme Court has already looked at the case on revision once; or
- (ख) the High Court decided it on appeal under s 8(3)(ख). That is a case where a District Court, on appeal from a judicial or quasi-judicial body, set aside that body's decision in whole or part.

**Time limit and filing.** The application is made within **45 days** of the date the applicant learned of the High Court's judgment. It may be filed through the High Court that decided the case, or directly in the Supreme Court (Act s 12(3); Code s 219(2)). It must attach the prescribed details and documents (Act s 12(4)). The Code adds that these must prove that one of the conditions exists (s 219(3)).

**Leave.** A **Joint Bench** decides whether one of the s 12(1) conditions exists, and grants or refuses leave (Act s 12(5)). The Code puts it this way. The Supreme Court hears whether a condition exists (s 219(4)). If it does, the court orders the other parties to be summoned for the revision (s 219(5)).

**Hearing.** If leave is granted, the case is heard by a bench without the justices who granted leave and without the judge who decided it before (Act s 12(6)). If the other party appears, the court hears him too and decides. His absence does not stop the decision (Code s 219(6) and proviso). The court decides only on the ground on which revision was allowed (Code s 221).

**Revision procedure (Supreme Court Rules).**

1. **Application.** The applicant files in the form in Schedule 7, with the fee (r 64(1)). The fee is Rs 100 (Schedule 3, item 4). He attaches certified copies of the first-instance and High Court judgments, and copies of the documents that support his claim (r 64(1)). He must state a clear ground for revision (r 64(2)).
2. **First hearing.** The application is normally listed within three days of registration (r 74(6)). A Joint Bench hears it (r 24(2)(ग)).
3. **Leave.** If the judgment should be revised, the bench gives reasons and grants leave (r 64(3)).
4. **Fees and registration.** The applicant pays the court fee, the appeal fee and the notice fee (r 64(4)). The application is then registered like an appeal, with a separate number showing that it came through revision (r 64(5)). The court informs the District Court concerned (r 64(6)).
5. **Notice to the respondent.** He is summoned to appear within 15 days, not counting travel time (r 67(1)). He may file a written counter-statement (r 68).
6. **Hearing and judgment.** A Joint Bench hears the revised case (r 24(2)(ग)). It may uphold, reverse or modify the High Court's judgment.
7. **Dormancy.** If the applicant misses a date and the time to restore it passes, before or after leave, the application is put aside (r 82(ख), (घ)).

**Other common rules.** A compromise is allowed in a review or revision case where the law permits one (Act s 13). Once review or revision is granted, the Code charges an extra court fee, worked out from the plaint fee, on the part of the claim being reviewed or revised (s 74).

## Model answers

??? question "2077 Q7(b): Judicial review. (short note)"
    **Meaning.** Judicial review is the power of the courts to examine laws and the acts of the executive and administrative bodies. The court tests them against the Constitution and the law, and strikes down or quashes those that conflict. It rests on the supremacy of the Constitution (Art 1(1)).

    **Kinds.**

    1. Review of legislation: the Supreme Court may declare a law void (Art 133(1)); the Constitutional Bench hears these (Art 137(2)).
    2. Review of administrative and quasi-judicial action: writs of habeas corpus, mandamus, certiorari, prohibition and quo warranto (Arts 133(2)-(3), 144).
    3. Review of judicial decisions: limited; appeal or revision comes first ([Kallu Chaudhary](cases/9-6-kallu-chaudhary.md)).

    **Grounds.** Lack or excess of jurisdiction, error of law, breach of natural justice, mala fide, unreasonableness, unconstitutional law.

    **Courts.** Supreme Court (Arts 133, 137), High Courts (Art 144; Administration of Justice Act s 8(1)). District Courts issue habeas corpus and injunctions under s 7(2)-(3) of the Act.

    **Limit.** A writ for a legal right lies only where no other remedy exists, or it is inadequate or ineffective (Art 133(2)).

    **Conclusion.** Judicial review keeps every organ of the state within the Constitution and protects the citizen against unlawful power.

??? question "2077 Q7(d): Revision. (short note)"
    **Meaning.** Revision (मुद्दा दोहोर्‍याउने) is the Supreme Court looking again at a High Court judgment or final order in a case where no appeal lies to the Supreme Court (Administration of Justice Act s 12; Civil Procedure Code s 219; Constitution Art 133(4)).

    **Conditions (s 12(1)).**

    1. Serious constitutional or legal error in the High Court's judgment.
    2. A Supreme Court precedent or legal principle not followed, or wrongly applied.
    3. Government or public property misappropriated or damaged because evidence was not properly evaluated.
    4. A child, woman, person with disability, mentally ill person or person aged 75+ not properly represented, materially affecting justice.

    **Bars (s 12(2)).** Already revised once; or a High Court appeal under s 8(3)(ख).

    **Procedure.** Within 45 days of learning of the judgment, through the High Court or directly (s 12(3)). A Joint Bench grants or refuses leave (s 12(5)). If granted, the case is registered like an appeal (SC Rules r 64(5)). The respondent is summoned (r 67). A bench without the leave-granting and earlier judges hears it (s 12(6)), only on the admitted ground (Code s 221).

    **Conclusion.** Revision is not a second appeal. It is a narrow door for serious errors in cases the law otherwise leaves final at the High Court.

??? question "2078 Q7(c): Stay order. (short note, old course)"
    **Meaning.** A stay order is an interim order (अन्तरिम आदेश) that stops an act, or keeps things as they are, until the case or petition is decided. It prevents the final decision from becoming useless.

    **In writ petitions.** The petitioner shows that his claim will become meaningless unless the status quo is kept (Supreme Court Rules r 49(1)). Grounds: irreparable loss, serious violation of a fundamental right or legal provision, or balance of convenience (r 49(2)). Forms: for a fixed period or until final decision; until a discussion with the other side; after hearing both sides; conditional, on an undertaking to compensate (r 49(2)(क)-(ङ)). Reasons must be given for granting or refusing (r 49(9)). It lapses at final decision (r 49(8)) and can be cancelled at any time (r 49(7), r 50). High Court Rules r 42 is similar.

    **Example.** In [Pradip Kumar Prasad Shah Kalwar](cases/9-9-pradip-kumar-prasad-shah-kalwar.md) the Supreme Court refused an interim order "for now", as the judgment was already being executed.

    **Civil suits.** Interim orders in civil cases are in the Civil Procedure Code; see Unit 4A.

??? question "Likely short note: Show-cause order in a writ petition"
    **Meaning.** An order of the court asking the respondent to explain, in a written reply within a fixed time, why the writ asked for should not be issued (कारण देखाउ आदेश).

    **When issued.** At the preliminary hearing before a single justice (SC Rules r 25(2)(क)), if the claim appears proper and lawful (r 46(1)). The High Court issues it if a prima facie case appears (High Court Rules r 41(2)).

    **Contents.** The time for the written reply; appearance in person or by representative; and, if asked, the decision on the interim order (r 46(1)).

    **Effect.** The case becomes a contested writ case. The respondent files a written reply with the fee (r 46(3)), and the case goes to a Joint Bench for final hearing (r 24(2)(ख)).

    **Habeas corpus.** The order requires a reply within three days, not counting travel time, and production of the detainee (r 34(1)).

    **Examples.** 15 days in [Pradip Kumar Prasad Shah Kalwar](cases/9-9-pradip-kumar-prasad-shah-kalwar.md); 7 days in [Samir Khan](cases/9-8-samir-khan.md).

??? question "Likely short note: Rejoinder in writ proceedings"
    **Meaning.** In this syllabus, the rejoinder is the respondent's written reply (लिखित जवाफ) to a writ petition or appeal. (2080 Q7(e) asked "rejoinder"; Unit 6 covers it for appeals.)

    **Writ.** Filed within the time in the show-cause order, with a Rs 50 fee (SC Rules r 46(3), Schedule 3). A summary of at most two pages if over three pages (r 48). Time may be extended once, up to 15 days (7 for habeas corpus) (r 55(1)). In habeas corpus, it comes with the detainee within three days (r 34(1)).

    **Contents.** The respondent's answer to each claim, the legal authority for the act, and defences such as alternative remedy, laches and lack of locus standi.

    **Example.** In [Kallu Chaudhary](cases/9-6-kallu-chaudhary.md) the replies argued that appeal or revision was the proper route and that the petition was late. The court accepted both points.

??? question "Likely short note: Review of judgment"
    **Meaning.** The Supreme Court looking again at its own judgment or final order (पुनरावलोकन). No other court may review its own judgment (Administration of Justice Act s 11(1)).

    **Grounds (s 11(2)).** New evidence that materially affects justice, known only after the decision; or a judgment contrary to Supreme Court precedent.

    **Bars (s 11(3)).** Reviewed once; Larger Full Bench; Constitutional Bench; old-law revision cases; s 12 cases upheld or refused.

    **Procedure.** Within 60 days of authentication (s 11(4)). Leave by other justices (s 11(5); r 63(4)). If granted, registered like an appeal (r 63(9)) and heard by a bench without the earlier and leave-granting justices (s 11(6)). Decided only on the admitted ground (Code s 221).

## Full 20-mark answers

These are written the way you would write them in the exam. Each takes about 35 minutes by hand. Learn the structure and the key sentences; you do not need to memorise every word.

??? example "Q1. Whether the writ jurisdiction is available or not when there is existence of alternative remedy? Explain on the basis of the case of Kallu Chaudhary v Appellate Court Dipayal. (2077 Q3, 20 marks)"
    **Introduction**

    A writ is a special order of a superior court that enforces a right or stops an unlawful act. Certiorari quashes an unlawful decision; mandamus orders an authority to do its duty. In Nepal, the Supreme Court and the High Courts issue writs under their extraordinary jurisdiction (असाधारण अधिकारक्षेत्र). It is called extraordinary because it is not the ordinary way to correct a decision. The ordinary ways are appeal and revision, which statutes provide.

    An **alternative remedy** (वैकल्पिक उपचार) is any other legal route that can correct the decision complained of. Examples are an appeal, a revision, or an application to a higher officer. The question is whether a person who has such a route can skip it and come by writ.

    **The constitutional rule**

    Article 46 of the Constitution of Nepal 2015 gives the right to constitutional remedies under Article 133 or 144. Article 133(2) gives the Supreme Court extraordinary power to enforce fundamental rights. It also covers "any other legal right for which no other remedy has been provided for or for which the remedy even though provided appears to be inadequate or ineffective". Article 144(1) gives the High Court the same power. So the Constitution itself makes the absence of an effective remedy a condition for writs about ordinary legal rights.

    The Supreme Court Rules 2074 apply the rule in three places. The registry may refuse to register a writ petition on a matter that, at first sight, can go on appeal or revision (r 18(2)). The petition must show that no alternative remedy exists, or that it is inadequate or ineffective (r 40(1)(घ)). And the court refuses relief at the hearing if the remedy was available and effective.

    **Kallu Chaudhary v Appellate Court, Dipayal (NKP 2060, No. 3/4, D.N. 7209)**

    *Facts.* Kallu farmed land in Kailali registered in Savitri Devi Bhandari's name. He said he was her tenant. He asked the Land Reform Office to divide the land between owner and tenant under the Lands Act 2021. On 2056/5/32 the Office refused, holding that he was not proved to be a lawful tenant. Section 26(च) of the Lands Act allows no appeal against such a decision. Kallu still appealed to the Appellate Court, Dipayal, which dismissed the appeal on 2056/11/23 without hearing its merits. On 2057/7/3 he filed a writ petition in the Supreme Court. He asked for certiorari to quash both decisions, and mandamus to order the division.

    *Issues.* Can a writ quash an Appellate Court judgment when the law gives appeal and revision against it? Having lost his appeal, can Kallu attack the Land Reform Office's decision again by writ? Was he too late?

    *Decision.* A Joint Bench (Justices Dilip Kumar Paudel and Khil Raj Regmi) dismissed the petition on 2060/1/3.

    1. Against an Appellate Court judgment, the route to the Supreme Court was appeal under s 9 or revision under s 12 of the Administration of Justice Act 2048. "Apart from that, it is not a matter for the writ jurisdiction at all." The claim was "meaningless from the outset".
    2. Kallu had already appealed against the Land Reform Office's decision and lost. Judicial certainty does not let a party who fails by one route take another.
    3. Time spent seeking a remedy the law does not provide counts as delay. Kallu came more than a year after the Office's decision, so laches applied.

    *Ratio.* Where the law provides appeal or revision against a judgment, the writ jurisdiction is not available. The same decision cannot be challenged again and again by different routes.

    **Is writ jurisdiction available where an alternative remedy exists?**

    *The general answer is no.* The reasons are these:

    1. The Constitution's own words make the absence of an effective remedy a condition for writs on legal rights (Arts 133(2), 144(1)).
    2. If writs were open whenever a party liked, appeal and revision would lose their meaning. Kallu Chaudhary applies exactly this.
    3. In **[Pradip Kumar Agrawal v Tax Office, Morang](cases/9-3-pradip-kumar-agrawal.md)** (NKP 2052), a Larger Full Bench refused a writ against a tax assessment. The Income Tax Act gave an application and an appeal. A required deposit did not make those remedies ineffective.
    4. In **[Pradip Kumar Prasad Shah Kalwar](cases/9-9-pradip-kumar-prasad-shah-kalwar.md)** (NKP 2073), the law gave a remedy for improper service of a notice, and a court had already dealt with the complaint. The writ jurisdiction could not reopen it.

    *But there are exceptions.* A writ is available despite another remedy:

    1. where the remedy is inadequate or ineffective (Arts 133(2), 144(1));
    2. where a fundamental right is violated, since the "no other remedy" words attach only to other legal rights;
    3. where the decision is void: made without jurisdiction, under an unconstitutional law, by a body without power, or without a hearing against natural justice (Pradip Kumar Agrawal, paragraph 16);
    4. where liberty is at stake. In [Samir Khan](cases/9-8-samir-khan.md), habeas corpus was issued although the government argued that another remedy existed.

    **Critical view**

    The rule is sound. It respects the routes the legislature created and gives finality to decisions. But it can be harsh. Kallu lost without any court deciding whether he was a lawful tenant. The appeal he tried was not a real remedy at all, since s 26(च) barred it, yet the time he spent on it counted against him. The line between an "ineffective" remedy and an inconvenient one is also not always clear; Pradip Kumar Agrawal was decided 5 to 2. Courts should apply the rule firmly against forum-shopping, but not so rigidly that a person is left with no remedy.

    **Nepal's position today**

    The Appellate Courts have been replaced by High Courts (Administration of Justice Act 2073 s 30). Appeal to the Supreme Court is now under s 9 and revision under s 12 of the 2073 Act. Article 88(2) of the 1990 Constitution, which applied when Kallu filed, is now Article 133(2), with almost the same words. Rule 40(1)(घ) asks every writ petition to show why the alternative remedy is missing or ineffective. Laches remains court-made; there is no fixed time limit for a writ.

    **Conclusion**

    Writ jurisdiction is generally not available where the law gives an effective alternative remedy such as appeal or revision. Kallu Chaudhary makes this clear: a writ is not a substitute for appeal or revision. But the writ remains open where no remedy exists, where the remedy is inadequate or ineffective, or where the decision is void. Even then, the petitioner must come promptly by the right route, because time spent on the wrong one counts as delay.

??? example "Q2. What are the existing legal provisions on review and revision? Explain. (2075 Q3, old course, 20 marks)"
    **Introduction**

    A judgment must end a dispute at some point. The maxim *interest reipublicae ut sit finis litium* says it is in the public interest that litigation should end. Section 18 of the Administration of Justice Act 2073 makes a court's decision final and bars the court from reopening it. Yet a judgment may rest on a serious error. So the law allows two narrow ways for the Supreme Court to look again: **review** (पुनरावलोकन) and **revision** (मुद्दा दोहोर्‍याउने).

    **Review** is the Supreme Court looking again at its own judgment or final order. **Revision** is the Supreme Court looking again at a High Court judgment or final order in a case where no appeal lies to the Supreme Court.

    **Sources of law**

    - Constitution of Nepal 2015, Art 133(4): the Supreme Court may revise cases and review its own judgments or final orders, as federal law provides. A review must be made by justices other than those who gave the judgment.
    - Administration of Justice Act 2073: s 11 (review), s 12 (revision), s 13 (compromise).
    - Muluki Civil Procedure Code 2074, Chapter 20: s 219 (revision), s 220 (review), s 221 (limit to the admitted ground); s 74 (court fee).
    - Supreme Court Rules 2074: rr 27, 30, 63 (review); rr 24(2)(ग), 64 (revision); rr 67-68 (notice and counter-statement).

    **Review: legal provisions**

    1. *General bar.* No court may review a judgment or final order it has made (Act s 11(1); Code s 220(1)). So the High Courts and District Courts cannot review.
    2. *Grounds.* The Supreme Court may review its own judgment or final order only if (s 11(2)):
        - (क) evidence of a fact that materially affects justice came to the party's knowledge only after the case was decided; or
        - (ख) the judgment is contrary to a precedent or legal principle established by the Supreme Court.
    3. *Bars.* No review (s 11(3)) where the judgment has already been reviewed once, was given by a Larger Full Bench or by the Constitutional Bench, was a revision decision under the old law, or was a s 12 case where the High Court's decision was upheld or leave refused.
    4. *Time.* Within 60 days of the date the justice signed and authenticated the judgment (s 11(4)).
    5. *Leave.* Justices other than those who decided first grant or refuse leave (s 11(5)). The Rules send a precedent-conflict review up a level: a Joint Bench judgment to a Full Bench, and a Full Bench judgment to a Larger Full Bench (r 27(2)). Leave may be heard in chamber, without oral argument (rr 30, 63(5)).
    6. *Hearing.* If leave is granted, the petition is registered like an appeal (r 63(9)). A bench without the earlier and leave-granting justices hears it (s 11(6); r 63(10)).

    **Revision: legal provisions**

    1. *Scope.* Only High Court judgments or final orders in cases not appealable to the Supreme Court (Act s 12(1); Code s 219(1)).
    2. *Grounds.* (क) serious constitutional or legal error; (ख) a Supreme Court precedent or principle not followed or wrongly applied; (ग) government or public property misappropriated or damaged through improper evaluation of evidence; (घ) a child, woman, person with disability, mentally ill person or person aged 75+ not properly represented, materially affecting justice.
    3. *Bars.* Already revised once; or a High Court appeal decided under s 8(3)(ख) (s 12(2)).
    4. *Time and place.* Within 45 days of learning of the judgment, through the High Court or directly in the Supreme Court (s 12(3)), with the prescribed documents (s 12(4)).
    5. *Leave.* A Joint Bench decides whether a ground exists (s 12(5)). The Code says the court then summons the other party (s 219(5)).
    6. *Hearing.* A bench without the leave-granting and earlier judges (s 12(6)). The court decides even if the other party is absent (Code s 219(6)).

    **Common provisions**

    - The court decides only on the ground on which review or revision was allowed (Code s 221).
    - A compromise is allowed where the law permits (Act s 13).
    - An extra court fee is charged once review or revision is granted (Code s 74).
    - The respondent is summoned within 15 days, not counting travel time, and may file a written counter-statement (rr 67-68).

    **Difference between review and revision**

    | Review | Revision |
    |---|---|
    | Supreme Court's own judgment | High Court's judgment |
    | 2 grounds (s 11(2)) | 4 grounds (s 12(1)) |
    | 60 days from authentication | 45 days from knowledge |
    | Leave by other justices | Leave by a Joint Bench |
    | Code s 220 | Code s 219 |

    **Critical view**

    The present law is clearer than the old one. It lists the grounds, sets time limits, needs leave, and keeps the original judges out. These are good safeguards for impartiality and finality. Some concerns remain. "Serious legal error" in s 12(1)(क) is wide, and may let parties treat revision as a second appeal. The bar on reviewing Larger Full Bench decisions means that even a clear error there cannot be corrected by review. And the Code and the Act describe the leave stage in slightly different words, which can confuse practitioners.

    **Conclusion**

    Review and revision are exceptional remedies, not further appeals. Review lets the Supreme Court correct its own judgment on new evidence or conflict with precedent. Revision lets it correct a High Court judgment that the law would otherwise leave final. Both need leave, both have short time limits, and both are confined to the grounds the law lists. They balance justice with finality.

??? example "Q3. Explain about in which condition the revision petition is to be filed. Write in details about revision proceeding. (2079 Q4, 20 marks)"
    **Introduction**

    Revision (मुद्दा दोहोर्‍याउने) means that the Supreme Court looks again at a case already decided by a High Court. It is available only where the law gives no appeal to the Supreme Court. Appeals to the Supreme Court are limited to the cases listed in s 9 of the Administration of Justice Act 2073. In all other cases the High Court's judgment would be final. Revision is the safety valve. It lets the Supreme Court correct serious errors in those cases.

    Revision is not a second appeal. It is a discretionary remedy, available on limited grounds, with the court's leave.

    **Legal basis**

    - Constitution of Nepal 2015, Art 133(4): the Supreme Court has power to revise cases (मुद्दा दोहोर्‍याउने), as federal law provides.
    - Administration of Justice Act 2073, s 12.
    - Muluki Civil Procedure Code 2074, s 219 (and ss 74, 221).
    - Supreme Court Rules 2074, rr 24(2)(ग), 64, 67, 68, 74(6), 82.

    **Conditions for filing a revision petition**

    *1. The case must not be appealable.* The High Court's judgment or final order must be in a case where no appeal lies to the Supreme Court under the Act or other law (s 12(1)). If an appeal lies, the party must appeal.

    *2. One of four grounds must exist* (s 12(1); Code s 219(1)):

    - **(क) Serious constitutional or legal error.** The High Court's judgment has a serious error of constitutional or legal interpretation. A mere difference of view on evidence is not enough.
    - **(ख) Precedent not followed.** The High Court did not follow a legal principle or precedent established by the Supreme Court, or interpreted and applied it wrongly. This protects Article 128(4), which binds all to the Supreme Court's principles.
    - **(ग) Public property harmed.** In a dispute involving government or public property, the evidence on the file was not properly evaluated, and the property was misappropriated or damaged as a result.
    - **(घ) Vulnerable party not represented.** A child, woman, person with disability, person suffering from mental illness, or person aged 75 or more was not properly represented, and this materially affected the justice of the case.

    *3. The case must not be barred* (s 12(2)):

    - the Supreme Court has not already revised it once; and
    - it is not a case the High Court decided on appeal under s 8(3)(ख). That covers cases where a District Court, on appeal, set aside a judicial or quasi-judicial body's decision.

    *4. Time limit.* The petition must be filed within 45 days of the date the applicant learned of the High Court's judgment (s 12(3); Code s 219(2)).

    *5. Documents.* The petition must attach the prescribed details and documents (s 12(4)). Under the Code, these must prove that a ground exists (s 219(3)).

    **Revision proceeding**

    *Step 1: Filing.* The applicant files the petition through the High Court that decided the case, or directly in the Supreme Court (s 12(3)). He uses the form in Schedule 7 of the Supreme Court Rules, with a Rs 100 fee (r 64(1); Schedule 3). He attaches certified copies of the first-instance and High Court judgments and the supporting documents. He must state a clear ground for revision (r 64(2)).

    *Step 2: Listing.* The petition is normally listed for first hearing within three days of registration (r 74(6)).

    *Step 3: Leave hearing.* A Joint Bench hears the petition (r 24(2)(ग)). It examines whether a ground under s 12(1) exists, and grants or refuses leave (अनुमति) (s 12(5)). If revision is justified, the bench gives reasons and grants leave (r 64(3)). If not, the petition ends and the High Court's judgment stands.

    *Step 4: Fees and registration.* After leave, the applicant pays the court fee, the appeal fee and the notice fee (r 64(4)). Section 74 of the Code charges an extra court fee for a revision granted. The petition is then registered like an appeal, with a separate number showing it came through revision (r 64(5)). The court informs the District Court concerned (r 64(6)).

    *Step 5: Notice to the respondent.* The other party is summoned to appear within 15 days, not counting travel time, using the Schedule 9 form. Copies of the petition and the order go with the notice (r 67(1)-(2); Code s 219(5)).

    *Step 6: Written counter-statement.* The respondent may file a written counter-statement (लिखित प्रतिवाद) with the fee (r 68).

    *Step 7: Hearing.* A bench without the justices who granted leave and without the judge who decided earlier hears the case (s 12(6)). If the respondent appears, he is heard; his absence does not stop the decision (Code s 219(6)). The court is limited to the ground on which revision was allowed (Code s 221).

    *Step 8: Judgment.* The bench may uphold, reverse or modify the High Court's judgment. A compromise is possible where the law allows one (s 13).

    *Step 9: If the applicant drops out.* If he misses a date and the time to restore it passes, before or after leave, the petition is put aside as dormant (r 82(ख), (घ)).

    **Revision and other remedies**

    Revision differs from appeal: appeal is a right, revision needs leave on limited grounds. It differs from review: review is of the Supreme Court's own judgment; revision is of a High Court's. And it is the proper route instead of a writ. In [Kallu Chaudhary](cases/9-6-kallu-chaudhary.md), the Supreme Court held that a writ could not quash an Appellate Court judgment because the law provided appeal or revision.

    **Critical view**

    The present law is an improvement. It lists precise grounds, protects vulnerable parties, and guards public property. Leave filters out weak petitions, and fresh judges protect impartiality. But ground (क), "serious legal error", is broad and can invite routine petitions. The 45-day period runs from knowledge of the judgment, which can be hard to fix. The Code and the Act also describe the leave stage in slightly different words.

    **Conclusion**

    A revision petition may be filed only against a non-appealable High Court judgment, on one of the four grounds in s 12(1), within 45 days, and if no bar in s 12(2) applies. The proceeding has two stages: a leave hearing before a Joint Bench, and, if leave is granted, a full hearing before fresh justices limited to the admitted ground. Revision keeps finality while making sure serious errors do not go uncorrected.

??? example "Q4. What do you mean by judicial review? Discuss about the courts doing judicial review along with its procedure. (2078 Q6, old course, 20 marks)"
    This answer also serves for a question on "the procedure of a writ petition from filing to execution".

    **Introduction**

    **Judicial review** (न्यायिक पुनरावलोकन) is the power of the courts to examine the acts of the legislature, the executive and administrative bodies. The court tests whether they are consistent with the Constitution and the law. If they are not, the court declares them void or quashes them. Judicial review rests on the supremacy of the Constitution. Article 1(1) of the Constitution of Nepal 2015 makes it the fundamental law and says that any inconsistent law is void to that extent.

    Judicial review must not be confused with review (पुनरावलोकन) of a judgment under s 11 of the Administration of Justice Act 2073. That is the Supreme Court correcting its own judgment.

    **Kinds of judicial review**

    1. *Review of legislation.* Any citizen may ask the Supreme Court to declare a federal, provincial or local law void for conflict with the Constitution or a higher law (Art 133(1)). The court may do so from the start or from the date of its decision.
    2. *Review of administrative and quasi-judicial action.* Through writs and orders, the courts quash unlawful decisions and compel lawful action (Arts 133(2)-(3), 144).
    3. *Review of judicial decisions.* Limited. Where appeal or revision lies, the writ is not available ([Kallu Chaudhary](cases/9-6-kallu-chaudhary.md)).

    **Grounds**

    Textbooks group the grounds as illegality, irrationality and procedural impropriety. In practice: lack or excess of jurisdiction (ultra vires), error of law, breach of natural justice, mala fide, unreasonable use of discretion, and action under an unconstitutional law. In [Pradip Kumar Agrawal](cases/9-3-pradip-kumar-agrawal.md), the Supreme Court said it can and must intervene against a decision made without jurisdiction, under an unconstitutional law, or without a hearing.

    **Courts doing judicial review**

    1. *Supreme Court.* The Constitutional Bench (the Chief Justice and four justices, Art 137(1)) hears petitions to declare laws void (Art 137(2)). Other benches exercise the extraordinary jurisdiction under Art 133(2). That covers fundamental rights, other legal rights without an effective remedy, and constitutional or legal questions of public interest. The writs are habeas corpus, mandamus, certiorari, prohibition and quo warranto (Art 133(3)).
    2. *High Courts.* Each High Court has the same writ power for fundamental and legal rights, and for legal questions of public interest (Art 144(1)-(2); Administration of Justice Act s 8(1)). It cannot declare a law void.
    3. *District Courts.* They have no writ jurisdiction under the Constitution. But s 7(2)-(3) of the Act lets them order habeas corpus for anyone unlawfully detained in the district, and issue injunctions where a legal right is harmed or threatened.

    The writ is generally not available where an effective alternative remedy exists (Arts 133(2), 144(1); SC Rules r 40(1)(घ)).

    **Procedure (Supreme Court Rules 2074, Ch 6; High Court Rules 2073, Ch 5)**

    1. *Filing.* The person whose right is affected, or for PIL a person with a meaningful relation and substantial interest, files a petition with a Rs 500 fee. Habeas corpus is free (r 32(1); Schedule 3). The petition states the facts, jurisdiction, the right infringed, why no alternative remedy is adequate, the remedy sought and its grounds (r 40(1)). It must state that no parallel petition is pending in a High Court or District Court (r 43).
    2. *Registration.* The registry may refuse registration (दरपीठ) where the point is settled, the matter is pending, appeal or revision lies, or no constitutional or legal question is raised (r 18(2)).
    3. *Preliminary hearing.* Normally within three days (r 74(6)), before a single justice (r 25(2)(क)). If the claim appears proper and lawful, the court issues a **show-cause order** asking the respondent to reply in writing within a fixed time (r 46(1)).
    4. *Interim order.* If asked, the court must decide it with the show-cause order (r 46(1)). It may grant one for irreparable loss, serious violation of rights or law, or balance of convenience (r 49(2)). It gives reasons either way (r 49(9)).
    5. *Notice.* The show-cause order is served on each respondent (High Court Rules r 47; SC Rules r 148 for other means).
    6. *Written reply (rejoinder).* The respondent files it within time with a Rs 50 fee (r 46(3)). One extension of up to 15 days is possible (r 55(1)).
    7. *Final hearing.* A Joint Bench hears the case in open court (rr 24(2)(ख), 80).
    8. *Decision.* The bench decides after the arguments, or within 15 days if the case is complex (rr 88, 90). It issues the writ, gives another order, or dismisses the petition. It may award costs (r 54).
    9. *Execution.* The Judgment Execution Directorate sends directive orders through the Office of the Attorney General and monitors them (rr 127(4), 130(घ)-(ङ)). The High Court writes to the body within three days and monitors (HC Rules r 50क). Disobedience is contempt (Art 128(4); Act s 17).

    *Habeas corpus* is faster. The respondent must reply and produce the detainee within three days (r 34(1)). The case is normally decided within seven days of the reply (r 74(7)).

    **Critical view**

    Judicial review has made Nepal's courts the guardians of the Constitution, especially through PIL. But there are concerns. Wide public-interest standing can draw the courts into policy matters. Directive orders are often not carried out, which is why the Directorate's monitoring role matters. Delay at the final hearing stage can defeat the remedy, which makes interim orders important but also open to misuse. And the courts must respect the alternative remedy rule, or they will become courts of first resort for every grievance.

    **Conclusion**

    Judicial review is the courts' power to keep laws and official acts within the Constitution. In Nepal, the Supreme Court reviews legislation and both the Supreme Court and the High Courts review executive and administrative action by writ. District Courts have a limited statutory role in habeas corpus and injunctions. The procedure moves from petition to show-cause order, written reply, hearing, decision and monitored execution. Used with restraint, judicial review is the main safeguard of the rule of law.

## One-minute revision

- **Writ basis:** Art 46 (right to remedy), Art 133(2)-(3) (Supreme Court), Art 144 (High Court; public-interest limb covers legal questions only). Art 133(1): laws void, Constitutional Bench (Art 137(2)).
- **Five writs:** habeas corpus, mandamus, certiorari, prohibition, quo warranto, plus other orders (directive orders).
- **Alternative remedy:** legal rights need no other remedy, or an inadequate or ineffective one (Art 133(2)); r 18(2) registration; r 40(1)(घ) petition. Kallu Chaudhary (appeal or revision, not writ; judicial certainty; laches), Pradip Kumar Agrawal (deposit not ineffective; void decisions exception), 9.9 (No. 208 remedy used).
- **Writ steps (SC Rules Ch 6):** petition (r 32, r 40, Rs 500) → registry (r 18) → single justice (r 25) → show-cause order (r 46) + interim order (r 49) → notice → written reply (r 46(3), Rs 50) → Joint Bench (r 24(2)(ख)) → decision (rr 88-91) → Directorate (rr 127, 130). High Court: Ch 5, rr 35-50क.
- **Habeas corpus:** free; reply and produce detainee in three days (r 34); decide within seven days of reply (r 74(7)).
- **Review (s 11; Code s 220):** own Supreme Court judgment; 2 grounds; 60 days from authentication; leave by other justices; barred for Larger Full Bench, Constitutional Bench, reviewed once.
- **Revision (s 12; Code s 219):** non-appealable High Court judgment; 4 grounds (serious error, precedent, public property, vulnerable party); 45 days from knowledge; Joint Bench leave; fresh bench; only admitted ground (Code s 221).
