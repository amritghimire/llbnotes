## Start here: the rules of the game

Think of a football match. One set of rules says what a goal is and what a foul is. Another set says how the match is run: who the referee is, how long each half lasts, how a free kick is taken, and how a team can complain about the referee.

Law has the same two parts.

- **Substantive law** is like the rule on what counts as a goal or a foul. The Muluki Criminal Code 2074 says what robbery is and how it is punished. The Muluki Civil Code 2074 says who owns property and who can inherit it.
- **Procedural law** is like the rule on how the match is run. The Muluki Civil Procedure Code 2074 says how to file a plaint, how the defendant is called, how evidence is taken, and how to appeal.

A right with no way to enforce it is worth little. If someone takes your land, the Civil Code says it is yours. But you get it back only by filing a case in the right court, within the time limit, with the right documents, and by proving your claim. All of that is procedure.

Nepal also has more than one track for running a case. Most cases follow the ordinary track, the **general procedure**. Small cases can take a faster track, the **summary procedure**. Some subjects, such as human trafficking, have a track of their own, a **special procedure**. This unit explains all three.

## 1.1 Meaning, nature and importance of procedural law

### Meaning

The word "procedure" comes from the Latin *procedere*, "to go forward". Procedural law is the law that moves a case forward, step by step, from the first complaint to the final result.

It answers questions like these:

- Which court can hear the case? (jurisdiction)
- Who may file it, and within what time? (standing and limitation)
- How is the other side told about it? (summons and notice)
- How are facts proved, and who must prove them? (evidence and the burden of proof)
- How is the case heard and decided? (hearing and judgment)
- How can the losing side challenge the decision? (appeal, review, revision, writ)
- How is the judgment carried out? (execution)

The two 2074 Procedure Codes describe their own subject in their preambles. The Muluki Civil Procedure Code 2074 amends and consolidates the law on the filing (दायरी), proceedings (कारबाही), hearing (सुनुवाई) and disposal (किनारा) of civil cases, and on carrying out decisions. Its stated aim is to make procedural law simple and suited to the times (सरलीकृत र समयानुकूल). The Muluki Criminal Procedure Code 2074 says the same for criminal cases, and adds investigation (अनुसन्धान) and prosecution (अभियोजन).

### Definitions to learn

| Source | Definition (standard textbook form) | What it stresses |
|---|---|---|
| **Roman law (Gaius)** | All law relates to persons, to things or to actions. The law of actions is the part we now call procedure. | Procedure as a separate branch of law |
| **Jeremy Bentham** | Coined the term "adjective law" for the law of procedure, as against substantive law. | Procedure serves substantive law |
| **T.E. Holland** | Substantive law defines rights; adjective law provides the means of enforcing and protecting them. | Rights vs remedies |
| **John Salmond** | "The law of procedure may be defined as that branch of the law which governs the process of litigation." He called it the law of actions, using "action" widely to cover all legal proceedings, civil and criminal. | Procedure governs litigation |
| **Salmond's test** | Substantive law decides what facts make a wrong; procedural law decides what facts prove a wrong, and how. | A simple test to tell them apart |
| **Supreme Court of Nepal** ([Pasang Dawa Tamang](cases/9-5-pasang-dawa-tamang.md)) | Procedural law does not create anyone's rights. It only fixes and regulates procedure. | Procedure creates no rights |

!!! tip "Exam tip"
    Start with Salmond's definition, add Holland's "rights vs means of enforcing them", and finish with the Supreme Court's line from *Pasang Dawa Tamang*. That gives a jurist, a contrast and a Nepali authority in three sentences.

### What procedural law covers

Procedural law is wider than the two Procedure Codes. Textbooks usually list these parts:

1. **Civil procedure:** how civil cases (property, contract, family, inheritance) are filed and decided.
2. **Criminal procedure:** how crimes are reported, investigated, prosecuted and tried.
3. **Law of evidence:** what counts as proof, and who must prove what.
4. **Limitation:** the time within which a case must be filed.
5. **Jurisdiction and organisation of courts:** which court hears which case (Unit 3).
6. **Appeal, review, revision and writ procedure** (Units 6 and 7).
7. **Execution of judgments** (Unit 8).
8. **Procedure of alternative dispute resolution:** arbitration and mediation (Unit 5).

### Nature of procedural law

1. **It is adjective or instrumental.** It is a means, not an end. Its job is to enforce rights and duties that substantive law creates. On its own, it gives no one a right to land or money.

2. **It is mostly public law.** It governs how a state organ, the court, deals with the parties. Courts apply it even when the parties have not raised it, for example on limitation or jurisdiction.

3. **It answers "how", not "what".** It does not say whether the defendant owes the money. It says how the plaintiff proves that he does, and how the court decides.

4. **It applies to pending cases.** A new procedure usually applies to cases already in court from the day it starts, unless the law says otherwise. The Supreme Court held in *Pasang Dawa Tamang* that filing a case, prosecution and the burden of proof follow the law in force when the case is heard, whenever the complaint was made. Substantive criminal law is the opposite: Art 20(4) of the Constitution bars punishing an act that was not an offence when done.

5. **It is formal, but it serves justice.** Procedure is often called the handmaid of justice, not its mistress. A small slip in procedure should not decide a case when no one was harmed by it. Section 14(1) of the Summary Procedure Act 2028 is an example: using the general procedure in a case meant for the summary procedure does not, by that alone, make the proceedings void. The Supreme Court applied it in [Tripura Devi Jha](cases/9-4-tripura-devi-jha.md).

6. **Some rules are mandatory, some are not.** Breaking a mandatory rule makes the step void. Section 6(4) of the Summary Procedure Act says that if the defendant was not given copies of the complaint and the documents, the proceedings are void. Other rules are directory: a court should follow them, but a breach does not undo the case by itself.

7. **It is mainly written down.** Nepal's procedure is in Acts and rules, not in custom. Since 1 Bhadra 2075 the two 2074 Procedure Codes are the main sources (CPC s 1(2); CrPC s 1(2)).

8. **General procedure fills gaps.** Where a special law sets its own procedure, that law applies. On any point it does not cover, the general Code applies (CPC s 3; CrPC s 3; Summary Procedure Act s 14(2)).

9. **It rests on the Constitution.** Article 20 gives the accused rights to be told the grounds of arrest, to a lawyer, to be presented before the adjudicating authority within 24 hours, to be presumed innocent, not to be tried twice for one offence, not to be forced to testify against oneself, to know of proceedings, and to a fair trial by an independent, impartial and competent court (Art 20(1)-(9)). Ordinary procedure must fit inside these guarantees.

### Importance of procedural law

1. **It turns rights into remedies.** The maxim *ubi jus ibi remedium* says that where there is a right there is a remedy. Procedure is the road from the right to the remedy. Without it, a right on paper cannot be used.

2. **It secures a fair trial.** Art 20(9) of the Constitution gives everyone the right to a fair trial by an independent, impartial and competent court. Rules on notice, hearing both sides (audi alteram partem) and giving reasons make this right real. The Muluki Criminal Code 2074 repeats it: no one shall be denied a fair hearing (s 10).

3. **It treats the parties equally.** The parties have equal status, and the court must treat each of them equally. Even the government or a legal person cannot claim special status or immunity in the proceedings, unless the law or the court provides otherwise (CPC s 9).

4. **It keeps justice open.** Cases are heard in open court unless the law or a court order says otherwise (CPC s 5). Open hearing lets the public see that justice is done. Exceptions protect victims, as in in camera hearings for trafficking cases (Human Trafficking Act 2064 s 27).

5. **It gives certainty.** Everyone knows in advance the steps, the time limits and the court. Lawyers can advise their clients, and like cases get like treatment.

6. **It controls power.** Procedure limits what police, prosecutors and judges can do. A person arrested must be presented before the adjudicating authority (मुद्दा हेर्ने अधिकारी) within 24 hours, not counting travel time (Art 20(3)). A judge may not hear a case in which he or a close relative has an interest (CPC s 271).

7. **It brings speed and finality.** Time limits, limitation and res judicata stop cases from dragging on or being reopened (Unit 2). The Summary Procedure Act exists so that small cases are decided quickly (its preamble).

8. **It protects the weak.** Free legal aid for those who cannot pay (Art 20(10)), and the guardian's right to act for a minor or a person of unsound mind (CPC s 267), keep the courts open to people who could not use them alone.

9. **It makes judgments work.** Execution rules (Unit 8) make sure the winning party actually gets the land, money or relief.

10. **It builds trust in courts.** People accept a decision they lost when they believe the process was fair. That trust supports the rule of law.

### When procedure gets in the way

Procedure can also defeat justice:

- **Technicality.** A good claim can fail for a missed date, a wrong form or a filing in the wrong court.
- **Delay.** Many steps, adjournments and appeals can keep a case alive for years. Justice delayed is justice denied.
- **Cost and complexity.** Poor people cannot easily follow complex rules or pay lawyers.
- **Abuse.** A party can use procedure to delay, for example by avoiding notice.

The answer is not less procedure but better procedure. Courts lean towards substance over form where no one is harmed, as in *Tripura Devi Jha*. Lawmakers create shorter tracks for small cases, as in the Summary Procedure Act. Recent reforms such as continuous hearing and the use of IT are in Unit 2.

## 1.2 Comparison between procedural and substantive law

### Substantive law

Substantive law is the law that defines rights, duties and liabilities. It says what a person may do, must do, or must not do, and what follows if he breaks the rule. It is called "substantive" because it deals with the substance of a dispute: who is right.

Examples in Nepal:

- **Muluki Civil Code 2074** (Act No. 34 of 2074): property, contract, family, inheritance, torts. Its preamble says it amends and consolidates the civil provisions of the Muluki Ain and other laws.
- **Muluki Criminal Code 2074** (Act No. 36 of 2074): offences and punishments. For example, s 286 defines robbery (लुटपिट) and punishes it with up to three months' prison, or a fine up to 25 per cent of the value taken, or both. The offender must also repay the victim the value taken.

The matching procedure codes are the **Muluki Civil Procedure Code 2074** (Act No. 35) and the **Muluki Criminal Procedure Code 2074** (Act No. 37). All four came into force on 1 Bhadra 2075. Under the old Muluki Ain, substantive chapters (such as the chapter on trafficking in persons) and the procedural chapter on court procedure (Adalati Bandobasta) sat in one book. The 2074 Codes split them into separate Acts.

### The differences

| Point | Substantive law | Procedural law |
|---|---|---|
| Meaning | Creates and defines rights, duties, offences and punishments | Sets out how those rights and duties are enforced in court |
| Question it answers | What are my rights? Is this act a crime? | How do I enforce my right? How is the accused tried? |
| Ends or means | The end that justice aims at | The means to reach that end |
| Salmond's test | What facts make a wrong | What facts prove a wrong, and how |
| Rights and remedies | Rights and duties | Remedies and the steps to get them |
| Independence | Can exist on its own | Exists to serve substantive law (adjective law) |
| Where it works | In daily life, inside and outside court | Mainly in courts and other bodies that decide cases |
| Who it addresses | Everyone in society | Mainly courts, parties, lawyers, police and prosecutors |
| Effect in time | Usually forward-looking; in criminal law, no punishment for an act not punishable when done (Art 20(4); Criminal Code s 7) | Applies to pending cases from the day it starts, unless the law says otherwise (*Pasang Dawa Tamang*) |
| Nepal's main Acts | Muluki Civil Code 2074; Muluki Criminal Code 2074 | Muluki Civil Procedure Code 2074; Muluki Criminal Procedure Code 2074; Evidence Act 2031; Summary Procedure Act 2028 |
| Example | Robbery is an offence (Criminal Code s 286) | An appeal lies within 30 days of learning of the judgment (CPC s 205(1)) |

### How they work together

The two are not rivals. They are two sides of one coin.

- **Substantive law needs procedure.** A right that cannot be enforced is close to no right at all.
- **Procedure needs substantive law.** A procedure with no right to enforce has nothing to do.
- **Procedure serves substance.** When the two clash, courts try not to let a technical rule defeat a real right. In *Tripura Devi Jha*, the Supreme Court refused to set aside an appeal only because the wrong procedure had been used. It then decided the case on the evidence.

### Grey areas: where the line is hard to draw

Some rules are hard to place. Learn these examples; they show you understand the topic.

1. **Burden of proof.** Who must prove a fact often decides who wins. Yet in *Pasang Dawa Tamang* the Supreme Court treated the burden of proof as procedure. Under the Evidence Act 2031, the prosecution must prove the offence in a criminal case (s 25), and the plaintiff must prove his claim in a civil case (s 26). A special Act may shift it: the Human Trafficking and Transportation (Control) Act 2064 makes the accused prove that he did not commit the offence (s 9). This is a reverse burden.

2. **Limitation.** The rule that a plaint must be filed within the limitation period is in the Civil Procedure Code (s 47). But many of the periods themselves sit in the chapters of the Civil Code, next to the rights they limit (for example ss 29, 41 and 53). Limitation does not destroy the right; it bars the remedy. Most writers treat it as procedural, but its effect on the party is substantive.

3. **Right of appeal.** The steps of an appeal are procedural. But whether a party may appeal at all is usually treated as a substantive right, because taking it away changes the party's position.

4. **Jurisdiction.** Which court hears a case is part of procedure. Yet a decision by a court without jurisdiction is void, so the rule has very strong effects.

5. **Mixed Acts.** One Act can hold both kinds. The Human Trafficking Act 2064 bans trafficking (s 3), defines it (s 4) and punishes it (s 15): that is substantive. It also sets out who may complain (s 5), how the victim's statement is attested (s 6), who bears the burden of proof (s 9) and in camera hearing (s 27): that is procedural. The Muluki Criminal Code 2074 is a substantive Code, but it also states procedural guarantees: no one tried twice for one offence (s 9), fair hearing (s 10), no compulsion to testify against oneself (s 11), and presumption of innocence (s 12).

!!! tip "Exam tip"
    In a "compare" question, the table alone is not enough. Add the grey areas and *Pasang Dawa Tamang*. That is what lifts a 12-mark answer to 16 or more.

### When the law changes: which law applies?

The difference matters most when the law changes while a case is under way.

**Substantive criminal law is not retrospective.** Article 20(4) of the Constitution says no one shall be punished for an act that was not punishable by the law in force when it was done. Nor shall anyone get a greater punishment than the law then provided. Section 7 of the Muluki Criminal Code 2074 says the same.

**Procedural law applies to pending cases.** The court follows the procedure in force when it acts. A party has no fixed right to a particular procedure. Steps already taken properly under the old procedure usually stay valid.

**The case: [Pasang Dawa Tamang (Lopchan) v HMG](cases/9-5-pasang-dawa-tamang.md)** (NKP 2058, No. 3, p. 188; D.N. 6992; decided 2057/10/19).

- **Facts.** Women from Rasuwa said Pasang took them to Bombay on 2042/1/1 and sold them there. He was charged and convicted under the Human Trafficking (Control) Act 2043, which came into force on 2043/7/24. The trial court gave him 10 years' imprisonment, and the Appellate Court, Patan upheld it.
- **Argument.** Pasang said he was punished under a law made after the act, and that the new Act wrongly made him prove his innocence.
- **Held: substantive side.** The old Muluki Ain chapter on trafficking in persons already made selling a person a crime, with 20 years' imprisonment. The 2043 Act gave 10 to 20 years. Pasang got 10. So the new Act did not punish an act the old law left unpunished, and did not punish him more heavily.
- **Held: procedural side.** Filing a case, prosecution and the burden of proof are procedural. Procedural law creates no rights; it only regulates procedure. So the procedure in force when the case is heard applies, whenever the complaint was made. The 2043 Act's rule making the accused disprove the victim's statement therefore applied.
- **Comment.** The ruling draws the line clearly. But treating the burden of proof as mere procedure is open to question, because it shifted the risk of losing onto the accused after the event. It also sits uneasily with Art 20(5) of today's Constitution, which presumes the accused innocent until proved guilty.

## 1.3 General, summary and special procedures

### The three at a glance

A case in Nepal can travel on one of three tracks.

| Track | Nepali | Set by | Used for |
|---|---|---|---|
| General procedure | सामान्य कार्यविधि | Muluki Civil Procedure Code 2074; Muluki Criminal Procedure Code 2074 | Every case, unless another law sets a different track |
| Summary procedure | संक्षिप्त कार्यविधि | Summary Procedure Act 2028 | Small cases, and the cases listed in its Schedule 1 |
| Special procedure | विशेष कार्यविधि | A particular Act, such as the Special Court Act 2059 or the Human Trafficking Act 2064 | The cases under that Act |

!!! note "Simple procedure in the old course"
    The 2078 old-course paper asks about the "simple procedure". It means the ordinary procedure, सामान्य कार्यविधि, which this page calls the general procedure. The Nepali word सामान्य can be put into English as "general", "ordinary" or "simple".

### General procedure

**Meaning.** The general procedure is the ordinary, full set of steps that most cases follow. Today it is in the Muluki Civil Procedure Code 2074 for civil cases and the Muluki Criminal Procedure Code 2074 for criminal cases. Before 1 Bhadra 2075 it was mainly in the Adalati Bandobasta chapter of the Muluki Ain.

**Main steps in a civil case** (details in Unit 4A):

1. The plaintiff files a plaint (फिरादपत्र) within the limitation period (CPC s 47).
2. The court sends a summons (म्याद) to the defendant.
3. The defendant files a reply (प्रतिउत्तरपत्र) within 21 days of service, not counting travel time (CPC s 119(1)).
4. The parties attend on the dates (तारिख) the court fixes.
5. The court takes evidence, hears both sides, and gives judgment.
6. A party may appeal within 30 days of learning of the judgment (CPC s 205(1)).
7. The judgment is executed.

In a criminal case the steps begin with a report of the crime and police investigation, then a charge sheet and trial (Unit 4B).

**Features.**

- **Default and residual.** It applies to every case unless another law says otherwise. It also fills gaps in the summary and special procedures (CPC s 3(2); CrPC s 3(2); Summary Procedure Act ss 12 and 14(2)).
- **Full and formal.** Written pleadings, formal service of notice, full evidence, hearing and reasoned judgment.
- **Longer time limits.** For example, 21 days for the reply, and restoration of a lapsed time limit for up to 15 days once, or of a date up to twice for up to 21 days, when the cause was beyond the party's control (CPC s 223).
- **Safeguards.** Open court (CPC s 5), equal status of the parties (CPC s 9), and rules against a judge hearing a case in which he has an interest (CPC s 271).

**Merits.** It is thorough, so mistakes are fewer. Both sides get a full chance to be heard. It suits complex and high-value disputes.

**Demerits.** It is slow and costly. Many dates and adjournments delay justice. It is heavy for a small claim, where the cost of the case can exceed the value in dispute.

### Summary procedure

**Meaning.** The summary procedure is a shorter, faster procedure for small and simple cases. It cuts the steps and the time limits. In Nepal it is set by the **Summary Procedure Act 2028** (संक्षिप्त कार्यविधि ऐन, २०२८), Act No. 17 of 2028.

**Purpose.** The preamble says it is desirable to adopt a summary procedure in small cases (साना मुद्दा) so that they are decided quickly.

**History.** The Act received the royal seal and was published on 2028/12/20. By a notice in the Nepal Gazette of 2029/10/1, it came into force throughout Nepal from 2030/1/1. It has been amended several times, most recently by the 2074 Act that amended, consolidated and repealed certain laws, and by the 2075 Act amending laws for the Muluki Codes (certified 2076/01/02). Those amendments link it to the 2074 Codes.

**Which courts use it.** "Court" in the Act means the Supreme Court, a High Court or a District Court. It also includes any other court, judicial body or officer that the law empowers to decide particular cases (s 2(ग)). The procedure applies both at trial and on appeal (s 3(1)).

#### Which cases go by the summary procedure (s 3 and the Schedules)

Under s 3(1), the Act's procedure must be followed in cases filed after it came into force, at trial and on appeal, if the case is:

- **(a) listed in Schedule 1**, or
- **(b) a small case:** a civil case where the value or claim (मोल वा बिगो) is up to Rs 1,000, or a criminal case punishable by a fine up to Rs 1,000, or up to six months' prison, or both. Cases listed in Schedule 2 are left out of this clause.

**Schedule 1** (cases that always go by the summary procedure) lists cases under:

1. the Nepal Agency Act 2014;
2. the Radio Act 2014;
3. the Patent, Design and Trademark Act 2022;
4. the Association Registration Act 2034 (संस्था दर्ता ऐन);
5. s 30 of the Standard Measurement and Weight Act 2025;
6. s 7 of the Lottery Act 2025 (चिठ्ठा ऐन);
7. s 17 of the Education Act 2028;
8. s 46 of the Tourism Act 2035;
9. the Labour Act 2048;
10. cases within the jurisdiction of the judicial committee of a rural municipality or municipality under prevailing law;
11. the Excise Act 2058;
12. the Copyright Act 2059;
13. the Companies Act 2063;
14. the Muluki Civil Code 2074: (a) pre-emption of immovable property (हकसफा) under Part 4, Chapter 13; (b) wages (ज्याला मजुरी) under Part 5, Chapter 14; (c) a lump sum or expenses in place of a share on divorce, under s 100; (d) food, clothing, education and health care according to status and income, under s 211; (e) removing a tenant contrary to s 401;
15. s 201 of the Muluki Criminal Code 2074: keeping someone in detention without the minimum humane facilities;
16. any case that prevailing law says must follow the Summary Procedure Act.

**Schedule 2** (cases kept out of the small-case clause, even if small):

1. any other case about immovable property, except those in Schedule 1;
2. sandhi sarpan (सन्धिसर्पन) cases, about rights between neighbouring properties;
3. partition or inheritance (अंश वा अपुताली);
4. establishing a family relationship (नाता सम्बन्ध कायम);
5. rape (करणी);
6. theft of any kind;
7. fraud or forgery, except when raised inside a case under s 3(3).

(Item 8 was struck out by the 2048 amendment.)

Why exclude these? They are serious, or they concern land, family status or inheritance. A wrong decision on them does lasting harm, even when the money value is small. So they need the full procedure.

#### Special procedures are not affected (s 3(2))

Whatever s 3(1) says, if another Act sets a special procedure for a case, nothing in the Summary Procedure Act affects it (s 3(2)). So a special procedure wins over the summary procedure.

#### Steps and time limits

| Step | Summary Procedure Act 2028 | Section |
|---|---|---|
| Starting the case | A complaint (उजुरी) includes a plaint, any other complaint, claim or counter-claim, and in criminal cases the charge sheet or report. A person may complain with evidence even without a formal plaint (बिरादपत्र); the court takes his statement if needed and proceeds | ss 2(क), 4 |
| Preliminary check | The court checks whether the claim or charge has a basis in law, and whether the evidence is relevant and enough. It may dismiss the complaint, giving reasons, if it cannot proceed or lacks credible evidence | s 5(1)-(2) |
| Defendant present | The court takes his statement at once. If he wants to file a written reply instead, he gets 7 days | s 5(3)(क) |
| Defendant absent | The court issues a summons or notice (इतलायनामा) within 3 days | s 5(3)(ख) |
| Court acting on its own | Where the law requires the court to act itself, it records the defendant's name and address, the reason and the evidence, then proceeds | s 5(4) |
| Copies to the defendant | The defendant must get copies of the complaint and the documents. If he does not, the proceedings are void | s 6, especially s 6(4) |
| Summons | In the form in Schedule 3, served by the usual rules. Service must be done within 2 days, not counting travel; an employee who delays without reason can be fined up to Rs 25 | s 7 |

| Deciding the case | Within 90 days of the reply or statement, or of the end of the time for it. If ready earlier, within 3 days. But not before the times under ss 8 and 9 have passed | s 10 |
| Missing dates | Once both sides' evidence is in, a party may skip dates with the court's permission. After the plaintiff's evidence is in, the case is not dismissed because a party misses a date; it is decided on the evidence | s 11 |
| Appeal | Within 30 days of learning of the judgment, subject to prevailing law | s 11क |
| Deciding the appeal | Within 90 days of the file reaching the appeal court; within 3 days if ready earlier | s 11ख |
| Fraud, forgery or coercion raised | The court decides that question in the same case, after following CPC s 172 or CrPC s 104 | s 3(3) |

#### Moving between the tracks (s 12)

- If, as the case goes on, it turns out not to be a summary case, the court records its reasons in a note (पर्चा) and follows the general procedure for the rest of the case (s 12(1)).
- If part of a case falls under the Act and part needs the procedure of other law, the whole case follows the other law's procedure (s 12(2)).

#### The saving clause (s 14) and *Tripura Devi Jha*

Section 14(1): in a case that needed this Act's procedure, the court may have followed the general procedure of other Nepal law instead. If so, what was done is not void for that reason alone. Section 14(2): on procedural points the Act and its rules do not cover, prevailing Nepal law applies.

**The case: [Tripura Devi Jha v Kali Devi Jha et al.](cases/9-4-tripura-devi-jha.md)** (NKP 2058, No. 3, p. 211; D.N. 6996; Joint Bench; decided 2057/10/6).

- **Facts.** Tripura Devi Jha said the defendants beat her and carried off goods worth Rs 1,405 from her house in Dhanusha. The District Court held the robbery proved. The Appellate Court, Janakpur reversed it. In the Supreme Court she argued that the defendants' appeal had been filed late. A Joint Bench called the respondents because the Appellate Court might have gone wrong on the time limits in ss 11क and 8 of the Summary Procedure Act.
- **Held.** Under s 14, using the general procedure in a summary case does not, by that alone, make the proceedings void. The Appellate Court had registered and decided the appeal under the general procedure, so there was no ground to set it aside on the time-limit point. On the evidence, the robbery was proved, and the Court reversed the Appellate Court.
- **Point for this unit.** A slip in choosing the procedure does not by itself undo a case. Procedure serves justice; it is not an end in itself.
- **Comment.** The reasoning on procedure is short. The Court did not say why the case was a summary case, or whether the appeal was in fact late. A time limit decides whether a right of appeal exists at all, so it is arguable that s 14 should not save a late appeal. Our reading: robbery under today's Criminal Code s 286 carries up to three months' prison or a fine of up to 25 per cent of the value, so a small robbery like this one would fit the small-case clause of s 3(1)(ख). Robbery is not theft (s 286(2) leaves theft out), so the Schedule 2 exclusion for theft would not apply. The judgment itself does not explain this.

#### Merits of the summary procedure

1. **Speed.** Fixed limits: 7 days to answer, 90 days to decide, 90 days for the appeal.
2. **Low cost.** Fewer steps and dates mean less expense for small claims.
3. **Less formality.** A complaint can be made without a formal plaint (s 4), and the defendant's statement can be taken at once.
4. **Less room for delay.** Very limited restoration of lapsed time (s 8), and no dismissal for missing dates after the evidence is in (s 11).
5. **Fairness kept.** Copies to the defendant are mandatory (s 6(4)), and an absent defendant's family can still put in evidence (s 9).
6. **Reduces backlog.** Small cases are cleared fast, so courts can give time to serious ones.

#### Demerits and criticism

1. **Old money limits.** The current text of s 3(1)(ख) still uses Rs 1,000 and six months, even though the 2074 and 2075 amendments updated the Act for the new Codes. Today very few civil claims are worth Rs 1,000 or less, so the small-case clause does little. Most of the Act's work now comes from Schedule 1.
2. **Time limits without teeth.** The Act sets 90 days to decide, but it gives no consequence if the court takes longer.
3. **Speed against fairness.** Seven days may be too short for a poor or distant defendant to find a lawyer and evidence.
4. **Old names in Schedule 1.** The Schedule lists many Acts by their original names and years. A reader must check each against current law.
5. **Uncertainty on which track applies.** As *Tripura Devi Jha* shows, courts sometimes use the general procedure in a summary case. Section 14 saves the proceedings, but the time limits then become unclear.

### Special procedure

**Meaning.** A special procedure is one that a particular Act sets for the cases under it, in place of, or in addition to, the general procedure. The Act that does this is often called a special Act (विशेष ऐन).

**Legal basis.**

- The Civil Procedure Code says procedure written in separate laws on particular subjects applies, and the Code does not affect it. The Code fills only the gaps (CPC s 3(1)-(2)).
- The Criminal Procedure Code says the same for the investigation, prosecution, jurisdiction, hearing, execution or other steps of an offence (CrPC s 3(1)-(2)).
- The Summary Procedure Act does not affect a special procedure (s 3(2)).
- For offences, the Muluki Criminal Code says a special Act's provisions apply where that Act makes an act a separate offence (s 5).

**Why special procedures exist.**

- Some cases need speed, such as cases given to a Special Court.
- Some victims need protection, such as victims of trafficking.
- Some subjects need expert bodies or a local, mediation-first approach, such as disputes before a local judicial committee.
- Some offences are hard to prove by ordinary rules, so the law shifts the burden of proof.

**Examples in Nepal.**

| Act | Special feature | Section |
|---|---|---|
| Special Court Act 2059 | The government, on the Judicial Council's advice, sets up a Special Court by Gazette notice to decide particular kinds of cases quickly and effectively | s 3(1) |
| | Summons: up to 15 days for a defendant in Nepal, up to 30 days if abroad, not counting travel time | s 10(1) |
| | A lapsed time limit or date can be restored once, for at most 15 days, if the cause was beyond the party's control | s 11 |
| | On matters not in the Act, the Special Court uses the powers and procedure of a District Court | s 12 |
| | The case should normally be decided within six months of filing; an appeal in the Supreme Court within three months of the file arriving | s 16 |
| | Appeal to the Supreme Court within 35 days of the judgment | s 17 |
| Human Trafficking and Transportation (Control) Act 2064 | The police take the victim's statement and bring her to the nearest District Court, where a judge attests it; the court may use it as evidence even if she does not come later | s 6 |
| | The accused must prove he did not commit the offence (reverse burden) | s 9 |
| | Hearing in a closed court (in camera) | s 27 |
| | Cases are government cases, counted in Schedule 1 of the Criminal Procedure Code | s 28 |
| Local Government Operation Act 2074 | A judicial committee must, as far as possible, encourage mediation and get the parties to settle by agreement (milapatra, मिलापत्र) | s 49(2) |
| | Its other procedure (registration, notice, time to decide) is set by local law | s 49(12) |
| | Appeal to the District Court within 35 days of learning of the decision | s 51 |

**Merits.** The procedure fits the subject. Victims are protected. Cases are decided faster by bodies that know the field.

**Demerits.** Many special procedures make the law scattered and hard to know. Some depart from basic guarantees: a reverse burden, as in s 9 of the 2064 Act, sits uneasily with the presumption of innocence in Art 20(5) of the Constitution. Parallel courts and bodies can confuse people about where to go.

**Case link.** In *Pasang Dawa Tamang*, s 11 of the Human Trafficking (Control) Act 2043 made it prevail over the Muluki Ain chapter on its subject. The Supreme Court said the Act had the place of a special Act, and its procedure could make an exception to the general procedure. So its reverse burden applied.

### How the three fit together

When a case comes to court, ask these questions in order:

1. **Does a special Act set a procedure for this case?** If yes, follow it (CPC s 3(1); CrPC s 3(1); Summary Procedure Act s 3(2)).
2. **If not, is it a summary case?** That is, is it in Schedule 1, or is it a small case under s 3(1)(ख) and not in Schedule 2? If yes, follow the Summary Procedure Act.
3. **Otherwise, use the general procedure.**
4. **On any gap** in a special or summary procedure, use the general procedure (CPC s 3(2); CrPC s 3(2); Summary Procedure Act s 14(2)).
5. **If the wrong track was used**, the proceedings are not void for that reason alone where the general procedure was used in a summary case (Summary Procedure Act s 14(1); *Tripura Devi Jha*).

??? question "Likely short note: Retrospective effect of procedural law"
    **Rule.** Substantive criminal law cannot punish an act that was not an offence when done, or punish more than the law then allowed (Constitution Art 20(4); Criminal Code s 7). Procedural law applies to pending cases from the day it starts, unless the law says otherwise.

    **Why.** Procedure creates no rights. No one has a right to be tried by an old procedure.

    **Case.** *Pasang Dawa Tamang v HMG* (NKP 2058): filing, prosecution and the burden of proof follow the law in force when the case is heard. The later Act did not harm the accused because the old law already punished the act, more heavily.

    **Limits.** Steps already taken validly under the old procedure stay valid. A rule that changes the outcome, such as the burden of proof or a right of appeal, is arguably substantive and should not reach back.

??? example "Q1. Compare between procedural law and substantive law in detail. (2079 Q1, 20 marks)"
    **Introduction**

    Every legal system has two kinds of rules. One kind says what our rights and duties are. The other says how those rights and duties are enforced when someone breaks them. The first is **substantive law** (सारवान कानून). The second is **procedural law** (कार्यविधि कानून), also called adjective law.

    Holland put the difference simply. Substantive law defines rights; adjective law provides the means of enforcing and protecting them. Salmond defined the law of procedure as "that branch of the law which governs the process of litigation". He gave a test: substantive law decides what facts make a wrong, while procedural law decides what facts prove a wrong, and how. The Supreme Court of Nepal has said that procedural law does not create anyone's rights; it only fixes and regulates procedure (*Pasang Dawa Tamang v HMG*, NKP 2058).

    **Substantive law**

    Substantive law creates, defines and regulates rights, duties, liabilities, offences and punishments. It governs people's conduct in daily life, inside and outside court. Examples in Nepal are the Muluki Civil Code 2074, on property, contract, family and inheritance, and the Muluki Criminal Code 2074, on offences and punishments. For example, s 286 of the Criminal Code makes robbery an offence, with up to three months' prison, or a fine of up to 25 per cent of the value taken, or both.

    **Procedural law**

    Procedural law sets out the steps by which a court enforces those rights and duties. It covers jurisdiction, filing a case, limitation, notice, evidence, hearing, judgment, appeal and execution. In Nepal the main sources are the Muluki Civil Procedure Code 2074 and the Muluki Criminal Procedure Code 2074, both in force from 1 Bhadra 2075. Others are the Evidence Act 2031, the Summary Procedure Act 2028, the Administration of Justice Act 2073 and the court rules. For example, s 205(1) of the Civil Procedure Code lets a party appeal within 30 days of learning of the judgment.

    **Differences**

    1. **Nature.** Substantive law is the end that justice aims at. Procedural law is the means to reach it.
    2. **Question answered.** Substantive law answers "what": what are my rights, is this a crime. Procedural law answers "how": how do I enforce my right, how is the accused tried.
    3. **Rights and remedies.** Substantive law creates rights and duties. Procedural law provides remedies and the steps to get them.
    4. **Independence.** Substantive law can exist on its own. Procedural law exists to serve it.
    5. **Field.** Substantive law works in daily life. Procedural law works mainly in courts and bodies that decide cases.
    6. **Addressees.** Substantive law speaks to everyone. Procedural law speaks mainly to courts, parties, lawyers, police and prosecutors.
    7. **Effect in time.** Substantive law usually looks forward. In criminal law, Art 20(4) of the Constitution and s 7 of the Criminal Code forbid punishing an act that was not an offence when done, or punishing more than the law then allowed. Procedural law applies to pending cases from the day it starts, unless the law says otherwise.
    8. **Salmond's test.** What facts make a wrong is substantive. What facts prove it is procedural.
    9. **Codes in Nepal.** The 2074 reform split them: the Civil Code and the Criminal Code are substantive; the Civil Procedure Code and the Criminal Procedure Code are procedural. Under the old Muluki Ain, both kinds sat in one book, with court procedure in the Adalati Bandobasta chapter.

    **Relationship**

    The two are interdependent. A right with no procedure to enforce it is of little use; a procedure with no right behind it has nothing to do. Procedure is the handmaid of justice, not its mistress. So when a technical rule would defeat a real right, courts lean towards substance. In *Tripura Devi Jha v Kali Devi Jha* (NKP 2058), the Supreme Court used s 14 of the Summary Procedure Act 2028 to refuse to set aside an appeal only because the general procedure had been used in a summary case. It then decided the case on the evidence.

    **Grey areas**

    - **Burden of proof.** The Evidence Act 2031 puts it on the prosecution in criminal cases (s 25) and on the plaintiff in civil cases (s 26). The Supreme Court treated it as procedural in *Pasang Dawa Tamang*. But who must prove a fact often decides who wins.
    - **Limitation.** The rule that a plaint must be filed within limitation is in the Civil Procedure Code (s 47), but many periods sit in the Civil Code chapters (for example ss 29, 41, 53). It bars the remedy, not the right.
    - **Right of appeal.** The steps are procedural, but the right to appeal is usually treated as substantive.
    - **Mixed Acts.** The Human Trafficking Act 2064 defines and punishes trafficking (ss 3, 4, 15) and also sets procedure (ss 6, 9, 27). The Criminal Code states procedural guarantees such as fair hearing (s 10) and presumption of innocence (s 12).

    **Nepal's position: *Pasang Dawa Tamang v HMG***

    Pasang was accused of taking women from Rasuwa to Bombay on 2042/1/1 and selling them. He was convicted under the Human Trafficking (Control) Act 2043, which came into force on 2043/7/24, and given 10 years. He argued that the later Act punished him for an earlier act and made him prove his innocence.

    The Supreme Court dismissed the appeal. On the substantive side, the old Muluki Ain chapter already made selling a person a crime, with 20 years' imprisonment; Pasang got only 10, the new Act's minimum. So the new law did not harm him. On the procedural side, filing a case, prosecution and the burden of proof follow the law in force when the case is heard, because procedural law creates no rights.

    **Critical view**

    The distinction is useful, but it is not watertight. Calling the burden of proof "procedure" let a later Act shift the risk of losing onto the accused. That sits uneasily with Art 20(5) of the Constitution, which presumes the accused innocent until proved guilty. Too much procedure also causes delay and cost; too little risks unfairness. The aim should be procedure that is fair, simple and quick, as the preambles of the 2074 Procedure Codes promise.

    **Conclusion**

    Substantive law tells us what our rights and duties are; procedural law tells us how to enforce them. The first is the end, the second the means. They differ in nature, content and effect in time, but each needs the other. Nepal now keeps them in separate Codes, and the Supreme Court in *Pasang Dawa Tamang* has drawn the line: substance cannot punish the past, but procedure follows the law of the day.

??? example "Q2. Explain about the general procedure and summary procedure. Also mention the differences between them. (2080 Q1, 20 marks)"
    **Introduction**

    Procedure is the set of steps a case goes through in court, from filing to judgment and appeal. Not every case needs the same steps. A dispute over a large estate needs full pleadings, evidence and argument. A small wage claim does not. So Nepali law has a full, ordinary track, the **general procedure** (सामान्य कार्यविधि), and a shorter, faster track for small cases, the **summary procedure** (संक्षिप्त कार्यविधि). A third track, the special procedure, is set by particular Acts for their own cases.

    **General procedure**

    *Meaning.* The general procedure is the ordinary, complete procedure that applies to every case unless a law provides otherwise. Today it is found in the Muluki Civil Procedure Code 2074 and the Muluki Criminal Procedure Code 2074, in force from 1 Bhadra 2075. Earlier it was mainly in the Adalati Bandobasta chapter of the Muluki Ain.

    *Steps in a civil case.* The plaintiff files a plaint within the limitation period (CPC s 47). The court serves a summons on the defendant, who must file a reply within 21 days, not counting travel time (CPC s 119(1)). The parties attend on fixed dates. The court takes evidence, hears both sides and gives a reasoned judgment. An appeal lies within 30 days of learning of the judgment (CPC s 205(1)). In a criminal case, the steps run from the report of the crime through investigation, charge sheet, trial and judgment.

    *Features.* It is the default and residual procedure: it fills the gaps in special and summary procedures (CPC s 3(2); CrPC s 3(2)). It is full and formal. It gives fuller time limits: a lapsed time limit can be restored for up to 15 days once, and a date up to twice for up to 21 days, where the cause was beyond the party's control (CPC s 223). It has safeguards such as open court (CPC s 5) and equal status of parties (CPC s 9).

    *Merits and demerits.* It is thorough and fair, and suits complex cases. But it is slow, costly and heavy for small claims.

    **Summary procedure**

    *Meaning and law.* The summary procedure is a shortened procedure for small and simple cases. It is set by the Summary Procedure Act 2028, Act No. 17 of 2028, in force throughout Nepal from 2030/1/1. Its preamble says small cases should be decided quickly by a summary procedure. It applies in the Supreme Court, High Courts, District Courts and other courts or judicial bodies that decide particular cases (s 2(ग)).

    *Which cases.* Under s 3(1), it applies at trial and on appeal to:

    - cases in Schedule 1, such as cases under the Patent, Design and Trademark Act 2022, the Copyright Act 2059 and the Companies Act 2063, cases within a local judicial committee's jurisdiction, and certain Civil Code cases on pre-emption, wages, maintenance and removing a tenant; and
    - small cases: civil cases worth up to Rs 1,000, and criminal cases punishable by a fine up to Rs 1,000 or prison up to six months or both.

    Schedule 2 cases are kept out even if small: other land cases, partition and inheritance, establishing a relationship, rape, theft and forgery. A special procedure under another Act is not affected (s 3(2)).

    *Main steps.*

    1. A complaint can be made with evidence even without a formal plaint (s 4).
    2. The court checks whether the claim has a basis in law and enough evidence, and may dismiss it with reasons (s 5(1)-(2)).
    3. If the defendant is present, his statement is taken at once; if he wants to reply in writing, he gets 7 days. If absent, a summons issues within 3 days (s 5(3)).
    4. The defendant must get copies of the complaint and documents, or the proceedings are void (s 6(4)).
    5. The summons gives 7 days, not counting travel time (s 8(2)).
    6. A lapsed time limit or date can be restored only for a cause beyond the party's control, for 15 days in all (s 8(1)).
    7. The case must be decided within 90 days of the reply or the end of the time for it (s 10).
    8. After the plaintiff's evidence is in, the case is not dismissed for missed dates (s 11(2)).
    9. An appeal lies within 30 days of learning of the judgment (s 11क), and must be decided within 90 days (s 11ख).

    *Switching and saving.* If a case turns out not to be a summary case, the court records why and continues under the general procedure (s 12(1)). If only part of it is, the whole case goes by the general procedure (s 12(2)). And if the general procedure was used in a summary case, the proceedings are not void for that reason alone (s 14(1)).

    **Differences**

    | Point | General procedure | Summary procedure |
    |---|---|---|
    | Law | Civil and Criminal Procedure Codes 2074 | Summary Procedure Act 2028 |
    | Cases | All cases unless a law says otherwise | Schedule 1 and small cases, not Schedule 2 |
    | Start | Formal plaint or charge sheet | Complaint, even without a formal plaint (s 4) |
    | Preliminary check | Registration checks | Court checks legal basis and evidence; may dismiss (s 5) |
    | Time to answer | 21 days (CPC s 119(1)) | 7 days (s 8(2)), or statement at once |
    | Restoring time | 15 days once; dates twice, 21 days (CPC s 223) | 15 days in all, only for a cause beyond control (s 8(1)) |
    | Time to decide | No general limit in the Code | 90 days (s 10) |
    | Appeal | 30 days (CPC s 205(1)) | 30 days (s 11क); decided in 90 days (s 11ख) |
    | Formality | High | Low |
    | Role | Default; fills gaps | Exception for small cases |

    **Nepal's position: *Tripura Devi Jha v Kali Devi Jha***

    In this case (NKP 2058, D.N. 6996), the Appellate Court had decided an appeal in a robbery case under the general procedure. In the Supreme Court, the appellant argued that the appeal had been filed late under the Summary Procedure Act. The Supreme Court applied s 14: using the general procedure in a summary case does not, by that alone, make the proceedings void. It refused to set aside the appeal on that ground, and decided the case on the evidence.

    **Critical view**

    The summary procedure is a good idea, but the Act is out of date. Its small-case clause still uses Rs 1,000 and six months, so it catches almost nothing today. The 90-day limits carry no sanction. Seven days may be too short for a poor defendant. And as *Tripura Devi Jha* shows, courts do not always use the right track, which leaves time limits unclear. The general procedure, for its part, is often slow. Both need regular review.

    **Conclusion**

    The general procedure is the full, default track for all cases; the summary procedure is a quick, cheap track for small ones. They differ in scope, formality and time limits, but they work together: the summary procedure gives way to the general one when a case is not small, and the general procedure fills its gaps. Updating the summary procedure's limits would let it do the job its preamble promises.

??? example "Q3. Explain the simple procedure, summary procedure and special procedure in the Nepalese context. (2078 Q2, old course, 20 marks)"
    **Introduction**

    Procedure is the path a case takes through the courts. Nepal has three paths. The **simple** or general procedure (सामान्य कार्यविधि) is the ordinary path for all cases. The **summary procedure** (संक्षिप्त कार्यविधि) is a short path for small cases. The **special procedure** (विशेष कार्यविधि) is a path that a particular Act lays down for its own cases. The word "simple" in the question translates सामान्य, which also means "general" or "ordinary".

    **1. Simple (general) procedure**

    *Meaning.* The ordinary, full procedure that applies to every case unless another law sets a different one.

    *Law.* The Muluki Civil Procedure Code 2074 and the Muluki Criminal Procedure Code 2074, in force from 1 Bhadra 2075. Their preambles say they amend and consolidate the law on filing, proceedings, hearing and disposal of cases and on execution, to make procedural law simple and suited to the times.

    *Features.* Formal plaint, reply within 21 days (CPC s 119(1)), evidence, hearing and reasoned judgment; appeal within 30 days (CPC s 205(1)); open court (CPC s 5); equal status of parties (CPC s 9). It is also the residual procedure: where a separate law sets its own procedure, the Code applies only to matters that law does not cover (CPC s 3(2); CrPC s 3(2)).

    *Assessment.* Thorough and fair, but slow and costly for small cases.

    **2. Summary procedure**

    *Meaning.* A shortened procedure for small and simple cases, with fewer steps and shorter time limits.

    *Law.* The Summary Procedure Act 2028, in force throughout Nepal from 2030/1/1. Its preamble: small cases should be decided quickly.

    *Scope (s 3(1)).* Cases in Schedule 1 (for example under the Patent, Design and Trademark Act 2022, the Copyright Act 2059, the Companies Act 2063, local judicial committee cases, and Civil Code cases on pre-emption, wages, maintenance and removing a tenant). Also small cases: civil cases worth up to Rs 1,000, and criminal cases punishable by a fine up to Rs 1,000 or prison up to six months or both, except Schedule 2 cases (land, partition, relationship, rape, theft, forgery).

    *Main features.* Complaint without a formal plaint (s 4); preliminary check and dismissal (s 5); 7 days to answer (s 8(2)); copies to the defendant, or the proceedings are void (s 6(4)); restoration only for a cause beyond control, 15 days in all (s 8(1)); decision within 90 days (s 10); appeal within 30 days (s 11क), decided in 90 days (s 11ख).

    *Links to the general procedure.* If a case is not a summary case, the court switches to the general procedure (s 12). Using the general procedure in a summary case does not void the proceedings (s 14(1)). The Supreme Court applied this in *Tripura Devi Jha v Kali Devi Jha* (NKP 2058).

    *Assessment.* Quick and cheap, but the Rs 1,000 threshold is outdated and the 90-day limit has no sanction.

    **3. Special procedure**

    *Meaning.* A procedure that a particular Act sets for the cases under it, in place of or alongside the general procedure.

    *Legal basis.* CPC s 3(1) and CrPC s 3(1): the procedure in a separate law applies, and the Code does not affect it. Summary Procedure Act s 3(2): a special procedure is not affected by the summary procedure. Criminal Code s 5: where a special Act makes an act a separate offence, that Act applies.

    *Examples.*

    - **Special Court Act 2059.** The government, on the Judicial Council's advice, sets up a Special Court to decide particular cases quickly and effectively (s 3(1)). Summons of up to 15 days in Nepal and 30 days abroad (s 10(1)); restoration once, up to 15 days (s 11); District Court powers on other matters (s 12); decision normally within six months (s 16(1)); appeal to the Supreme Court within 35 days (s 17).
    - **Human Trafficking and Transportation (Control) Act 2064.** Victim's statement attested by a District Court judge and usable as evidence even if she does not return (s 6); the accused must prove he did not commit the offence (s 9); in camera hearing (s 27); government case (s 28).
    - **Local Government Operation Act 2074.** The judicial committee must first try mediation (s 49(2)); its other procedure is set by local law (s 49(12)); appeal to the District Court within 35 days (s 51).

    *Case.* In *Pasang Dawa Tamang v HMG* (NKP 2058), the Supreme Court held that the Human Trafficking (Control) Act 2043 had the place of a special Act, and its procedure could make an exception to the general procedure. So the accused bore the burden of disproving the victim's statement.

    *Assessment.* Fits the subject and protects victims, but scatters the law, and some features, like a reverse burden, strain the presumption of innocence in Art 20(5) of the Constitution.

    **How the three fit together**

    A court first asks whether a special Act governs the case; if so, that procedure applies. If not, it asks whether the case is a summary case under s 3 of the Summary Procedure Act. Otherwise it uses the general procedure. On any gap in a special or summary procedure, it falls back on the general procedure (CPC s 3(2); CrPC s 3(2); Summary Procedure Act s 14(2)).

    | Point | Simple (general) | Summary | Special |
    |---|---|---|---|
    | Law | Procedure Codes 2074 | Summary Procedure Act 2028 | The particular Act |
    | Scope | All cases | Schedule 1 and small cases | Cases under the Act |
    | Time to answer | 21 days (civil) | 7 days | As the Act says |
    | Priority | Last; fills gaps | Gives way to special (s 3(2)) | First |

    **Critical view**

    Three tracks let the law match the procedure to the case. But they also cause confusion, as *Tripura Devi Jha* shows. The summary procedure's money limits need updating. Special procedures should keep to the constitutional guarantees in Art 20, especially fair trial (cl 9) and presumption of innocence (cl 5).

    **Conclusion**

    The simple procedure is the full road every case can travel; the summary procedure is a short road for small cases; special procedures are separate roads for particular subjects. Together they aim at what Art 126(1) of the Constitution requires: justice under the Constitution, the laws and recognised principles of justice.

??? example "Q4. What is procedural law? Explain its meaning, nature and importance. (likely question; first half of 2075 Q1, old course, 20 marks)"
    **Introduction**

    A right is only as good as the means of enforcing it. If a neighbour takes my land, the Civil Code says the land is mine. But I get it back only by filing a case in the right court, on time, giving notice to the other side, proving my claim and enforcing the judgment. The law that governs these steps is **procedural law** (कार्यविधि कानून).

    **Meaning and definitions**

    "Procedure" comes from the Latin *procedere*, "to go forward". Procedural law moves a case forward from complaint to final result.

    - Roman law divided all law into the law of persons, of things and of actions; the law of actions is our procedure.
    - **Bentham** coined the term "adjective law" for it, as against substantive law.
    - **Holland:** substantive law defines rights; adjective law provides the means of enforcing and protecting them.
    - **Salmond:** "The law of procedure may be defined as that branch of the law which governs the process of litigation." It covers all legal proceedings, civil and criminal.
    - **Supreme Court of Nepal** (*Pasang Dawa Tamang v HMG*, NKP 2058): procedural law creates no rights; it only fixes and regulates procedure.

    In Nepal, the preambles of the 2074 Procedure Codes describe its subject: filing, proceedings, hearing and disposal of cases, and execution of decisions, and in criminal cases investigation and prosecution as well.

    **Scope**

    Civil procedure; criminal procedure; evidence; limitation; jurisdiction; appeal, review, revision and writ procedure; execution; and the procedure of arbitration and mediation.

    **Nepal's position**

    - **Constitution of Nepal 2015.** Art 126(1): powers relating to justice are exercised by courts and judicial bodies under the Constitution, other laws and recognised principles of justice. Art 20 lists the rights relating to justice that every procedure must respect. Art 128(4): everyone must follow the Supreme Court's interpretations and legal principles, so its rulings on procedure bind lower courts.
    - **The 2074 Codes.** The Muluki Civil Procedure Code 2074 and the Muluki Criminal Procedure Code 2074 came into force on 1 Bhadra 2075 and took over the role of the Muluki Ain's court-procedure chapter (Adalati Bandobasta). They set the general procedure. Section 3 of each lets a separate law's procedure prevail and makes the Code fill the gaps.
    - **Other Acts.** The Evidence Act 2031 (proof and burden of proof, ss 25-26); the Summary Procedure Act 2028 (small cases); the Administration of Justice Act 2073 (courts, and the Supreme Court's power to make rules on procedure, s 36); special Acts such as the Special Court Act 2059 and the Human Trafficking and Transportation (Control) Act 2064.
    - **Rules.** Supreme Court Rules 2074, High Court Rules 2073, District Court Rules 2075 and the Civil and Criminal Procedure Rules 2075.
    - **Cases.** *Pasang Dawa Tamang* (procedure follows the law in force at the hearing) and *Tripura Devi Jha* (a wrong choice of procedure does not by itself void proceedings).

    **Nature**

    1. **Adjective.** A means, not an end; it serves substantive law.
    2. **Public law.** It governs how the court, a state organ, deals with the parties.
    3. **About "how".** It decides how a right is proved and enforced, not whether it exists.
    4. **Applies to pending cases.** Procedure follows the law in force when the case is heard (*Pasang Dawa Tamang*). Substantive criminal law cannot reach back (Art 20(4)).
    5. **Formal, but serving justice.** A wrong choice of procedure does not by itself void proceedings (Summary Procedure Act s 14(1); *Tripura Devi Jha*).
    6. **Mandatory and directory rules.** Some breaches void the step, e.g. not giving the defendant copies under s 6(4) of the Summary Procedure Act; others do not.
    7. **Codified.** Mainly in the 2074 Codes, in force from 1 Bhadra 2075.
    8. **Gap-filling.** The general Codes apply where a special law is silent (CPC s 3(2); CrPC s 3(2)).
    9. **Constitutional base.** It must respect the guarantees in Art 20: notice of arrest, a lawyer, presentation within 24 hours, presumption of innocence, no double jeopardy, silence, information about proceedings, fair trial and legal aid.

    **Importance**

    1. **Gives life to rights.** *ubi jus ibi remedium*: where there is a right, there is a remedy, and procedure is the path to it.
    2. **Fair trial.** Art 20(9) guarantees a fair trial by an independent, impartial and competent court; Criminal Code s 10 says no one shall be denied a fair hearing.
    3. **Equality.** The parties have equal status, and even the government cannot claim special treatment unless the law or court allows it (CPC s 9).
    4. **Open justice.** Hearings are in open court unless the law or the court provides otherwise (CPC s 5).
    5. **Certainty.** Known steps and time limits make outcomes predictable.
    6. **Control of power.** Limits on arrest and detention (Art 20(3)); a judge with an interest may not hear the case (CPC s 271).
    7. **Speed and finality.** Limitation, time limits and res judicata end disputes; the summary procedure exists so that small cases are decided quickly.
    8. **Access to justice.** Free legal aid (Art 20(10)); a guardian may sue for a minor or incapable person (CPC s 267).
    9. **Execution.** It makes sure judgments are carried out.
    10. **Public trust.** A fair process makes even the loser accept the result.

    **Critical view**

    Procedure can also defeat justice. Technical rules, many dates and long appeals cause delay and cost, and poor parties struggle to follow them. The answer is not less procedure but better procedure: courts preferring substance to form where no harm is done, as in *Tripura Devi Jha*; short tracks for small cases, as in the Summary Procedure Act; and reforms such as continuous hearing and the use of IT (Unit 2).

    **Conclusion**

    Procedural law is the machinery that turns rights on paper into real remedies. It is adjective in nature, public in character and applies to cases as they are heard. Its importance lies in fairness, equality, certainty, speed and trust. Nepal's 2074 Procedure Codes, the Summary Procedure Act and the Constitution's Art 20 together aim to make that machinery fair and quick.

## One-minute revision

- **Procedural law:** Salmond, "governs the process of litigation"; Bentham and Holland, adjective law, the means of enforcing rights; SC in *Pasang Dawa Tamang*, creates no rights, only regulates procedure.
- **Nature:** adjective; public; "how" not "what"; applies to pending cases; formal but serves justice (SPA s 14); mandatory vs directory (SPA s 6(4)); codified (2074 Codes from 1 Bhadra 2075); fills gaps (CPC s 3, CrPC s 3); rests on Art 20.
- **Importance:** right to remedy (*ubi jus ibi remedium*); fair trial (Art 20(9), Criminal Code s 10); equality (CPC s 9); open court (CPC s 5); certainty; control of power; speed and finality; access (Art 20(10)); execution; trust.
- **Substantive vs procedural:** what vs how; end vs means; rights vs remedies; prospective (Art 20(4), Criminal Code s 7) vs pending cases (*Pasang*). Grey areas: burden of proof (Evidence Act ss 25-26; HTA 2064 s 9), limitation (CPC s 47; Civil Code chapter sections), right of appeal, mixed Acts.
- **General procedure:** Procedure Codes 2074; reply 21 days (CPC s 119); restoration (CPC s 223); appeal 30 days (CPC s 205); default and gap-filler.
- **Summary procedure:** SPA 2028, in force 2030/1/1; Schedule 1 + small cases (Rs 1,000; six months), not Schedule 2 (s 3(1)); special procedure saved (s 3(2)); complaint without plaint (s 4); check and dismiss (s 5); copies or void (s 6(4)); 7 days (s 8(2)); 15 days' restoration (s 8(1)); decide in 90 days (s 10); appeal 30 days (s 11क), decided 90 days (s 11ख); switch (s 12); saving (s 14; *Tripura Devi Jha*).
- **Special procedure:** CPC s 3, CrPC s 3, SPA s 3(2), Criminal Code s 5. Special Court Act 2059 (ss 3, 10, 11, 12, 16, 17); HTA 2064 (ss 6, 9, 27, 28); LGOA 2074 (ss 49, 51). Order: special, then summary, then general.
