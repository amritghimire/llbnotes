# Unit 4A: Civil Proceedings

!!! abstract "In one line"
    This unit follows a civil case in the court where it starts (the court of first instance): filing the plaint, notice to the defendant, the defence, tarikh, attorneys, court orders and stay, evidence, statements, postponement, compromise, and finally the judgment.

!!! note "Unit 4 has two pages"
    Syllabus Unit 4 covers both civil and criminal cases, so it is split. This page (4A) covers 4.2, civil proceedings. Syllabus 4.1, the kinds of hearing (preliminary, merit or final, and sentencing), is on the [criminal page, Unit 4B](unit-04b-criminal-proceedings.md#41-kinds-of-hearing), where the exam asks it.

    In civil cases, briefly: a **preliminary hearing** (प्रारम्भिक सुनुवाई) is held only if the defence pleads that the plaintiff has no locus standi, the claim is time-barred or the court lacks jurisdiction. The court decides those points before taking evidence (Muluki Civil Procedure Code 2074 ss 131-134). The **final hearing** comes after the evidence: the court hears both sides, generally within a month, and decides the case (ss 192, 197).

## Start here: the life of a civil case

Sita lent Ram Rs 5 lakh on a written loan deed. Ram has not paid. Sita cannot take the money by force; she must go to court. Here is what happens, step by step.

1. **Sita files a plaint** in the District Court. She says what she claims, why, and on what evidence. She pays the fees. A court officer checks the plaint and registers it.
2. **The court sends Ram a notice** (myad), with a copy of the plaint. Ram gets 21 days to answer. A process server delivers it to him, or to an adult at his home, or pastes it on his door.
3. **Ram files his defence.** He may admit or deny the claim. He may say Sita sued too late, or in the wrong court. He may even make his own claim against her (a counterclaim).
4. **Both sides get a tarikh**, a date to come back. From now on each must keep turning up.
5. **The court sorts out the issues.** If Ram raised limitation or jurisdiction, the court decides that first. Then it finds which facts the two agree on and which they dispute.
6. **The court takes evidence:** documents first, then witnesses. Ram is shown the loan deed and must say whether it is genuine or forged.
7. **Along the way, the court may make orders**: for example, to stop Ram selling his land while the case runs. It may also put the case on hold (multavi) until another case ends.
8. **The parties may settle** at any stage, by a compromise deed or through a mediator.
9. **The court hears arguments and decides.** It writes the judgment and tells both sides. The loser may appeal within 30 days.

Each step has rules in the **Muluki Civil Procedure Code 2074** (मुलुकी देवानी कार्यविधि संहिता, २०७४), in force from 1 Bhadra 2075 (s 1(2)). The details are filled in by the **Muluki Civil Procedure Rules 2075** (मुलुकी देवानी कार्यविधि नियमावली, २०७५) and the **District Court Rules 2075** (जिल्ला अदालत नियमावली, २०७५). Below, "the Code" means the Civil Procedure Code, and "the Rules" means the Civil Procedure Rules. The old Muluki Ain, with its Adalati Bandobasta chapter, governed these steps until 2075. The prescribed cases were decided under it.

## 4.2.1 Filing of suits and requirements

### What a suit is

A **suit** is a civil case started in court. It starts with a **plaint**. The Code defines a plaint as a complaint, claim or petition made to establish one's right, interest or concern, or to enforce a claim. It includes a statement made in court in place of a plaint, and a counterclaim (s 2(ञ)). A person who wants a legal claim enforced must bring it to court, by filing a plaint that meets the rules of Chapter 7 (s 85).

### Who may sue: locus standi and own right

- **Locus standi** (हकदैया). The plaint must show that the plaintiff has a right in the claim, or a lawful interest or concern in it (s 10).
- **Only one's own right.** Generally, a person may sue only on a matter in which he has a right (s 86(1)). A plaint against this rule is not registered, and if registered by mistake it is struck out (s 86(2)).
- **Public interest.** The exception is a dispute involving the interest of the federal, provincial or local government, or a public interest. Anyone may sue on such a dispute with the court's leave. Leave is sought by a separate petition with the plaint, and the court decides it the same day (s 91).
- **Third party harmed by a judgment or compromise.** A person whose right is affected by another case's judgment or compromise may sue to void it. He must do so within 35 days of learning of it (s 92).
- **Bodies corporate.** A company or other body sues through a natural person authorised by its board or committee. A copy of that decision is attached. A government office sues through its administrative head (s 93).

### What one plaint may contain

- **Joinder of claims.** One person may file a single plaint against another on a civil claim, however many related matters it covers (s 87(1)).
- **Joinder of parties.** On one subject of claim, one plaint may be filed by one person against many, by many against one, or by many against many (s 88).
- **No splitting.** A person who could have sued on everything at once, but waits for the result of the first case and then sues again, cannot proceed (s 87(2)).
- **Only once.** After suing on a matter, a person cannot sue the same person on it again, in that court or any other. If both sides sue on the same matter, the plaint registered first goes ahead and the others are struck out (s 89).
- **No suit on a decided matter.** Once a judgment is given, the same party cannot sue the same person on the same matter again instead of appealing. Such a plaint is struck out, and any judgment on it is void (s 94). This is res judicata, which the Code also states as a general principle (s 14). See Unit 2.

!!! example "Case: Padam Bahadur Karki v Man Kumari Basnet (NKP 2061)"
    Bhakta Bahadur sold plot No. 180 to Padam Bahadur Karki. The same day, Padam Bahadur sold it on to Jamuni Das and Ramchhabila Das. Bhakta Bahadur's father, mother and brothers filed one plaint to cancel both deeds. The buyers said Adalati Bandobasta No. 72 needed a separate plaint for each deed. The Supreme Court disagreed. Both deeds concerned the same land, were made on the same day, and formed one continuous chain. So they were one subject of dispute, and one plaint was enough. The Court also held that the limitation period runs from when each claimant himself learned of the deed. Today ss 87-88 of the Code carry No. 72 forward. Read the [brief](cases/9-7-padam-bahadur-karki.md).

### Limitation

The plaint must be filed within the limitation period set by law. It must also say which law's limitation period it relies on. A plaint that does not is returned with an endorsement (s 90). Unit 2 covers limitation in detail.

### Form and contents

- **Form.** The plaint must follow the format in Schedule 1 of the Code (s 95).
- **A clear claim.** It must state exactly what is claimed, about what, and on what grounds and evidence. A plaint whose claim is unclear, or which does not say what the court is asked to do, is not properly made and is not registered (s 96).
- **What it must show.** Rule 9 of the Rules lists the matters, besides those other laws require:
    - the plaintiff's locus standi, and the limitation period with its law;
    - the court's jurisdiction, and that no plaint on the same matter was filed elsewhere;
    - for a body corporate, the authority to file; for a public-interest dispute, the court's leave;
    - for a guardian or curator, proof of that status; any other required permission;
    - the law the claim rests on, and details of the evidence;
    - the court fee and plaint fee paid, and the Schedule 1 format followed;
    - enough copies of the plaint and evidence;
    - whether the plaintiff will serve the notice himself; and in a partition case, a list of the property.
- **General form of court papers.** Rule 6 sets the paper size, margins, font and the details of each party (name, family names, address, contacts). Every person named in the body of the document must be identified clearly (r 6(4)). The last paragraph must contain a declaration that the contents are true. An electronic copy must also be filed (r 11).
- **Evidence with the plaint.** The plaintiff files the originals and copies of documents supporting the claim with the plaint (Code s 161(1)). The Evidence Act 2031 adds that the plaint must list all the plaintiff's witnesses, documents and exhibits, as far as available (s 37ख).

### Fees and copies

- **Plaint fee** of Rs 200 (s 97), and **court fee** (अदालती शुल्क) under Chapter 6 (s 63(1)). A case cannot proceed without the court fee (s 64(1)). No court fee is charged where the federal, provincial or local government is plaintiff (s 63(3)).
- **Copies.** Two copies of the plaint and evidence if there is one defendant, and one more for each further defendant. Defendants living in one household need only one copy between them (s 98(2)).

### Registration, or return with endorsement (darpith)

The court officer checks the plaint as soon as it is filed. Is it in the right format, in time, within the court's jurisdiction, complete and free of errors (s 126)?

- **If it meets the rules**, it is entered in the register and the plaintiff gets a receipt (s 98(1); r 12).
- **If it has curable defects** (wrong format, missing details, errors in writing, figures not matching words), the officer writes what must be corrected and returns it. If the plaintiff brings it back corrected in time, it is registered. "In time" means within the time left, or within seven days if less than three days were left (s 127).
- **If it cannot be registered at all** (time-barred, outside jurisdiction, not for a court, or against the law), the officer returns it with an endorsement (s 128(1)). Where the reason was lack of jurisdiction, and the limitation period had not run out by the day of return, the plaintiff gets 15 days, excluding travel time, to file in the right court (s 128(2)).
- **Challenge.** A party unhappy with the endorsement may petition the judge within seven days. If the judge made it, he may petition the appeal court. The judge or court must decide within 15 days (s 130(1)-(2)).

### Case management after filing

Once a case is filed, the court officer prepares a **timetable** (कार्यतालिका) for it. It covers notice, service, defence, preliminary hearing, evidence, pre-hearing discussion and judgment (Rules r 26). District Courts now also place each case on a **track** (मार्गसमूह): simple, general or special. Simple-track cases should generally be decided within six months, general ones within a year, and special ones within 18 months (District Court Rules r 40क).

## 4.2.2 Withdrawal of cases

A plaintiff may decide not to go on with his case. Perhaps he cannot prove it, or the dispute has ended.

- **Who and when.** A party to any case, except one where the Government of Nepal is plaintiff, may apply at any time before judgment. He may give up the claim, or withdraw the plaint, appeal or petition. He must show why he cannot prove the claim or why its purpose has ended (s 196(1)).
- **Leave.** On such an application the court must give leave to withdraw (s 196(2)), and it strikes the case off its register (s 196(3)).
- **No second suit.** Having withdrawn, the same person cannot complain again on the same matter against the same opponent (s 196(4)).
- **No appeal.** No appeal lies where the claim was withdrawn (s 206(c)).
- **Through an attorney.** An authorised attorney may be given power to withdraw a claim (s 153(1)).

**Withdrawal compared.** Withdrawal is the plaintiff's own choice. **Dismissal** (डिसमिस) happens to him, for example when he misses his tarikh before the defence is filed (s 141(1)(a)). A **compromise** ends the case by agreement of both sides (s 193). All three bar a fresh suit on the same matter (ss 141(2), 193(5), 196(4)).

## 4.2.3 Notice to the defendants

### Why notice matters

A court cannot decide against a person who has not been told of the case. This is audi alteram partem, a core rule of natural justice (Unit 2). The Constitution of Nepal 2015 gives every person the right to be informed of any proceedings taken against him (Article 20(8)), and the right to a fair trial (Article 20(9)).

The Code calls the notice **myad** (म्याद): a notice or summons a court issues in a person's name in a case (s 2(ड)). Chapter 8 (ss 99-118) sets out how it is issued and served. It applies to every notice in a civil case, including the notice of judgment (s 200(3)) and the appeal court's notice to a respondent (s 214(2)).

### Who serves the notice

**(a) The plaintiff himself.** The plaintiff may say in his plaint that he will deliver the notice himself or through his lawyer (s 99(1)).

- He takes two copies of the notice and certified copies of the plaint and evidence from the court, and signs a receipt (s 99(3); r 15(1)).
- He must deliver them within three days of registration, excluding travel time (s 99(1)-(2)).
- The defendant must accept it, and acceptance is valid service. For a company, delivery at its head office is enough (s 99(4)-(5)). The defendant signs a receipt (s 99(6)).
- The plaintiff files that receipt in court within seven days (s 99(7)). The 21 days for the defence run from the date of the defendant's receipt (s 99(8)).
- If the defendant refuses, the plaintiff reports this within two days, and the court then serves the notice itself (s 99(9)-(10)).

**(b) The court.** Otherwise the court must issue the notice within three days of registering the plaint (s 100(1); r 15(4)). A copy of the plaint and evidence goes with it (s 100(2)). The defendant may ask for certified copies of papers in the file before answering (s 100(3)).

### Time given, form and copies

- **Time limit.** The notice gives the defendant **21 days**, excluding travel time, to come and defend or counterclaim (s 101(1)). A person the court adds as a defendant under s 123 gets seven days (s 101(2)).
- **Travel time** (bato ko myad) is counted at the actual days by bus or train where regular transport runs. Elsewhere it is one day per 15 km, and distances under 15 km get none (s 227).
- **Copies.** The officer prepares the notice in three copies, signs it and seals it (s 102(1)). Two copies are enough if the plaintiff serves it (s 102(2)). One plaint copy goes to each defendant, but one copy covers all defendants in one household (s 102(3)).
- **Format.** Schedule 6 for a defendant, Schedule 7 for anyone else (s 103).

### How the process server serves it (s 105)

The process server (तामेलदार) must serve the notice normally within **seven days**, excluding travel time. If he cannot, he reports why. A false report can lead to departmental action (s 104).

1. **In person, anywhere.** If he knows the person, he may hand it to him wherever he finds him, recording this (s 105(2)).
2. **At home, before witnesses.** Otherwise he goes to the person's home or rented room. There he hands it over, gets a receipt and records the date. Witnesses must be present: a ward chair, ward member, secretary or any government employee of the local level, and at least two local people (s 105(3)).
3. **To a family member.** If the person is not found, he gives it, with the same witnesses, to an adult of the same household (s 105(4)).
4. **Pasting on the door.** If no one is found, or they refuse it, he pastes it on the house door where all can see. The same witnesses are present, and he records this on the notice (s 105(5)).
5. **Record.** On the back of the notice he writes how, when and to whom it was served. The witnesses sign then and there (s 105(6)). He signs and returns it to court with a report (s 105(7); r 15(5)).
6. **Copy to the local level.** After pasting, the third copy goes to the local level, which puts it on its notice board (s 105(8)-(9)). But a notice properly pasted on the door is valid even if this step is missed (s 105(10)).
7. **Bodies corporate and offices.** For a company, it goes to its chief executive or another listed officer, or failing them any office-bearer or employee there (s 105(11)). For a government office, constitutional body or court, it goes to its head or administrative chief, or to the office itself (s 105(12)-(13)). If that fails, the court may serve by registered post. Once the return receipt is filed, service is presumed (s 105(14)-(17)).
8. **Post and electronic means.** Where an area keeps numbered house records, or a person has agreed to service by post, the court may use post (s 105(18)). Where the parties have given each other an email or fax address for notices, the court may serve there (s 105(21)).
9. **Newspaper.** If every method fails and the report seems reasonable, the court orders publication in a national daily. The party concerned pays (s 105(22)-(24)).

### Other methods and rules

- **At court.** Notice may be handed over at court to a court employee, a party there on tarikh in another case, an attorney or lawyer appearing there, or a witness. If such a person refuses, the court may bar him from acting in that court until he accepts it (s 106).
- **Deemed service.** A notice delivered under the Chapter, or made known to the person by any other means that can be recorded, is served. A person may also come to court and ask to receive it (s 107).
- **To an attorney or lawyer.** Where a person has lawfully appointed an attorney or lawyer to receive notices, service on that person is valid (s 113).
- **Address unknown.** The notice is posted at the village, town or street given, or on the court's notice board, and published in a national daily. That is valid service (s 114).
- **Outside the court's area.** The notice is sent to the court where the person lives, which serves it and sends it back. Electronic means may be used (s 115; r 15(6)). For a party abroad, notice may be served under the law on mutual legal assistance (r 17).

### Checks, duties and penalties

- **Duty to help.** Ward officials and local people must help the server find the house and witness the service (s 108(1)). But a notice served properly in other respects is not invalid just because they refused (s 108(2)). A person who refuses to help may be fined up to Rs 5,000 (s 109).
- **Court check.** As soon as the server reports, a court officer checks whether service was proper. If so, he certifies it and puts it in the file (s 110).
- **Re-service.** If service was improper, or a complaint shows it was improper or never made, the court orders fresh service (s 111). A server who fails to give the third copy to the local level may be fined up to Rs 1,000 each time (s 112).
- **Offences.** Failing to serve without reason, serving on a false record, obstructing service, or tearing off a pasted notice: fine up to Rs 10,000, up to one month's jail, or both (s 116). Such reports go before the bench (r 16).

### Remedies for improper service

- **Before judgment.** A party may complain at any time before the case is decided that a notice was not properly served, or not served at all. If the court agrees, it voids the notice and orders fresh service. If its own check finds a defect, it must do so (s 117).
- **After an ex parte judgment.** A defendant may have lost ex parte because the notice was improperly served. He may file an **appeal equal to a statement of defence** (प्रतिउत्तरपत्र सरहको पुनरावेदन) in the appeal court. He must file it within 35 days of learning of the judgment, and within six months of it. The appeal court first decides whether service was proper. If not, it voids the judgment, treats the appeal as the defence, and sends the case back. The lower court then gives the plaintiff seven days' notice and hears the case (s 118; r 18).
- **But proper service binds.** If a notice was properly served and the defendant filed no defence, he cannot appeal (s 206(a)).

!!! example "Case: Pradip Kumar Prasad Shah Kalwar v Ram Bhajan Raya Yadav (NKP 2073)"
    A District Court decided a loan case against Pradip. Its notice of time to appeal was pasted on his door, since he was not home and his household refused it. Two witnesses were present and a copy went to the VDC. Pradip did not appeal, and later said he never got the notice. The Supreme Court (Joint Bench, 2072/12/22) dismissed his writ petition. A party has a right to learn of a judgment and to appeal. But once notice is served by the legal procedure, the person who says it was improper must prove which step was missed. The law does not mean a properly served notice should be cancelled without reason to give a second chance. Today Chapter 8, mainly s 105, governs service. Read the [brief](cases/9-9-pradip-kumar-prasad-shah-kalwar.md).

## 4.2.4 Filing of defence

### Time and form

- The defendant must file his **statement of defence** (pratiuttar) within **21 days** of service, excluding travel time (s 119(1)). A defence filed late is not registered, and if registered it is not accepted (s 119(2)).
- A statement of defence is a written reply that admits or denies the plaint. It includes a statement made in court in place of a defence (s 2(छ)).
- It follows Schedule 9 (s 120). The fee is Rs 200 (s 125).
- The court checks whether it is in time and in proper form, registers it and gives a receipt. If not, it returns it with the reasons (s 122). The defence must show it is in time and in the right format, with the fee and evidence attached (r 19).
- The defendant files his documents with the defence (s 161(1)). He must list all his witnesses, documents and exhibits, as far as available (Evidence Act s 37ख).
- If the defendant could not come within the 21 days for reasons beyond his control, he may get the time extended once, by up to 15 days (s 223). See 4.2.5.

### Preliminary pleas

If the defendant wants to argue that the plaintiff has **no locus standi**, that the plaint is **time-barred**, or that the court has **no jurisdiction**, he must say so in the defence (s 131). The court then holds a preliminary hearing on those points before taking evidence (s 132). If any plea succeeds, the court gives judgment dismissing the case (s 133). If the court rejects the pleas, the defendant cannot raise them again on appeal. He may, however, challenge the order under s 157 (s 134).

### Counterclaim

- The defendant may make his own claim against the plaintiff in the defence. It is then a **counterclaim** (s 121(1)).
- It must be written in the defence itself, and be limited to the subject of the plaint (s 121(2)).
- Copies go to the plaintiff, who must file a written reply admitting or denying it within 15 days (s 121(3)-(5)).
- The defendant pays court fee on it within seven days. Only then does the counterclaim stand (s 121(7); r 20).
- Where the plaintiff misses his tarikh, the court still decides the counterclaim on the evidence (r 21(2)).

### If no defence is filed

The case does not stop. If the defendant files no defence, the court examines the plaint and the evidence and decides accordingly (s 141(1)(c)). That is an **ex parte** judgment.

### Others joining as defendants

- **The court may add a defendant** (s 123). It may do so where a case would affect a person's right, or the plaint or defence shows that he is involved. See 4.2.6.
- **A person may ask to be added** after the defence is filed and before judgment. He must show how the judgment would affect his right or interest. If the court agrees, he is added, but he cannot counterclaim (s 124; r 22).

## 4.2.5 Date of presence (tarikh)

### Meaning

Tarikh (तारिख) is the date the court fixes for a party to appear next. A party "on tarikh" (तारिखमा रहेको) must come to court on each date given. If he does, he signs the tarikh receipt (तारिख भरपाई), and that is his attendance. The system keeps both parties under the court's control while the case runs.

### The rules (Chapter 12, ss 135-143)

- **Duty to keep parties on tarikh.** The court proceeds with a case by keeping each party on tarikh, unless the law or a court order says otherwise (s 135(1)).
- **Release after evidence.** Once both sides' evidence has been taken, a party may, with the court's leave, stop attending on tarikh (s 135(2)). He applies in the Schedule 7 form of the Rules. He may later ask to be put back on tarikh (r 24).
- **Same date for both.** Where both are on tarikh, they get the same date (s 136(1)). The plaintiff's first date is fixed allowing for the time the defence may take. After the defence, both get one common date (s 136(2)-(3)).
- **Purpose stated.** The court records in the tarikh book and slip why the date was fixed and what will be done on it, and it must do that work then. If it cannot, it records why and fixes another date (s 137).
- **No clashing dates.** A party with a case in a court over 15 km away may tell the court, which must avoid a clash. A person with several cases in one court may ask for one common date (s 138(1)-(3)).
- **Attendance and default.** A party who comes signs the tarikh receipt (Schedule 12). One who does not is taken to have **missed his tarikh**, and an officer certifies this (s 138(4)-(5); r 25).
- **Holidays.** If the court is closed on the date, the tarikh falls on the next working day (s 139).
- **Detention.** A party held in custody must tell the court, through family, lawyer or attorney. The court asks the holding body why and for how long. If he will be out within a month and comes within seven days of release, the case goes on from that day. If not, the court need not wait for him. He may appoint an attorney (s 140).
- **Government exempt.** The federal, provincial and local governments, constitutional bodies and courts need not stay on tarikh. But a government lawyer must appear for evidence, hearing, or when the court orders (s 142).
- **Appeal level.** An appeal may be heard without keeping the parties on tarikh, unless the court orders otherwise (s 143).

### What happens if a party misses tarikh (s 141)

The consequences apply only after the period for extending the tarikh (Chapter 21) has run out (s 141(1)).

| Who misses | Result |
|---|---|
| Plaintiff, before the defence is filed | Plaint dismissed |
| Plaintiff, after the defence | Judgment on what the defendant admitted; the rest dismissed |
| Defendant (no defence, or misses after filing it) | Court decides on the plaint and both sides' evidence |
| Both | Case dismissed |

A plaintiff whose case is dismissed under (a), (b) or (d) cannot sue the same defendant on the same matter again (s 141(2)).

### Extending a missed myad or tarikh (Chapter 21)

- **Missing is not allowed** (s 222). But a party prevented by circumstances beyond his control may get a missed **myad extended once, by up to 15 days**. He may get a missed **tarikh extended up to 21 days, at most twice** (s 223).
- **Listed situations** (s 225). The court must extend if the party applies within these times, excluding travel time, with proof:
    - mourning rites after a death: seven days after the fifteenth day from the death;
    - childbirth: 60 days;
    - road closed by flood, landslide or snow, or a curfew, or no public transport: 15 days from reopening;
    - earthquake or similar disaster: ten days;
    - kidnapping or hostage-taking: 15 days from release;
    - hospital stay after an accident or serious illness: 15 days.
- **Death or disappearance of a party.** A guardian or close heir may apply within 21 days to take over the case (mudda sakar) and have the missed date extended (s 226).
- **No decision meanwhile.** The court must not decide the case until the extension period has passed (s 228).

!!! tip "Myad and tarikh: do not mix them up"
    A **myad** is a notice with a time limit, sent to a person not yet before the court (e.g. 21 days to file a defence). A **tarikh** is a date given to a party already before the court. A missed myad can be extended once, up to 15 days. A missed tarikh can be extended twice, up to 21 days each time (s 223).

### Critical view

Tarikh keeps parties within reach and stops a case stalling. But it makes litigants travel to court again and again, often for a few minutes' work. A missed date can cost a whole case. The law softens this. A party may be released after evidence (s 135(2)), may act through an attorney (s 144), and may have dates extended (ss 223, 225). Case timetables and tracks (r 26; District Court Rules r 40क) aim to make each tarikh do real work.

## 4.2.6 Persons mentioned in referred writings and deeds (petbolimo ultekhbhayeko)

### Meaning

The syllabus phrase petbolimo ultekhbhayeko is पेटबोलीमा उल्लेख भएको: "mentioned in the body" of a document. A plaint, a defence, or a deed filed as evidence often names people who are not parties. Examples are a co-owner, a later buyer, the writer of the deed, or its witnesses. The question is when the court must identify, call and hear such persons.

### The rules

- **Identify them.** Every person named in the body of a court document must be described clearly enough to be identified: full name, surname, address and other details (Rules r 6(4)).
- **The court may call them** (s 123). It may order a person to be examined, recording its reasons, in three situations:
    - the case would affect his right, interest, claim or concern;
    - the plaint or defence shows he is involved in the subject of the claim; or
    - there is another reasonable ground to ask him something.

    He gets seven days, excluding travel time (s 123(2)). He may make a statement, or file a written reply within up to seven days (s 123(3)). If he is involved, or the judgment would affect his right, he is kept on tarikh **as a defendant** (s 123(4)). If he is only like a piece of evidence, he is not kept on tarikh (s 123(5)).
- **Third persons revealed by the file** (s 176). The court may find, from the papers in the file, that a decision would affect a third person, or that he is connected with the case. It must then examine him and give him a fair chance to be heard. It keeps him on tarikh as a principal party (कारणी सरह).
- **Documents signed by non-parties.** A party shown a document bearing the signature of a non-party may get up to 15 days to say whether it is genuine, forged or fraudulent (s 172(3)). A document filed by one defendant that affects another must be shown to that other defendant, and his statement taken (s 173(2)).
- **Persons holding documents.** The court may order a person who holds needed evidence to produce it, and may fine him up to Rs 10,000 if he does not (s 169; r 31). For evidence named in the plaint or defence but held by someone else, see s 162.

### Why it matters

These rules protect natural justice and prevent wasted judgments. A decree cancelling a deed binds only those who were parties. If a later buyer named in the deed was never heard, he can start fresh litigation. That is why, in [Padam Bahadur Karki](cases/9-7-padam-bahadur-karki.md), the plaintiffs sued both Padam Bahadur and the later buyers Jamuni Das and Ramchhabila Das. All of them took part in a chain of deeds over the same plot.

## 4.2.7 Agent and power of attorney

### Attorney (waris)

- **Appointment.** A party may appoint another person as his representative (प्रतिनिधि) to do any act in the case, or a specific act. That person is his attorney (waris). The appointment must be made by a deed, the power of attorney (वारिसनामा) (s 144).
- **Who may be one** (s 145). A person who:
    - is competent to make a contract;
    - owes no government dues, court fee, fine or penalty under a judgment; and
    - has not been convicted of forgery, fraud, corruption or an offence of moral turpitude.

    Even so, a member of one's own joint household may always be appointed.
- **Form.** The deed follows Schedule 13. The party signs or puts his thumbprint, before at least two witnesses. The deed names the witnesses and its writer, and they sign too (s 146).
- **Scope.**
    - One person may be attorney in several cases, or for several persons in one case (s 147).
    - He may not act for both sides at once (s 147, proviso).
    - One co-plaintiff or co-defendant may act for the others (s 148).
    - He may act in cases in different courts (s 149).
- **Powers.** An attorney has exactly the powers written in the power of attorney (s 150).
- **Registration.** The court checks the deed. If defective, it gives three days to correct it (s 151).
- **Change.** A party may revoke the deed at any time and appoint another attorney, or take over the case himself (s 152).
- **Use.** Notices may be served on an attorney appointed to receive them (s 113). An attorney may keep tarikh. A party in detention may act through one (s 140(6)).

### Authorised attorney (adhikrit waris)

- **Wide powers.** A person may appoint an authorised attorney to act in a named case or generally (s 153(1)). Such an attorney may file a plaint or defence, withdraw a claim, enter a compromise or take other legal action.
- **Authentication.** A general power of attorney, not naming a case, must be signed before a District Court judge. If made abroad, it is signed before a Nepali ambassador or consul general, who authenticates it. Photographs and citizenship or passport copies of both are attached. The fee is Rs 500 (s 153(2)-(4)).
- **Property.** A person who cannot attend the land office may transfer immovable property through an authorised attorney, giving the reason (s 154(1)-(3)). A gift that takes effect after death cannot be made this way (s 154(4)). Authenticating such a power to sell, or give up rights, to someone outside three generations costs Rs 5,000 (s 154(5)).
- **End of authority** (s 155). It ends automatically when:
    - the task or period is complete, or the stated event ends;
    - the giver cancels it by notice in two national dailies;
    - either person dies before the task is done;
    - the giver does the transfer himself;
    - a case arises between them about the property, or any case arises between them; or
    - the attorney withdraws in writing and returns the original deed.

### Attorney and lawyer

An attorney stands in for the party: he appears on tarikh, receives notices and, if authorised, files or settles. A **law practitioner** (कानून व्यवसायी) gives legal advice and argues the case (s 192(2)). One person can be both. But a lawyer does not become a party's attorney without a power of attorney.

## 4.2.8 Court's orders: interlocutory, interim and final

A court makes many orders between the plaint and the judgment. The Code groups the important ones in Chapter 14, "Interlocutory or Interim Orders" (ss 156-160).

### Interlocutory orders (अन्तर्कालीन आदेश)

- **Definition.** An interlocutory order is **any order other than a decision or judgment** in a case (s 156(1), Explanation). So orders on tarikh, bail, court fee, darpith complaints, preliminary pleas and evidence are all interlocutory.
- **Order to protect the claim.** During a case, the plaintiff or appellant may ask for an interlocutory order. The ground is that the claim will become **meaningless** (निरर्थक) unless the court acts. The court may stop an act at once, have something done at once, or keep something in **status quo** (s 156(1)).
- **Reasons.** If it seems right after hearing the petition, the court may issue the order, giving reasons (s 156(2)).
- **Order made on one side's hearing.** The other side may ask the same court to cancel it. The court hears both and decides. A party still unhappy may go to the appeal court (s 156(3)-(4)).
- **Challenge in the appeal court** (s 157). A party unhappy with an order of a lower court during a case may petition the appeal court within **15 days**. This covers orders that:
    - keep a party on tarikh or take a guarantee or court fee from him;
    - reject his complaint against a darpith;
    - decide locus standi, limitation or jurisdiction at the preliminary hearing; or
    - are any other order made during the case.

    He may also complain that a step was taken out of time, or not taken in time (s 157(2)). The appeal court calls for a report or the file, hears it, and may void the order or direct the lower court (s 157(3)-(4)). Such petitions are generally disposed of within a month (r 27).
- **The Administration of Justice Act 2073** says the same: any interlocutory order made during a case may be challenged in the appeal court one level up (s 15(1)).

### Interim orders (अन्तरिम आदेश)

An interim order is made while a **petition** is pending. Examples are an injunction petition, or a petition within a case, as in Ratan Lal. It holds the subject still until the petition is finally decided.

- **Ground.** The petitioner shows that the claim would be meaningless unless the subject is kept in status quo until the final decision (s 158(1)).
- **Kinds of order.** If it finds it proper and necessary, the court may, until the petition is settled (s 158(2)):
    - (a) issue an interim order for a set period;
    - (b) call the other side to discuss whether it should continue;
    - (c) issue a **conditional** interim order, if the applicant gives a written undertaking to compensate the other side for harm; or
    - (d) order the subject to be kept in status quo.
- **Final decision instead.** The court may decide the petition finally at once if that seems proper (s 158(3)).
- **End.** The interim order lapses automatically when the petition is finally decided (s 158(4)). It also lapses if the discussion cannot take place because of the petitioner (s 158(7)).
- **Second try.** Once refused, it may be sought again only on a fundamental change in circumstances (s 158(5)-(6)).
- **Reasons.** The court must give reasons for granting or refusing (s 158(8)). The main case goes on meanwhile (s 158(9)).
- **Vacating.** A party who was not heard may ask the same court to cancel it. The court may cancel it, or change its terms (s 159).
- **Compensation.** If an interim order is later cancelled or changed, the party it stopped may claim compensation from the party who sought it. The court awards it for actual loss only, not for imaginary or indirect loss (s 160).
- **Procedure.** The court fixes a date for discussion and may keep the parties on tarikh meanwhile. A non-party may ask to join the discussion. If the discussion is postponed, the interim order continues till the new date (r 28).

### Final orders and judgment

A **final order** (अन्तिम आदेश) or **judgment** (फैसला) ends the matter in that court. Any party unhappy with it has a right to appeal (s 15). A judgment decides a case begun by plaint. A petition, such as an injunction petition, ends in a final order. Both are covered under 4.2.14.

!!! example "Case: Ratan Lal Agrawal v Father James Dressman (NKP 2044)"
    An organisation feared that Ratanlal would build a house on two plots at Thapathali. It claimed the plots and had cases pending about them. It asked for an injunction until the cases ended. The Bagmati Zonal Court refused, because the organisation's right was disputed. The Supreme Court Full Bench (2043/11/22) issued the injunction. An injunction stops a feared act and keeps things as they were. It does not decide who owns the land. So the applicant need not first prove an undisputed right; otherwise injunctions would be pointless. A real fear of the act is needed, and Ratanlal's own written reply showed it. Today this power is in the Administration of Justice Act 2073 s 7(3) and Code ss 156-160. Read the [brief](cases/9-2-ratan-lal-agrawal.md).

## 4.2.9 Provisions and conditions for stay

### What "stay" means

To **stay** (स्थगन) is to halt something for a time, without deciding the dispute. In civil cases the law halts four kinds of thing:

| What is halted | Nepal's provision | How |
|---|---|---|
| **An act by a party** (building, selling, evicting) | Injunction: Administration of Justice Act 2073 s 7(3); interlocutory order: Code s 156; interim status quo: s 158(2)(d) | By court order on petition |
| **The case itself** | Multavi (postponement): s 201 | By court order |
| **A hearing date** | Adjournment of a listed hearing: District Court Rules r 65(2) | By court order, at most twice |
| **Carrying out a judgment** | Stay of execution: s 231 | Automatic by law, with exceptions in s 232 |

### Stay of an act: injunction

- **Power.** A District Court may issue an injunction (निषेधाज्ञा) to enforce a person's legal right. It may do so where any person or body in its district has harmed that right, or there is a fear that it will (Administration of Justice Act 2073 s 7(3)).
- **Petition.** It must show the court's jurisdiction, which right is feared to be harmed and how, the remedy sought, and its grounds (District Court Rules r 31(1)). The fee is Rs 500 (r 40(2)). The petitioner must say whether he has sought the same relief in the High Court (r 33(2)).
- **Show cause.** If the petition shows a **prima facie** (प्रथम दृष्टि) case, the court orders the respondent to show cause, with a written reply, why the order should not issue (r 31(2)). The reply is due within 15 days (r 36).
- **Interim order.** Meanwhile the court may issue an interim order, as in Code s 158 (r 34).

### Stay during a case: interlocutory and interim orders

These are the s 156 and s 158 orders above. A party asks the court to stop an act, have something done, or keep the subject in status quo, so that the claim does not become meaningless.

### Stay of execution

- **Automatic stay.** The court must stay execution of a judgment (s 231):
    - while the time to appeal is running;
    - while an appeal is pending, until it is finally decided;
    - after a revision order under s 219, until it is decided; and
    - after a review order under s 220, until it is decided.
- **No automatic stay.** A pending petition for review or revision does not by itself stop execution, unless the court orders otherwise. Nor does a suit to void a judgment or compromise (s 232(a)-(b)).
- **Early execution.** The court may allow execution before the appeal ends in two cases. One is where the property in dispute would spoil. The other is a compensation case where the winner gives a written undertaking to repay if he loses on appeal (s 232(c)).
- **Petition to stay execution.** In a High Court, the petition must give the date of the judgment and of any auction order or auction date, and the facts relied on (High Court Rules 2073 r 29(4)).

### Conditions for a stay

The statutes and the cases give these conditions.

1. **The claim would become meaningless** without it (ss 156(1), 158(1)). This is the core statutory test.
2. **A prima facie case** (District Court Rules r 31(2)). The applicant need not prove an **undisputed** right. A real dispute before a court is enough ([Ratan Lal](cases/9-2-ratan-lal-agrawal.md)).
3. **A real fear of the act** (आशंका). An injunction is basically issued only where an act is feared ([Ratan Lal](cases/9-2-ratan-lal-agrawal.md); Administration of Justice Act s 7(3)).
4. **Status quo, not a decision.** The order keeps things as they are. It must not decide the main dispute ([Ratan Lal](cases/9-2-ratan-lal-agrawal.md)).
5. **Reasons** for granting or refusing (ss 156(2), 158(8)).
6. **Hearing the other side.** The court may set a time limit and call the other side to discuss whether the order should continue (s 158(2)(a)-(b)). The other side may have an order made on one side's hearing cancelled (ss 156(3), 159).
7. **Undertaking and compensation.** A conditional order may require a written promise to compensate (s 158(2)(c)). A party harmed by an order later cancelled may claim actual loss (s 160).
8. **Use it or lose it.** The order lapses if the discussion cannot take place because of the petitioner (s 158(7)). In a District Court, if the party who got an interim order seeks to adjourn the hearing, the court may make the order inoperative (District Court Rules r 65(5)).
9. **No futile orders.** A stay that can no longer serve its purpose is refused. In [Pradip Kumar](cases/9-9-pradip-kumar-prasad-shah-kalwar.md), the Supreme Court refused an interim order "for now", because the petition itself said the judgment was already being carried out.

Textbooks, drawing on English and Indian practice, sum up these conditions as three tests. Is there a **prima facie case**? Does the **balance of convenience** favour the order? Would refusal cause **irreparable harm**? Nepal's statutes do not use these words, except "prima facie" in r 31(2). But the "claim would become meaningless" test does the same work as irreparable harm.

## 4.2.10 Collection of evidence

### Who must prove

In a civil case the plaintiff must prove his claim, and the defendant his defence (Evidence Act 2031 s 26). The court examines evidence only on the points it must decide.

### Documents (Chapter 15)

- **Filed with the pleadings.** Originals and copies of documents go with the plaint or defence (s 161(1)). If an original cannot be filed at once, a proper copy may be filed with the reason (s 161(4)). Where the Government of Nepal is a party, it may name the office holding the document, and the court may obtain it (s 161(2)-(3)).
- **Held by others.** If a document is in someone else's custody, the party says so. The court may order the holder to produce it, and fine him up to Rs 1,000 if he does not (s 162).
- **More time.** The court may give up to 15 days to file a document. A document not filed in that time is not taken as evidence (s 163).
- **New evidence.** New and important evidence found later may be filed with leave (s 164).
- **Originals returned.** The court keeps a certified copy and returns the original, but may demand it back. If not produced, it is not used (s 165).
- **Timing.** Generally no evidence is taken before the defence is filed or the time for it ends (s 166). Records in a government office named in the pleadings are ordered within seven days after that (s 167).

### Framing the issues

On the tarikh for the parties' appearance, the court reads the plaint, defence and documents in their presence and questions them. It then works out where they agree and where they differ (s 168(1); also s 190(1)-(2)).

- If they agree on the claim, or the defendant admits it, the court decides that day (ss 168(2), 190(3)).
- Otherwise it frames the **issues to be decided** (ठहर गर्नु पर्ने कुरा). It names the further evidence needed and fixes a date to take it (s 168(3)).
- No evidence is taken on anything outside the issues (s 168(4)).

**Pre-hearing discussion.** The court may hold a discussion with the parties and their lawyers to settle the issues (s 191). At it, the court may (r 34):

- send the case to mediation;
- identify the agreed and disputed points;
- fix any further evidence needed; and
- revise the case timetable.

### Taking the evidence

- **Order.** Plaintiff's evidence first, then the defendant's (s 170(1)).
- **Objections.** An objection that a document is inadmissible or irrelevant is decided at once and recorded (s 170(2)-(3)).
- **Genuine or forged** (सद्दे-कीर्ते). Each side is shown the other's original documents and must say whether each is genuine, forged or fraudulent. Its statement is recorded (s 172(1)). It may get up to three days to decide, or 15 days for a document signed by a non-party (s 172(2)-(3)). If it cannot tell, the court may send the document to an expert (s 172(7)). The party alleging forgery pays the expert's fee. If the document is found forged, the party who filed it repays that cost (r 32).
- **Survey and maps.** Where a land case cannot be decided without measuring the land, the court must order a survey. A court employee and an amin (surveyor) go to the site. There they prepare a map and a muchulka in front of the parties, a local level representative and two local people. A survey that breaks these rules is not admissible (s 177).
- **Site inspection.** The judge, or an expert he names, may inspect the site or object and report (s 178).
- **Video.** Oral evidence may be taken by video conference (Evidence Act s 37क; Code s 182).

## 4.2.11 Statements of parties and witnesses

### Statements of parties

- **In place of pleadings.** A party may make an oral statement in court in place of a plaint or defence. It counts as one (s 2(छ), (ञ)). A counterclaim in such a statement is recorded and a copy given to the plaintiff (s 121(6)).
- **Court questioning** (कागज). The court may at any time question a party about the case, in the other party's presence, and record his answers (s 171(1)).
- **On documents.** The statement on whether a document is genuine or forged (s 172) is a key party statement. Where an attorney or lawyer is on tarikh, the court may call the party himself (s 172(6)).
- **Persons called by the court.** A person called under s 123 makes a statement or written reply (s 123(2)-(3)).
- **Admissions.** What a party says may be used as evidence against him (Evidence Act s 9(1)). If the defendant admits the claim, the court may decide at once (s 190(3)).

### Witnesses (Chapter 16)

- **The party brings them.** Each party must produce his own witnesses on the date fixed. A witness who does not come is not examined (s 179(1)-(2); r 29). An exception applies where a road closure, disaster or similar event stopped him. The party must then apply within seven days with a local level recommendation, and the court fixes a new date within seven days (s 179(3)).
- **Court summons.** In a case involving the government or a public interest, the court itself summons a needed witness to appear within 15 days (s 180).
- **Where the witness is.** The judge or an employee may examine an ill witness where he lives. The applicant pays (s 181). An old, infirm, ill or overseas witness may be examined by video conference (s 182).
- **Commission** (banda sawal, बन्द सवाल). A witness in another district who cannot travel may answer written questions sent to that district's court (s 189). A party may ask for his own questions to be added (r 33).
- **On the parties' tarikh.** Witnesses are examined on the parties' tarikh (s 183(1)). All witnesses present must be examined that day, or the next working day if time runs out (s 186; r 30).
- **Oath.** The witness takes an oath to tell the truth, in the Schedule 16 form (s 184).
- **The judge examines.** The judge before whom the matter is listed examines the witness (s 185). He records the questions, answers and any cross-examination. He asks if there is anything more, has the witness and parties sign, and authenticates it (s 187). Refusals to answer are noted (s 188).
- **Order of questioning** (Evidence Act s 50).
    - The party calling the witness questions him first (सोधपुछ).
    - The other side may then cross-examine (जिरह).
    - The calling party may re-examine, limited to the cross-examination unless the court allows a new point.
    - Leading questions to one's own witness are barred if the other side objects, unless the court allows them.
    - A party may ask in writing for the court to do the questioning.
- **Fairness.** Witnesses are examined before all parties present. One witness must not hear another's evidence (Evidence Act s 49(2)-(3)). The court may stop questions that needlessly insult or annoy a witness (Evidence Act s 51). It must forbid improper, irrelevant, insulting or annoying questions (r 30(6)). If the other party has not come by 1 pm, the examination of the witnesses present still goes ahead (r 30(5)).

## 4.2.12 Postponement (multavi)

### Meaning

Multavi (मुलतबी) means putting a case on hold, without deciding it, until something else happens. Usually that is the decision of a linked case. It avoids two courts deciding the same point differently. For example, a court may hold back a case on a sale deed until a pending partition case decides whether the seller owned the land.

### The rules (s 201)

- **Grounds.** The court may order a case postponed, recording its reasons (s 201(1)). It may do so where:
    - one case cannot or should not be decided until a case in another court is decided;
    - deciding one would substantially affect the verdict in the other; or
    - postponement otherwise appears necessary during the proceedings or hearing.

    It may act on a party's petition or by itself.
- **Notice.** The court tells the parties and the other court at once (s 201(2)). When the other court decides its case, it sends a copy of the judgment within 30 days. If the parties are on tarikh, it sends them to the postponing court with a date within 15 days (s 201(3)-(4)).
- **Disagreement between courts.** If courts differ on which case to postpone, the appeal court decides within a month. If they have different appeal courts, the Supreme Court decides within three months (s 201(5)-(8)).

### Reviving the case (s 202)

- A party may apply, giving reasons, to revive (जगाउने) the case (s 202(1)-(2)).
- The court must revive it at once when the other case is decided, or when no reason remains to wait (s 202(3)).
- While postponed, the parties are released from tarikh (s 202(4)). On revival, they get seven days' notice to appear (s 202(5)).
- Earlier extensions of myad or tarikh are not counted again after revival (s 224(3)).

### Procedure (Rules r 42)

- Only the bench (इजलास) may postpone or revive a case.
- Parties on tarikh are told the same day. Others are sent notice within three days.
- A register of postponed cases is kept.
- On revival, the case keeps its original registration date.

### Critical view

Multavi avoids conflicting judgments and wasted work. But a party can misuse it to delay. A case may sleep for years waiting for another. The duties to pass on judgments within 30 days and to revive at once (ss 201(3), 202(3)) are the main safeguards.

## 4.2.13 Compromise of case (milapatra and melmilap)

### The principle

Before deciding any case, the court must give the parties a chance to compromise, directly or through mediation. If they agree, it can be done at any level (s 13). The Code treats settlement as part of justice, not a failure of it.

### Milapatra (compromise deed): s 193

1. The parties make a **joint petition** to compromise, at any stage and any level (s 193(1)).
2. The court reads out the petition and explains what it means and what follows from it (s 193(2)).
3. If they still agree, the court drafts the compromise deed in three copies (s 193(3)).
4. The deed is read out. If they agree, both sign or thumbprint it. The judge certifies that it was made before him. One copy goes in the file and one to each side (s 193(4)).
5. **Final.** No complaint lies against the deed, except that the other side has not done what it promised (s 193(5)). No appeal lies (s 206(b)).
6. **Land records.** If the deed needs dakhil kharej, namsari, registration or splitting of a plot, the court writes to the office within three days. The office acts within three days (s 193(6)-(7)).

**Court fee.** If a case is compromised in the first-instance court before evidence is taken, 25% of the court fee is kept and the rest refunded. After that stage, at any level, half is kept (s 82(1)).

### Melmilap (mediation): s 194

- The parties may jointly ask the court to send the case to mediation. The judge orders it if mediation seems suitable (s 194(1)-(2)). The court may also send it on its own view, if the parties agree (s 194(3)).
- Mediation then proceeds under the law. That law is the Mediation Act 2068 (s 3(3)-(4)). The parties name a mediator by agreement within the time the court sets, or the court appoints one with their consent (s 7).
- If the dispute is settled, the parties tell the court, which has them sign a compromise deed. The s 193 rules apply (Code s 194(5)-(7)).
- If mediation fails, the mediator reports within seven days. The court fixes tarikh and decides the case (Code s 194(8); Mediation Act ss 17-18).
- **District Courts.** On the first tarikh when both sides are present, the court's registrar (srestedar) must encourage the parties to mediate. If both agree in writing, he refers the case to a listed mediator, giving up to a month (District Court Rules r 51). The bench may refer a case with the parties' consent, giving up to three months in all (r 52). The parties choose the mediator, or the court does if they cannot agree (r 53).

### Limits

- A case brought with the Government of Nepal as plaintiff concerning public, government or community property cannot be compromised or mediated. A compromise that preserves the property as public, government or community property is allowed (s 195, as amended).
- A third party whose right is harmed by a compromise may sue to void it within 35 days of learning of it (s 92). A compromise may be made even while a review or revision is pending (Administration of Justice Act s 13).

!!! example "Compromise in a prescribed case"
    In [Padam Bahadur Karki](cases/9-7-padam-bahadur-karki.md), after the Supreme Court gave leave for revision, part of the plaintiffs' side settled. ## 4.2.14 Judgment

### Meaning

A **judgment** (फैसला) is the court's final, reasoned decision on the issues in a case begun by plaint. A party unhappy with a judgment or final order may appeal (s 15).

### From hearing to judgment

- **Hearing.** After the evidence, the court generally hears and decides the case within one month. Parties argue themselves or through lawyers or attorneys. The court may ask for written notes of argument (बहस नोट) (s 192).
- **Decision.** The court decides the issues normally on the day the hearing ends, and records it in the **verdict book** (rai kitab) (s 197(1)). If notes were ordered, it decides within seven days of receiving them (s 197(2)). If a complex point of law or fact prevents a same-day decision, it records why. It then fixes a date within at most 15 days to announce the decision (s 197(3)-(4)).
- **Preparation.** Before the hearing, the court staff prepare a summary of the file. The judge may edit it, and it must not contain personal opinion (s 197(6); r 35). The court must not give judgment while leaving part of the matter to be examined later (r 37).
- **Writing it.** The full judgment is prepared within **21 working days** of the decision, in the Schedule 19 form. The judge signs and dates it (s 198(1)-(2)). If it is late, a party may complain within three days to the High Court's Chief Judge, or for the Supreme Court to the Chief Justice. The judge is then directed to finish within a week (s 198(2क)-(2ख)).
- **Publication.** Each week, the court posts details of judgments authenticated that week on its notice board and website (s 198(3); r 38).

### Parts of a judgment (Schedule 19)

The form has three parts:

1. **Facts part** (तथ्य खण्ड): the parties, the witnesses and evidence of each side and those the court examined. Then the brief facts, the plaintiff's claim, the defence, and the evidence.
2. **Decision part** (ठहर खण्ड): the issues to be decided, the main points argued, and the court's finding with its grounds and the law applied.
3. **Schedule part** (तपसिल खण्ड): what is to be executed (court fee, amount awarded, compensation), whether an appeal lies, and other matters.

### After the judgment

- **No amendment.** Once signed, a judgment cannot be changed. Only minor errors that do not affect the decision may be corrected, such as a slip in figures or a name, or a typing error (s 199; rr 39-40).
- **Telling the parties.** Parties present hear the judgment and sign a paper saying so. If only the attorney is present, he signs (s 200(1)-(2)). An absent party is served a notice of the judgment within three days of its preparation. If an appeal lies, the notice also gives the time to appeal. Service follows Chapter 8 (s 200(3)). A party whose lawyer argued at the hearing is treated as present (s 200(4)). The file goes to the records section only after this notice has been sent (r 41).
- **Ex parte judgment.** Within 30 days of authentication, the court serves the absent party a notice of the judgment under Chapter 8. If that fails, it publishes a notice in a national daily (s 197(5)).
- **Appeal.** Within 30 days of learning of the judgment. This runs from signing the paper of having heard it, or from service of the notice (s 205(1)-(2)). If neither happened, it runs from taking a copy, if the copy is taken within six months (s 205(4)). Compare [Shivanath Shah Kanu v Bishwanath Shah Kanu](cases/9-10-shivanath-shah-kanu.md): a court's procedural slip cannot take away a party's right to appeal (Unit 6).
- **Execution waits** while the time to appeal runs and while an appeal is pending (s 231).
- **Finality.** Once final, the same matter cannot be tried again between the same parties (ss 14, 94).

| Point | Stay order | Interlocutory order |
|---|---|---|

| Overlap | A stay made during a case is a kind of interlocutory order | Only some interlocutory orders stay anything |

## Model answers

??? question "2077 Q7(c): Interlocutory order. (short note)"
    **Meaning.** An interlocutory order (अन्तर्कालीन आदेश) is any order a court makes during a case other than its decision or judgment (Muluki Civil Procedure Code 2074 s 156(1), Explanation). It deals with a step in the case, not the final rights.

    **Examples.** Orders keeping a party on tarikh or taking bail or a guarantee; orders on court fee; rejecting a complaint against a darpith; deciding locus standi, limitation or jurisdiction at the preliminary hearing; orders on evidence; and protective orders to stop an act or keep the status quo.

    **Protective interlocutory order (s 156).**

    - The plaintiff or appellant may apply where the claim would become meaningless unless an act is stopped, something is done, or the status quo is kept.
    - The court may grant it after a hearing, giving reasons.
    - An order made on one side's hearing may be cancelled by the same court on the other side's petition, after hearing both.

    **Challenge (s 157).** Within 15 days, to the court that hears appeals from that court. It may void the order or direct the lower court. Such petitions are generally decided within a month (Rules r 27). The Administration of Justice Act 2073 s 15(1) also allows any interlocutory order to be challenged one level up.

    **Case.** In [Ratan Lal Agrawal](cases/9-2-ratan-lal-agrawal.md) the Supreme Court upheld an order keeping disputed land as it was until the title case ended. Such an order keeps the status quo but does not decide ownership.

    **Conclusion.** Interlocutory orders keep a case moving and protect its subject. Because they are reviewable within 15 days, a wrong order can be corrected before it spoils the final judgment.

??? question "2078 Q7(c), old course: Stay order. (short note)"
    **Meaning.** A stay order (स्थगन आदेश) halts something for a time without deciding the dispute. It may halt an act, the case itself, a hearing, or the carrying out of a judgment.

    **Kinds in Nepal.**

    1. **Injunction.** A District Court may restrain a feared harm to a legal right (Administration of Justice Act 2073 s 7(3); District Court Rules rr 31, 34).
    2. **Protective orders in a case.** A protective interlocutory order (Code s 156) or an interim status quo order (s 158(2)(d)).
    3. **Multavi.** The case itself is postponed until a linked case is decided (s 201).
    4. **Stay of execution.**
        - Automatic while the time to appeal runs, while an appeal is pending, and after a revision or review order (s 231).
        - Not automatic for a mere pending review or revision petition (s 232).

    **Conditions.**

    - The claim would become meaningless without the stay (ss 156, 158).
    - A prima facie case (District Court Rules r 31(2)) and a real fear of harm. An undisputed right is not needed ([Ratan Lal Agrawal](cases/9-2-ratan-lal-agrawal.md)).
    - Reasons (s 158(8)).
    - A possible time limit and an undertaking to compensate (s 158(2)).
    - It lapses on the final decision (s 158(4)). Compensation is payable for actual loss if it is later cancelled (s 160).

    **Conclusion.** A stay preserves the subject of the dispute, so the final judgment is not made useless. The safeguards stop it being used merely to delay.

??? question "2078 Q7(d), old course: Date of presence (tarikh). (short note)"
    **Meaning.** Tarikh (तारिख) is the date a court fixes for a party to appear next. A party on tarikh must attend each date and sign the tarikh receipt (Code s 138(4)).

    **Rules.**

    - Every party is kept on tarikh unless the law or the court says otherwise (s 135(1)). After both sides' evidence, a party may be released with leave (s 135(2)).
    - Both get the same date (s 136). The purpose of each date is recorded and that work done (s 137).
    - Dates must not clash with a case in another court over 15 km away (s 138).
    - A holiday moves the date to the next working day (s 139).
    - A party in detention must inform the court (s 140).
    - Governments and constitutional bodies need not stay on tarikh (s 142). Appeals may proceed without tarikh (s 143).

    **Missing tarikh (s 141).**

    - The plaintiff before the defence: plaint dismissed.
    - The plaintiff after the defence: judgment on the admitted part, the rest dismissed.
    - The defendant: decided on the evidence.
    - Both: dismissed.
    - No fresh suit after dismissal.

    **Extension.** A missed tarikh may be extended up to 21 days, at most twice, for reasons beyond one's control (s 223). Listed reasons include mourning, childbirth, road closure, disaster, kidnapping and hospital stay (s 225). The case cannot be decided while the extension period runs (s 228).

    **Conclusion.** Tarikh keeps parties within the court's control, but it is costly for litigants. Release after evidence, attorneys and timetables reduce the burden.

??? question "Likely short note: Multavi (postponement)"
    **Meaning.** Putting a case on hold until something else, usually another case, is decided (मुलतबी).

    **Grounds (s 201(1)).** One case cannot or should not be decided before a case in another court, or deciding one would substantially affect the other. Or postponement is otherwise necessary. The court gives reasons.

    **Procedure.**

    - The parties and the other court are informed (s 201(2)).
    - The other court sends its judgment within 30 days and sends the parties back with a date within 15 days (s 201(3)-(4)).
    - Disputes between courts go to the appeal court (one month) or the Supreme Court (three months) (s 201(5)-(8)).
    - Only the bench may postpone or revive (r 42).

    **Revival (s 202).** On a petition, or at once when the other case is decided. Parties released from tarikh get seven days' notice.

    **Conclusion.** Multavi prevents conflicting judgments but must not become a tool for delay.

??? question "Likely short note: Milapatra"
    **Meaning.** A compromise deed made in court that ends a pending case by agreement (मिलापत्र).

    **Principle.** The court must give the parties a chance to compromise or mediate before deciding any case (s 13).

    **Procedure (s 193).**

    1. Joint petition.
    2. The court explains its meaning and effects.
    3. A deed is drafted in three copies, signed by both, and certified by the judge.
    4. Copies go to the file and to each party.
    5. If land records must change, the court writes to the office within three days.

    **Effects and limits.**

    - No complaint except non-performance (s 193(5)), and no appeal (s 206(b)).
    - Court fee refund: 75% if before evidence at first instance, otherwise 50% (s 82(1)).
    - Not allowed where the Government of Nepal is plaintiff, or for public, government or community property, except to keep it public (s 195).
    - A third party harmed may sue to void it within 35 days (s 92).

    **Mediation route.** Section 194 and the Mediation Act 2068 also lead to a milapatra.

??? question "Likely short note: Counterclaim"
    **Meaning.** A claim the defendant makes against the plaintiff in his statement of defence (प्रतिदाबी; s 121(1)).

    **Rules.**

    - It must be in the defence itself and limited to the subject of the plaint (s 121(2)).
    - Copies go to the plaintiff (s 121(3)-(4)), who files a written reply within 15 days (s 121(5)).
    - Court fee is due within seven days, or the counterclaim fails (s 121(7); r 20).
    - A person added later as a defendant cannot counterclaim (s 124(2)).
    - If the plaintiff misses tarikh, the counterclaim is still decided on the evidence (r 21(2)).

    **Value.** It settles both sides' claims on one subject in one case, saving time and avoiding conflicting judgments.

??? question "Likely short note: Persons mentioned in referred writings and deeds (petbolimo ultekhbhayeko)"
    **Meaning.** Persons named in the body (पेटबोली) of a plaint, defence or deed filed in a case who are not parties.

    **Rules.**

    - They must be clearly identified in the document (Rules r 6(4)).
    - The court may examine any person whose right a case would affect, or whose involvement appears from the plaint or defence. He gets seven days. If he is involved, he is kept on tarikh as a defendant; if he is only like evidence, he is not (s 123).
    - A third person whose right the decision would affect, as shown by the file, must be heard and treated as a principal party (s 176).
    - A party gets up to 15 days to say whether a document signed by a non-party is genuine (s 172(3)).
    - A holder of needed documents may be ordered to produce them (s 169).

    **Why.** No one should be bound by a decision without a hearing, and a judgment that leaves out an affected person invites fresh litigation.

??? example "Q1. Discuss in detail the process of notice by the court to the respondent in a civil case. (2080 Q2, 20 marks)"
    **Introduction**

    No one should be condemned unheard. This rule of natural justice, audi alteram partem, means a court must first tell a person that a case has been brought against him. It must also give him time to answer. The Constitution of Nepal 2015 makes this a fundamental right: every person has the right to be informed of any proceedings taken against him (Article 20(8)).

    In a civil case, this notice is called myad (म्याद). The Muluki Civil Procedure Code 2074 defines it as a notice or summons a court issues in a person's name in a case (s 2(ड)). At first instance the person is the defendant (प्रतिबादी). At the appeal stage he is the respondent (प्रत्यर्थी). Chapter 8 of the Code (ss 99-118) governs both. The Civil Procedure Rules 2075 (rr 15-18) fill in the details.

    **1. Issue of the notice**

    The notice may be delivered in two ways.

    - **By the plaintiff.** If the plaintiff says in the plaint that he will deliver it, he collects two copies with certified copies of the plaint and evidence. He must deliver them within three days of registration, excluding travel time. The defendant must accept and sign a receipt, and the plaintiff files it within seven days. The time for the defence runs from that receipt. If the defendant refuses, the plaintiff reports within two days, and the court serves the notice itself (s 99; r 15(1)-(3)).
    - **By the court.** Otherwise the court issues the notice within three days of registering the plaint, with a copy of the plaint and evidence (s 100; r 15(4)).

    The notice gives **21 days**, excluding travel time, to file a defence or counterclaim. A person the court itself adds as a defendant gets seven days (s 101). Travel time is the actual days by bus or train, or one day per 15 km where there is none (s 227). The notice is made in three copies, signed and sealed, in the Schedule 6 format (ss 102-103). Defendants in one household get one plaint copy between them (s 102(3)).

    **2. Service by the process server**

    The process server (तामेलदार) must serve it normally within seven days, or report why not (s 104). Section 105 sets an order of methods.

    1. **Personal delivery** anywhere, if he knows the person (s 105(2)).
    2. **At the home**, before a ward chair, member, secretary or government employee of the local level and two local people. He takes a receipt and records the date (s 105(3)).
    3. **To an adult of the same household**, with the same witnesses, if the person is absent (s 105(4)).
    4. **Pasting on the door** where all can see, with the same witnesses, if no one is found or they refuse (s 105(5)).

    Afterwards he records on the back of the notice how, when and to whom it was served. The witnesses sign (s 105(6)), and he reports to the court (s 105(7)). After a pasting, the third copy goes to the local level for its notice board. A proper pasting stays valid even if this step is missed (s 105(8)-(10)).

    **3. Special cases**

    - **Bodies and offices.** A company is served through its chief executive or other officers, else any employee. A government office, constitutional body or court is served through its head, or at the office. If that fails, it is served by registered post, and the return receipt proves service (s 105(11)-(17)).
    - **Modern means.** Post where houses are numbered or the person agreed (s 105(18)). Email or fax where the parties gave such an address for notices (s 105(21)). As a last resort, publication in a national daily, at the party's cost (s 105(22)-(24)).
    - **At court.** Notice may be handed to court staff, parties on tarikh in another case, attorneys, lawyers and witnesses there. One who refuses may be barred from acting in that court (s 106).
    - **Attorney or lawyer.** Service on one appointed to receive notices is valid (s 113).
    - **Unknown address.** Posting at the known village or the court notice board, plus newspaper publication (s 114).
    - **Outside the court's area.** Sent to the local court to serve (s 115). Abroad, through mutual legal assistance law (r 17).

    **4. Checks and sanctions**

    - The court officer must check every served notice and certify it if proper (s 110). Improper or missing service leads to fresh service (s 111).
    - Local officials must help (s 108). Refusal can bring a fine up to Rs 5,000 (s 109), but does not by itself spoil a proper service (s 108(2)).
    - A server who fails to give the local level its copy may be fined Rs 1,000 each time (s 112).
    - False service, obstruction or tearing off a notice may bring a fine up to Rs 10,000, one month's jail, or both (s 116).

    **5. Remedies for the defendant**

    - Before judgment, he may complain at any time that service was improper or never made. The court voids it and orders fresh service (s 117).
    - After an ex parte judgment caused by bad service, he may file an **appeal equal to a statement of defence**. It is due within 35 days of learning of the judgment, and within six months of it. If the appeal court finds service improper, it voids the judgment and sends the case back, treating the appeal as the defence (s 118).
    - But if notice was properly served and he ignored it, no appeal lies (s 206(a)).

    **6. Notice at other stages**

    - **Judgment.** An absent party gets a notice of judgment, with time to appeal, within three days of its preparation, served under Chapter 8 (s 200(3)). An ex parte judgment must be notified within 30 days, or published in a newspaper (s 197(5)).
    - **Appeal.** If the appeal court finds the lower decision may change, it orders the respondent to be called. He is served a 15-day notice under Chapter 8. He may file a written rebuttal (ss 213-214).

    **Case law**

    In [Pradip Kumar Prasad Shah Kalwar v Ram Bhajan Raya Yadav](cases/9-9-pradip-kumar-prasad-shah-kalwar.md) (NKP 2073), a notice of time to appeal was pasted on the defendant's door. Two witnesses were present and a copy went to the VDC. The Supreme Court held that the service followed the law. A party has a right to know of a judgment and to appeal. But once notice is properly served, the person claiming otherwise must show which step was missed. A properly served notice is not cancelled without reason to give a second chance.

    **Critical view**

    The Code improves on the old Muluki Ain. It adds plaintiff-delivered notice, email, post and newspaper service, strict court checks, penalties and the s 118 appeal. Weaknesses remain:

    - Pasting on a door can be abused, and witnesses are often hard to find.
    - Servers are overloaded, so the seven-day target is often missed.
    - Electronic service depends on the parties having agreed to an address.

    Courts must therefore check service strictly, as s 110 requires. They must also guard against defendants who dodge notice to delay, as Pradip Kumar shows.

    **Conclusion**

    Notice is the gateway to a fair civil trial. The Code sets a clear sequence: issue within three days, 21 days to answer, service within seven days, and checks and remedies. These rules balance the defendant's right to be heard with the plaintiff's right to a speedy decision. Proper notice binds the defendant; improper notice can undo even a final ex parte judgment.

??? example "Q2. Highlight stay orders and interlocutory orders. What are the basic differences between them? (2075 Q6, old course, 20 marks)"
    **Introduction**

    A civil case takes time. While it runs, a party may sell the land in dispute, pull down a house, or carry out a decision before an appeal is heard. The final judgment could then be useless. Courts therefore make orders during a case, and orders that halt things for a time. These are **interlocutory orders** and **stay orders**. In Nepal they are governed mainly by Chapter 14 of the Muluki Civil Procedure Code 2074 (ss 156-160), the Administration of Justice Act 2073 (ss 7(3), 15) and the court rules.

    **Interlocutory orders**

    *Meaning.* The Code defines an interlocutory order (अन्तर्कालीन आदेश) as any order other than a decision or judgment in a case (s 156(1), Explanation). It is an order on a step in the case, not on the final rights.

    *Examples.* Keeping a party on tarikh; taking bail, a guarantee or court fee; rejecting a complaint against a darpith; deciding locus standi, limitation or jurisdiction at the preliminary hearing; admitting evidence; and protective orders.

    *Protective interlocutory orders.*

    - The plaintiff or appellant may apply where his claim would become meaningless unless the court acts. The court may stop an act at once, have something done at once, or keep the status quo (s 156(1)).
    - The court hears the petition and, if satisfied, issues the order with reasons (s 156(2)).
    - If the order was made on one side's hearing, the other side may ask the same court to cancel it. The court hears both. A party still unhappy may go to the appeal court (s 156(3)-(4)).

    *Review.* Any party unhappy with an order of a lower court made during a case may petition the appeal court within 15 days (s 157(1)). He may also complain of steps taken out of time (s 157(2)). The appeal court may void the order or direct the lower court (s 157(4)). Such petitions are generally decided within a month (Rules r 27). The Administration of Justice Act 2073 s 15(1) confirms this route.

    *Interim orders.* While a petition is pending, the court may issue an interim order (अन्तरिम आदेश) to keep its subject in status quo (s 158). It may:

    - set a time limit;
    - call the other side to discuss continuing it;
    - make it conditional on a written undertaking to compensate; or
    - simply order status quo.

    It must give reasons (s 158(8)). The order lapses on the final decision (s 158(4)). A party not heard may have it vacated (s 159). A party harmed by an order later cancelled may claim compensation for actual loss (s 160).

    **Stay orders**

    *Meaning.* A stay order (स्थगन आदेश) halts something for a time without deciding the dispute. Nepali law knows four forms.

    1. **Stay of an act (injunction).** A District Court may issue an injunction where a person's legal right has been harmed or is feared to be harmed in its district (Administration of Justice Act s 7(3)). The petition shows the right, the threat and the remedy sought (District Court Rules r 31(1)). If a prima facie case appears, the court orders the respondent to show cause (r 31(2)). Meanwhile it may issue an interim order (r 34).
    2. **Stay within a case.** Protective interlocutory orders (s 156) and interim status quo orders (s 158(2)(d)).
    3. **Stay of the case (multavi).** The court postpones a case until a linked case in another court is decided (s 201), and revives it afterwards (s 202).
    4. **Stay of execution.** Execution is stayed by law while the time to appeal runs, while an appeal is pending, and after an order to revise or review (s 231). A mere petition for review or revision, or a suit to void the judgment, does not stop execution unless the court orders (s 232).

    *Conditions.*

    - The claim would become meaningless without it (ss 156, 158).
    - A prima facie case (District Court Rules r 31(2)) and a real fear of the act.
    - Reasons, a hearing of the other side, and possibly an undertaking (s 158).
    - Liability to compensate for actual loss if the order is later cancelled (s 160).

    *Case law.* In [Ratan Lal Agrawal v Father James Dressman](cases/9-2-ratan-lal-agrawal.md) (NKP 2044), the Supreme Court Full Bench upheld an injunction against building on disputed land until the title case ended. An injunction stops a feared act and keeps things as they were. It does not decide ownership. So an applicant need not first prove an undisputed right; otherwise injunctions would be pointless.

    **Basic differences**

    1. **Nature.** A stay halts something. An interlocutory order is any order during a case except the final decision.
    2. **Scope.** Interlocutory orders are a wide class covering many procedural steps. A stay is narrower: an order to stop.
    3. **Relationship.** A stay made during a case is one kind of interlocutory order. Many interlocutory orders, such as on tarikh or evidence, stay nothing.
    4. **Independence.** An interlocutory order exists only within a pending case. A stay can be the main relief, as in an injunction petition, or arise after judgment, as in a stay of execution.
    5. **Source.** Interlocutory orders: Code ss 156-157; Administration of Justice Act s 15. Stays: Act s 7(3); Code ss 156, 158, 201, 231-232; District Court Rules rr 31-34.
    6. **By law or by order.** An interlocutory order is always a court order. The stay of execution in s 231 operates by law.
    7. **Conditions.** A protective stay needs a prima facie case, a feared act, a risk that the claim becomes meaningless, and possibly an undertaking. Procedural interlocutory orders need only legal grounds.
    8. **Remedy.** Interlocutory orders: petition to the appeal court within 15 days (s 157). Interim stays: vacation in the same court (s 159) and compensation (s 160).
    9. **Duration.** An interim stay lapses on the final decision of the petition (s 158(4)). A multavi lasts until the other case is decided (s 202). Other interlocutory orders last until changed.

    **Critical view**

    Both kinds of order are needed: without them, final judgments could be emptied of meaning. But they are open to misuse. Parties seek stays to delay, and courts sometimes grant them without reasons. The Code answers with safeguards:

    - reasons are required (s 158(8));
    - an undertaking may be demanded (s 158(2)(c)) and compensation awarded (s 160);
    - the order lapses if the petitioner causes the discussion to fail (s 158(7));
    - an order may be made inoperative if its beneficiary seeks an adjournment (District Court Rules r 65(5)).

    Courts should also keep to Ratan Lal's limit: a stay preserves the status quo and must not decide the case in advance.

    **Conclusion**

    Interlocutory orders are the court's working orders during a case. Stay orders are the special class among them, together with injunctions and stays of execution, that halt events. Both protect the final judgment. A stay is narrower in purpose but can stand on its own; an interlocutory order is broader but exists only inside a pending case. Used with reasons and safeguards, both serve justice rather than delay.

??? example "Q3. Describe the stages of a civil suit from the filing of the plaint to the judgment under Nepalese law. (likely question, 20 marks)"
    **Introduction**

    Civil procedure is the set of rules by which a person enforces a civil right in court. In Nepal the main source is the Muluki Civil Procedure Code 2074, in force from 1 Bhadra 2075. It replaced the Adalati Bandobasta chapter of the Muluki Ain. The Civil Procedure Rules 2075 and District Court Rules 2075 add the details. A civil suit passes through a series of stages. The Rules make the court prepare a timetable for these stages in every case (r 26).

    **1. Plaint**

    - **Claim and standing.** A person must bring a claim to court by a plaint (s 85). He must show locus standi (s 10), and may sue only on his own right, except public-interest claims with leave (ss 86, 91).
    - **Joinder.** One plaint may cover related matters and any number of parties (ss 87-88). In [Padam Bahadur Karki v Man Kumari Basnet](cases/9-7-padam-bahadur-karki.md), one plaint was allowed against a chain of two deeds over the same plot made on the same day.
    - **Limits.** The plaint must be within the limitation period, naming the law (s 90). It must not repeat a decided matter (s 94). It must state the claim clearly in the Schedule 1 form (ss 95-96).
    - **Fees and papers.** The plaint fee is Rs 200, plus court fee (ss 63, 97). Documents and a list of witnesses go with it (s 161; Evidence Act s 37ख).

    **2. Scrutiny and registration**

    - A court officer checks format, limitation, jurisdiction and errors (s 126). A proper plaint is registered and a receipt given (s 98).
    - Curable defects are returned for correction (s 127). A plaint that cannot be registered is returned with an endorsement, darpith (s 128).
    - The plaintiff may petition the judge, or the appeal court, within seven days (s 130).

    **3. Notice to the defendant**

    - The plaintiff may deliver the notice himself within three days (s 99). Otherwise the court issues it within three days, with the plaint copy (s 100).
    - The defendant gets 21 days plus travel time (s 101).
    - The process server serves it within seven days. He tries personal delivery, then home service before witnesses, then an adult of the household, then pasting on the door (ss 104-105).
    - The court checks service, and orders fresh service if it was improper (ss 110-111, 117).

    **4. Defence and counterclaim**

    - The defendant files his defence within 21 days of service (s 119), in Schedule 9 form, with documents.
    - He may counterclaim on the same subject; the plaintiff replies within 15 days (s 121).
    - If no defence is filed, the court decides on the plaintiff's evidence (s 141(1)(c)).
    - The court may add affected persons as defendants, including persons named in the documents (ss 123-124, 176).

    **5. Tarikh**

    - Both parties are kept on tarikh with the same dates, and the purpose of each date is recorded (ss 135-137).
    - Missing tarikh can lead to dismissal or an ex parte decision (s 141).
    - Missed dates may be extended for reasons beyond one's control (ss 223, 225).

    **6. Preliminary hearing**

    If the defence pleads lack of locus standi, limitation or jurisdiction, the court hears those points first (ss 131-132). If a plea succeeds, the case is dismissed (s 133). If not, the plea cannot be raised again on appeal (s 134).

    **7. Interlocutory and interim orders**

    - During the case, the plaintiff may seek orders to stop an act or keep the status quo where the claim would otherwise become meaningless (s 156).
    - Interim orders may be conditional on an undertaking (s 158), and are vacated or compensated if wrong (ss 159-160).
    - Any interlocutory order may be challenged within 15 days (s 157). In [Ratan Lal Agrawal](cases/9-2-ratan-lal-agrawal.md), the Supreme Court held such an order keeps the status quo without deciding title.

    **8. Framing issues and pre-hearing discussion**

    - The court reads the pleadings with both parties present and identifies agreed and disputed points (ss 168, 190).
    - If the defendant admits the claim, it decides at once (s 190(3)). Otherwise it frames the issues and the evidence needed (s 168(3)).
    - It may hold a pre-hearing discussion, which may lead to mediation (s 191; r 34).

    **9. Evidence**

    - **Documents.** Plaintiff's evidence first (s 170). Each party says whether the other's documents are genuine or forged (s 172). The court may order documents produced (ss 162, 169), survey land (s 177) or inspect a site (s 178).
    - **Witnesses.** Each party produces his witnesses on tarikh (s 179). The judge examines them on oath and records each witness's deposition (ss 184-187). There is cross-examination and re-examination (Evidence Act s 50). Video conference and commission are available (ss 182, 189).

    **10. Postponement, withdrawal and compromise**

    - The case may be postponed (multavi) until a linked case is decided (s 201), and revived later (s 202).
    - The plaintiff may withdraw before judgment (s 196).
    - The parties may settle at any stage by a milapatra (s 193) or through mediation (s 194). The court must offer them that chance (s 13).

    **11. Final hearing**

    After the evidence, the court hears the parties or their lawyers, generally within a month. It may order written notes of argument (s 192).

    **12. Judgment**

    - **Decision.** The court decides on the day of hearing and records it in the verdict book. It may take seven days after notes, or 15 days for complex issues (s 197).
    - **Writing.** The judgment is prepared within 21 working days in the Schedule 19 form, with facts, decision and schedule parts. It is signed and published weekly (s 198). It cannot be amended except for minor errors (s 199).
    - **Notice.** Absent parties get notice of judgment and of time to appeal within three days (s 200). Ex parte judgments are notified within 30 days (s 197(5)).
    - **Appeal and execution.** An appeal lies within 30 days (s 205). Execution waits meanwhile (s 231).

    **Critical view**

    The Code sets time limits for nearly every stage: 3 days, 7, 21, 15, a month, and 21 working days. It adds modern tools such as email service, video witnesses, pre-hearing discussion and case tracks. District Court Rules r 40क aims to finish simple cases in six months, general ones in a year and special ones in 18 months. In practice delays remain. Service fails, parties miss tarikh, hearings are adjourned and cases are postponed. Many time limits say "generally", so missing them carries no sanction. Better case management and use of mediation are the main hopes.

    **Conclusion**

    A civil suit in Nepal moves from plaint, through notice, defence, tarikh, preliminary hearing, interim orders, issues, evidence and final hearing, to judgment. Each stage protects either the right to be heard or the right to a speedy decision. The Code's design is sound. Its success depends on courts enforcing its time limits and on parties using compromise and mediation where they can.

## Test yourself

??? question "1. Within how many days must the court issue a notice to the defendant, and how long does the defendant get to answer?"
    The court issues it within three days of registering the plaint (s 100(1)). The defendant gets 21 days, excluding travel time, to file a defence (ss 101(1), 119(1)).

??? question "2. List, in order, the methods by which a process server serves a notice under s 105."
    Personal delivery anywhere if he knows the person. Then at the home before local level officials and two local people. Then to an adult of the household. Then pasting on the door with a copy to the local level. Post, email or fax, or newspaper publication are used in the cases the section allows.

??? question "3. What remedy does a defendant have if an ex parte judgment was given because the notice was improperly served?"
    An appeal equal to a statement of defence in the appeal court, within 35 days of learning of the judgment and within six months of it (s 118). If service was improper, the judgment is voided and the case sent back.

??? question "4. What is the difference between a myad and a tarikh, and how long can each be extended?"
    A myad is a notice with a time limit sent to a person; a tarikh is a date given to a party already before the court. A missed myad can be extended once by up to 15 days; a missed tarikh up to 21 days, at most twice (s 223).

??? question "5. What happens if the plaintiff misses tarikh before the defence is filed? And after?"
    Before: the plaint is dismissed. After: judgment is given on whatever the defendant admitted and the rest is dismissed. In both cases no fresh suit lies (s 141).

??? question "6. Define an interlocutory order and say how it can be challenged."
    Any order in a case other than the decision or judgment (s 156(1), Explanation). A petition lies to the appeal court within 15 days (s 157); an order made on one side's hearing may first be challenged in the same court (s 156(3)).

??? question "7. Give four conditions the Code attaches to interim orders."
    Any four of: a set period; calling the other side to discuss continuation; a written undertaking to compensate; reasons for granting or refusing; lapse on final decision; lapse if the petitioner causes the discussion to fail; a fresh petition only on a fundamental change; compensation for actual loss if vacated (ss 158-160).

??? question "8. What did Ratan Lal Agrawal v Father James Dressman decide?"
    An injunction keeps the status quo and does not decide title, so an applicant need not prove an undisputed right; a real fear of the act is needed. The Full Bench upheld the injunction against building on disputed land.

??? question "9. When does execution of a judgment stay automatically?"
    While the time to appeal runs, while an appeal is pending, and after an order for revision or review until decided (s 231).

??? question "10. What fraction of court fee is kept when a case is compromised?"
    25% if compromised in the first-instance court before evidence is taken; half at any later stage or level. The rest is refunded (s 82(1)).

??? question "11. Who may not be appointed an attorney?"
    A person not competent to contract, one who owes judgment dues, court fee or fines, or one convicted of forgery, fraud, corruption or moral turpitude. A member of one's joint household may still be appointed (s 145).

??? question "12. Within what time must a judgment be written up, and what are its three parts?"
    Within 21 working days of the decision (s 198(1)). The facts part, the decision part and the schedule part (Schedule 19).
