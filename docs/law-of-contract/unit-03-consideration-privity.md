# Unit 3: Consideration and privity

!!! abstract "In one line"
    Consideration asks what is exchanged for a promise; privity asks who may enforce it. They are different questions, and Nepal's statutory answers differ from familiar textbook rules.

!!! warning "Unverified"
    Nepal provisions were checked against the Nepali government copy through Jestha 2076. Later amendments remain incompletely verified. Comparative English rules here are introductory, not an account of every exception.

## Why it matters for the exam

- Privity was a short-note topic in both available papers, 2078 Q7(a) and 2080 Q7(e).
- A strong answer states section 530's rule and beneficiary exception. Do not stop at the English common-law slogan.

## 3.1 Meaning of consideration

A buyer's promised price and a seller's promised delivery explain exchange. In India, section 2(d) covers an act, abstinence or promise at the promisor's desire, supplied by the promisee **or another person**. Something done without the promisor's request is not automatically consideration under that definition.

**England:** consideration supports a simple contractual promise. It is not merely a person's private motive. Affection may explain why someone promises a gift but is not itself a bargained exchange. An English deed is a distinct formal route. Do not treat English deed rules as Nepal's general writing rule.

**Nepal:** section 505 does not separately require consideration. A Nepal answer starts with its four statutory conditions. This omission alone does not settle every promise of a gift or every special transaction.

## 3.2 Rules of consideration

| Rule | Explanation and example | Jurisdiction |
|---|---|---|
| At the promisor's desire | Requested delivery differs from unsolicited work later billed to a stranger | India, 2(d) |
| Promisee or another person | A parent can supply the requested payment for the promisee | India, 2(d). This is not permission for every stranger to sue |
| Real, lawful and sufficiently certain | An impossible or unlawful exchange cannot validate the agreement | India, 10, 23, 29; Nepal separately applies 505 and 517 |
| Need not be equal in value | A poor bargain is not automatically no bargain. Inadequacy may be evidence about consent | India, 25 explanation 2; introductory English doctrine distinguishes sufficiency from adequacy |
| Existing duties require care | Doing only what is already owed may raise questions about fresh exchange | English textbook issue. Its detailed exceptions are beyond the verified account here |

## 3.3 Kinds of consideration

**Past:** work already done before the later promise. India 2(d) can cover requested prior acts. Section 25(2) separately addresses compensation for specified earlier voluntary or legally required acts. English law generally rejects purely past consideration, but requested acts undertaken on an understood remunerated basis need closer analysis. *Pao On v Lau Yiu Long* explains such conditions.

**Executed:** an act is performed in exchange for the promise, such as completing a reward task. **Executory:** the exchange is of promises to act later. These describe timing, not value. A promise to deliver next week can be consideration even though the goods have not arrived.

## 3.4 Exceptions to “no consideration, no contract”

This is an **Indian and English comparative heading**, not a statement of Nepal's section 505.

India section 25 protects specified agreements based on natural love and affection between near relations where written and registered, promises to compensate for specified earlier acts, and signed written promises to pay all or part of a time-barred debt. Its explanation preserves gifts actually made. Section 185 says consideration is unnecessary to create agency. Section 63 allows the promisee to remit performance, extend time or accept another satisfaction. Do not describe each as an identical application of section 25.

England distinguishes a properly made English deed from a simple contract. Do not say every written promise is a deed. Detailed execution requirements must be separately checked before applying that route to a real instrument.

## 3.5 Privity of contract

Nepal section 530(1) begins with the rule that a party may demand performance from the other party. Its proviso, the exception attached to the rule, permits a person for whose benefit a contract was made to demand performance despite not being a party.

Example: A contracts with B to pay a stated sum to C for C's education. Analyse whether the agreement was made for C's benefit. Do not dismiss C solely as a stranger. A passer-by who would incidentally benefit from A's new shop is not in the same position. Subsection (2) addresses joint demands where several persons contracted jointly, subject to contrary terms.

Distinguish an intended beneficiary from an authorised agent and an assignee. The agent acts for a principal; assignment concerns transferred contractual rights under section 529. Those are different legal routes.

**England:** the traditional party-only rule has been modified by the Contracts (Rights of Third Parties) Act 1999. Section 1 allows enforcement in specified express-right or benefit cases, subject to conditions including identification and contrary intention. “No third party can ever sue in England” is outdated.

## Nepal's law

Use 505 for enforceability, 529 for assignment and 530 for parties and beneficiaries. Nepal's beneficiary rule is express statutory text. It need not be justified by borrowing an Indian family-settlement exception or an English trust analysis.

## Full 20-mark answers

??? example "Q1. Explain consideration, its rules and exceptions, comparing Nepal with India."
    !!! warning "Unverified"
        This answer uses the checked Nepal text through Jestha 2076. Later amendments remain incompletely verified.

    Consideration concerns what is supplied in exchange for a contractual promise. In an ordinary sale, the buyer promises payment and the seller promises delivery. Each commitment explains the return sought for the other. This differs from a motive such as affection, generosity or gratitude. The doctrine is prominent in Indian and English textbooks, but a Nepal examination answer must distinguish those systems from the Civil Code 2074. The correct starting point is not to assume that a familiar foreign maxim is Nepal's statutory rule. Comparison is valuable precisely because the systems do not use an identical formulation.

    **The Indian statutory definition**

    Section 2(d) of the Indian Contract Act 1872 describes an act, abstinence or promise at the desire of the promisor, supplied by the promisee or another person. The definition has several parts. The act must have the relevant connection with the promisor's desire. Something done without that request is not automatically the price of a later promise. Consideration may also involve refraining from an act rather than transferring money. Finally, the exchange can be a promise to act in the future. Physical performance at the moment of agreement is therefore not always necessary.

    The reference to another person is important. If a seller promises delivery to a buyer in return for payment supplied by the buyer's parent, the parent's involvement does not automatically mean that there is no consideration under section 2(d). However, this is a rule about the source of exchange, not a complete rule about who may enforce the agreement. Privity must be considered separately. A stranger's supplying value and a stranger's having a right to sue are different propositions. An answer that merges them loses a distinction central to understanding both doctrines and their operation.

    **Rules about quality and value**

    The exchange must be assessed with the Act's rules about lawfulness and certainty. An unlawful object or consideration cannot validate an agreement. The mere presence of a payment does not cure a prohibited transaction. Similarly, an exchange which cannot be identified sufficiently may fail the relevant statutory requirements. These limits show that consideration is one part of a wider framework. It is not a device which transforms every arrangement into a lawful contract. Capacity, consent, legality and other applicable conditions still matter when the court decides whether the agreement may be enforced.

    Adequacy and existence should also be distinguished. The value given need not always equal the market value of what is promised. Section 25's second explanation recognises that inadequacy alone does not make an agreement void where consent is freely given, though inadequacy may help determine whether consent was free. A bicycle sold below market price is not automatically without consideration. Conversely, an extreme imbalance may be relevant evidence of exploitation. The point is to examine the actual legal defect rather than replace consent analysis with a rule that every unequal bargain is invalid.

    **Kinds of consideration**

    Past, executed and executory consideration describe timing. Past consideration concerns an earlier act relied upon to support a later promise. India's section 2(d) can include requested prior conduct within its wording. Section 25(2) also provides a distinct exception for promises to compensate specified earlier acts. Executed consideration involves an act performed in exchange for a promise, as in completing the task of a reward offer. Executory consideration involves exchanged promises for future performance. A supplier's promise to deliver next month can therefore form part of a bargain before the delivery actually occurs.

    English textbook law generally rejects an act completed independently in the past as consideration for a later promise. That statement needs qualification where the earlier act was requested and undertaken on an understood remunerated basis. Pao On explains that a prior request alone is insufficient without further conditions. The comparative lesson is not that all earlier acts have the same effect in every jurisdiction. The student must ask when the act occurred, why it occurred and which legal rule is being applied. Nepal's Code should not be displaced by either an Indian or English shortcut.

    **Indian exceptions and related provisions**

    Section 25 states a general rule against agreements without consideration and then sets out exceptions. One concerns natural love and affection between near relations where the agreement meets the specified writing and registration requirements. Near relationship by itself is not enough. Another concerns a promise to compensate for specified earlier voluntary or legally required acts. A further exception concerns a signed written promise to pay all or part of a debt which limitation prevents the creditor from enforcing. These are distinct conditions, and a short answer should not reduce them all to moral obligation.

    The section's explanation also preserves gifts already made. Elsewhere, section 185 provides that consideration is unnecessary for creation of agency. Section 63 permits the promisee to dispense with or remit performance, extend time or accept another satisfaction. These provisions explain why the broad maxim requires careful legal treatment even within India. They do not all operate through the same exception or have identical facts. English law also distinguishes a properly executed English deed from a simple contract. Writing alone does not necessarily turn a document into a deed or satisfy its particular legal requirements.

    **Nepal's different approach**

    Nepal's section 505 lists binding consent, capacity, a definite subject creating an obligation and lawful obligation. It does not separately list consideration. An examination answer should state that difference directly and then apply the Code. It would be wrong to say that Indian section 25 automatically invalidates a Nepal promise without exchange. It would be equally unsafe to infer that every gift promise must therefore be enforceable in Nepal. Required form, special transaction rules and the other conditions of validity still require examination. Omission from one list is not a substitute for analysing the whole legal framework.

    In conclusion, consideration explains exchange and remains a major comparative doctrine. Its rules distinguish requested value, timing, adequacy and legal quality, while its exceptions qualify an overly broad maxim. Nepal's statutory framework requires its own analysis rather than automatic transplantation. A good answer first explains the doctrine fairly, then identifies the governing jurisdiction and applies the correct conditions to a concrete example. This method preserves the educational value of Indian and English materials while ensuring that a Nepal legal conclusion is actually grounded in the Civil Code's provisions and the relevant facts.

??? example "Q2. Explain privity of contract and the position of an intended beneficiary in Nepal."
    !!! warning "Unverified"
        This answer uses the checked Nepal text through Jestha 2076. Later amendments remain incompletely verified.

    Privity of contract concerns the relationship between being a party to an agreement and having the right to enforce it. The familiar starting rule is that contracting parties demand performance from each other. A person outside the agreement does not acquire an enforcement right merely because they would like it performed. However, a starting rule is not always the complete rule. Nepal's Civil Code section 530 expressly addresses an intended beneficiary who is not a party. The subject therefore requires both the general principle and the statutory exception, together with a distinction between privity and consideration.

    **Meaning and purpose of the rule**

    Contractual duties normally arise from the commitment which the parties have undertaken. A agrees to repair B's roof, and B agrees to pay. They define the work, price and performance period. Privity identifies them as the ordinary persons entitled to demand the promised performance. The rule helps connect rights with the agreement that created them. A neighbour who prefers the roof repaired cannot simply demand the same contractual remedies as B. Incidental interest in a transaction is not the same as a legal right under its terms. This is the practical point behind the party relationship.

    The principle also encourages care in identifying the parties. The person who signs a document may act for someone else, while the person receiving goods may be different from the buyer. A complete answer should therefore ask in whose name and on whose behalf the agreement was made. Labels such as customer, representative and beneficiary can describe different positions. They should not be exchanged loosely. Determining who actually undertook the obligation is necessary before deciding whether a claimant is a party, a representative of a party or a person claiming under a separate legal exception.

    **Nepal's express beneficiary exception**

    Section 530(1) begins with the rule that a party can demand performance from the other party. Its proviso then allows a person for whose benefit a contract was made to demand performance even though that person is not a party. This is a direct statutory qualification. It means that the answer cannot end with the proposition that only parties may ever enforce. The beneficiary must connect the claim to the purpose of the agreement. A mere hope of gaining something from someone else's transaction does not establish that the contract was made for that person's benefit.

    Consider an agreement between a parent and a financial contributor that a stated sum will be paid for a named student's education. The student is not necessarily one of the contracting parties, yet the promised payment may be intended for the student's benefit. Section 530 directs attention to that feature. The agreement's words, object and circumstances help establish whether the student falls within the proviso. This is more precise than saying that all family members may sue on all family agreements. The right follows from the contract's beneficial purpose, not simply from kinship or sympathy.

    Contrast a shop owner's supply contract with a wholesaler. Nearby residents may benefit from the shop having stock, but that alone does not make each resident the intended beneficiary of every supply promise. A disappointed resident must identify more than an indirect commercial advantage. This distinction protects the contracting framework from unlimited claims while giving effect to genuinely intended benefits. The exact remedy still depends on the agreement and the applicable law. Recognising an enforcement route does not guarantee that every term, amount or remedy asserted by the beneficiary has actually been proved.

    **Joint parties and other legal routes**

    Section 530(2) addresses joint demands where two or more persons contracted jointly, subject to contrary terms. This concerns people who are already parties acting together. It differs from the proviso admitting a nonparty beneficiary. The distinction is useful in an examination because several claimants may appear in one problem. Their number does not decide their legal status. Some may be joint promisees, one may be an agent and another may be an intended beneficiary. The answer should classify each person before applying the relevant rule to their particular demand for performance.

    Assignment is another separate route. Section 529 concerns transfer of contractual rights and obligations under stated conditions. An assignee claims through a transfer, whereas an intended beneficiary relies on the benefit contemplated in the original contract. Agency likewise involves authority to act for a principal. The principal may be the actual contracting party even though an agent handled negotiations. These doctrines are sometimes grouped as exceptions to a broad privity slogan, but their legal mechanisms differ. Explaining the mechanism prevents an answer from becoming an unexamined list of names with no connection to the facts.

    **Privity is not consideration**

    Consideration concerns the exchange supporting a promise in systems that require it. India's section 2(d) allows the requested act, abstinence or promise to come from the promisee or another person. That provision does not, by itself, give every person supplying value a right to sue on every contract. A person can be a stranger to the exchange yet still be a contracting party, or can supply value without being a party. The questions must therefore be asked separately. Who supplied the exchange is not automatically the answer to who may enforce the promise.

    Nepal's section 505 does not separately list consideration among its enforceability conditions. That difference makes it especially important to avoid teaching privity as if it were simply another name for the consideration rule. Section 530 provides the relevant Nepal enforcement framework. The student should apply its words directly. Comparative doctrines may explain why the issue has generated debate elsewhere, but they cannot remove an exception expressly contained in the Code. A short note that accurately states this distinction can be stronger than a longer answer containing several foreign authorities without any explanation of Nepal's actual text.

    **English reform and conclusion**

    England's traditional common-law rule has been modified by the Contracts (Rights of Third Parties) Act 1999. Section 1 permits enforcement in specified cases involving an express right or a term purporting to confer a benefit, subject to conditions including identification and contrary intention. This is not a universal licence for any stranger to sue. It shows that modern English law also requires more than repetition of the historical party-only slogan. The comparison should identify the statute and its limited purpose without assuming that its detailed conditions are identical to Nepal's proviso.

    In conclusion, privity links contractual enforcement with the parties but is qualified in Nepal by an express intended-beneficiary rule. A sound answer identifies the parties, reads the promised benefit, distinguishes incidental advantage and considers the claimant's legal route. It then separates beneficiary enforcement from assignment, agency and consideration. This approach respects both the security of private agreements and the intention to benefit a person outside them. It also gives a practical method for answering a factual question instead of treating privity as an absolute barrier unaffected by statutory language or the purpose of the transaction.

## One-minute revision

- Exchange and enforcement standing are different questions.
- India 2(d) defines consideration; 25 provides a rule and exceptions.
- Requested value may come from another person under Indian law.
- Nepal 505 does not separately list consideration.
- Nepal 530 expressly protects an intended beneficiary.
- England's 1999 Act modifies the historical privity rule.
