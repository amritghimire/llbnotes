# Unit 1: Introduction to contract law

!!! abstract "In one line"
    Contract law decides which promises create legal duties, what those duties require, and what happens when a party fails to perform.

## 1.1 Nature of contract law

Contract law is part of private civil law. It supports voluntary cooperation, such as hiring a vehicle or agreeing to repair a roof. It protects an enforceable promise through civil remedies. Breaking a contract is not automatically a crime.

Freedom is limited. Parties cannot make an unlawful purpose valid by agreeing to it. Nepal's section 507 permits choices about terms, remedies and dispute resolution subject to the Code. Section 499 requires good faith in fulfilling obligations. Section 494 distinguishes duties created by contracts from duties created by law, unjust enrichment and wrongful harm. A duty to compensate someone you injure need not arise from a promise.

## 1.2 Concept and meaning of contract

Under section 504, an enforceable agreement between two or more persons to do or not do something is a contract. An offer and its acceptance create the agreement. The legal requirements make it enforceable.

Example: Sita agrees to repair Hari's roof for a stated price by a stated date. Identify both parties, the work and their duties. An invitation to tea usually lacks the expressed commitment to legal obligation required by section 505. Do not assume that every family arrangement is legally ineffective. Its terms and circumstances matter.

**England:** *Balfour v Balfour* concerned a domestic maintenance promise held unenforceable for lack of contractual intention. It is not a Nepal decision or a rule that relatives can never contract. A later English judgment, *Rogers v Wills*, paragraphs 177–178, explains that intention depends on evidence.

## 1.3 Essential elements of a valid contract

Section 505(1) requires:

1. Consent expressing a wish to be legally bound.
2. Capacity to contract.
3. A definite subject creating an obligation.
4. A lawful obligation.

Read these with section 504 on agreement, section 506 on capacity, sections 517–519 on validity, and section 505(3) on required formalities. A signature does not cure coercion, incapacity or illegality. Consideration means something given in exchange. It is central to Indian and English textbook doctrine but is not separately listed in Nepal's section 505. See [Unit 3](unit-03-consideration-privity.md).

## 1.4 Types of contract

| Basis | Types | Example or caution |
|---|---|---|
| Legal effect | Valid, void, voidable, unenforceable | These are different legal consequences, not synonyms |
| Expression | Written, oral, by conduct | Section 505(2), subject to required formalities |
| Exchange of promises | Bilateral and unilateral | A roof-repair agreement; a public reward accepted by the specified act |
| Performance | Executed and executory | Work already completed; work still owed |
| Event | Unconditional and contingent | An obligation depending on a future event is addressed by section 513 |
| Subject | Sale, guarantee, bailment, agency and others | Specific chapters supplement general rules under section 514 |

The last four classifications help organise study. They do not replace statutory tests. A public reward is specifically regulated by section 511.

## 1.5 History of contract law in Nepal

The Law Commission annual report 2067/68, printed page 25, records that the Contract Act 2056 replaced the Contract Act 2023. The Civil Code 2074 was authenticated on 2074/6/30 and began on 2075 Bhadra 1 under section 1(2). Its Part 5 brings contracts and other obligations into a wider civil code.

## 1.6 Basic features of the Civil Code 2074 on contract

Part 5 has 18 chapters, sections 493–691. It combines general obligations, formation, validity, performance, remedies and specific transactions. Sections 507 and 515 support autonomy and interpretation of the parties' common intention. Sections 517–519 distinguish legal defects. Section 530 permits an intended beneficiary to demand performance even though not a party. The Code also addresses quasi-contract and unjust enrichment separately. Its last two Part 5 chapters concern torts and defective products, which are outside this paper's substantive unit sequence.

??? example "Q1. Explain the essentials of a valid contract under Nepal's Civil Code."
    !!! warning "Unverified"
        This answer uses the checked Nepal text through Jestha 2076. Later amendments remain incompletely verified.

    A contract is a legally enforceable agreement, not merely any promise that two people exchange. Section 504 of Nepal's Muluki Civil Code 2074 describes an agreement between two or more persons to do or abstain from doing something which the law can enforce. This definition connects voluntary agreement with legal obligation. A promise to meet a friend and an agreement to repair a shop may both involve expectations, but only an arrangement satisfying legal requirements creates contractual duties. The essentials must therefore be explained through the Code rather than through a list copied unchanged from a foreign textbook.

    **Agreement and binding consent**

    Section 504 connects agreement with offer and acceptance. One person proposes a commitment and the other accepts it in the same meaning. Suppose a carpenter offers to make ten desks of specified dimensions for a school at an agreed price. The school's acceptance must concern that proposal. An answer asking for twenty desks at another price raises a different proposal. Agreement requires a meeting on identifiable terms. The communication rules help decide when that meeting occurs. They matter because negotiations can contain several proposals before a final commitment is reached.

    Section 505(1) first requires consent expressing a person's willingness to be bound. This distinguishes a legal undertaking from casual conversation or an incomplete negotiation. The words, setting and conduct provide evidence. A written quotation accepted by an authorised school representative differs from a friendly remark that the carpenter might help one day. The requirement does not mean that every contract must expressly mention litigation. It means that the parties must express a binding commitment, read in context. Section 515 directs interpretation towards their common intention and the agreement as a whole.

    **Capacity of the parties**

    The second condition is the ability to contract. Section 506 excludes minors and persons not of sound mind, subject to its provisions about representatives and special law. A person usually lacking soundness may contract during a sound interval, while someone usually sound cannot contract during an unsound interval. The legal inquiry concerns capacity at the relevant time. A guardian's power to act for a protected person is different from that person's own power to bind themselves. The Code also requires legal persons to act through properly authorised management or representatives.

    This condition protects people whose apparent agreement may not represent legally competent choice. A trader cannot avoid the statutory protection by getting a young child to sign a printed form. Conversely, a company is not incapable merely because it has no human body. Its legal personality allows transactions through authorised people. Both points matter to the legal analysis. Capacity concerns the party's legal ability, while authority concerns whether a representative can bind that party. Both must be considered before treating an apparent agreement as enforceable against a particular person.

    **A definite subject and lawful obligation**

    The third condition is a definite subject creating an obligation. The parties must be able to identify what is owed. Ten desks of stated specifications, a price and a delivery date provide a workable subject. A vague promise to provide something useful whenever convenient may not. Certainty is practical rather than a demand to anticipate every possible event. The question is whether the terms can bear a reasonable meaning and define the obligation. Section 517 addresses uncertainty, vagueness and impossibility among its grounds of invalidity.

    The fourth condition is lawfulness. The parties cannot enforce an agreement whose purpose is prohibited by law. A bargain to deliver banned goods does not become valid because its price and delivery date are clear. Section 517 also addresses immoral purposes, public order and public interest. Section 507 grants autonomy subject to the Code, so freedom of contract operates inside legal limits. This protects the wider community as well as the contracting parties. Private consent cannot authorise conduct that the applicable law forbids or removes from private bargaining.

    **Free consent and legal form**

    The conditions must be read with the validity chapter. Section 518 allows an injured party to ask a court to set aside a contract produced by coercion, undue influence, fraud or misrepresentation. A signature obtained through an unlawful threat does not settle the consent question. However, a voidable contract is not identical to one invalid from its beginning. Until avoided, it operates as a lawful contract under the checked text. A good answer identifies the defect and its legal consequence instead of calling every defective agreement simply illegal.

    Section 505(2) recognises writing, speech and conduct. An ordinary transaction need not fail merely because no paper was signed. Nevertheless, subsection (3) preserves required procedures and formalities. Section 519 addresses failures such as absence of legally required writing or registration and lack of representative authority. Thus, the general recognition of oral agreement is not permission to ignore a special statutory form. The relevant transaction must first be identified. Its particular legal rules may impose requirements beyond the general conditions, which the parties cannot remove by convenience or consent.

    **Consideration and the conclusion**

    Indian and English textbooks commonly describe consideration as an essential exchange for a promise. India's Contract Act defines it in section 2(d), and section 25 states a general rule with exceptions. Nepal's section 505 does not separately list it. This difference should be stated carefully. It does not prove that every promise of a gift is enforceable. The other Code requirements, special rules and form still matter. A Nepal answer should neither import the Indian rule automatically nor replace it with an equally sweeping claim about gratuitous promises.

    In conclusion, a valid contract requires enforceable agreement, binding consent, competent parties, a definite subject and lawful obligation, together with the applicable rules on consent and form. These requirements work as a connected inquiry. Each requirement can be applied to an ordinary Nepal transaction to show what changes if it fails. Contract law supports reliable private cooperation while protecting vulnerable parties and public interests. Its central balance is voluntary commitment within legal conditions, rather than enforcement of every promise regardless of the manner in which it was made.

??? example "Q2. Distinguish the main types of contracts and explain their legal effects."
    !!! warning "Unverified"
        This answer uses the checked Nepal text through Jestha 2076. Later amendments remain incompletely verified.

    Classification makes contract law easier to apply because different questions require different kinds of distinction. Calling an arrangement written tells us about its expression. Calling it void tells us about its legal effect. Calling it bilateral tells us about its structure. These descriptions can overlap. A written bilateral agreement may still be void because its purpose is prohibited. The first task is therefore to identify the basis of classification. Nepal's Civil Code supplies important legal categories, while textbooks provide additional descriptive categories. Both are useful if their functions and sources remain clear throughout the answer.

    **Classification by legal effect**

    A valid contract satisfies the requirements for legal enforcement. Under sections 504 and 505, parties must reach an enforceable agreement with binding consent, capacity, a definite subject and lawful obligation. A shopkeeper's agreement with a competent painter to repaint the shop for an agreed price illustrates the category, assuming the other legal requirements are satisfied. The obligations are not merely social expectations. Each party must perform what the agreement requires. The fact that a dispute later arises does not itself mean that no valid contract ever existed between the parties.

    A void arrangement is different. Section 517 describes contracts that are not legally valid and identifies grounds including unlawful purpose, certain restraints, incapacity, initial impossibility and mutual mistake about an essential fact. Under subsection (3), invalidity exists from the beginning. An agreement to carry out prohibited conduct cannot gain validity simply because both parties willingly signed it. The parties' intended contractual rights cannot override the statutory defect. Subsection (4), however, recognises that an invalid part does not automatically prevent enforcement of every remaining lawful part. The possibility of separation must be examined rather than assumed.

    A voidable contract can be set aside by a court at the injured party's initiative. Section 518 addresses coercion, undue influence, fraud and misrepresentation. Consider a seller who uses an unlawful threat to obtain a buyer's consent. The injured party has a statutory route to challenge the contract. The category preserves a choice that does not exist in the same form for an arrangement void from the start. Under the checked text, a voidable contract remains enforceable until avoided. It is therefore inaccurate to say that coercion always means that no operative agreement ever existed.

    An unenforceable contract is a third category of defect under section 519. The section addresses required writing, formal procedure or registration, and transactions made without sufficient representative authority. Suppose a transaction legally requires registration but the parties omit it. Their agreement may be clear, competent and freely made, yet the court cannot enforce it in the manner claimed. This category shows why validity analysis needs more than asking whether there was a signature or a willingness to perform. The particular legal form can matter independently of the ordinary formation requirements.

    **Classification by expression**

    Section 505(2) recognises written contracts, oral contracts and contracts inferred from conduct. A written repair order records terms on a document. An oral arrangement may arise when a customer asks a tailor to stitch a specified garment for an agreed price. Conduct can show agreement when a person accepts a service in circumstances expressing assent to its terms. The practical difference concerns how commitment is expressed and proved. It does not establish that writing is always necessary or that conduct always creates obligations without the other requirements of a contract.

    These general forms remain subject to section 505(3). Where the applicable law requires a particular procedure or formality, the parties must comply. A student should distinguish freedom of expression from freedom to disregard mandatory form. Writing also has an evidential advantage even when not compulsory. It can record scope, price and deadlines and reduce later disagreement. Nevertheless, the legal question remains whether the agreement meets the applicable rules. A long written document may contain unlawful terms, while a short oral agreement may be enforceable where no special form is required.

    **Classification by the structure of promises**

    A bilateral contract involves an exchange of promises. A carpenter promises to build desks and a school promises to pay. Both sides undertake future obligations. A unilateral reward arrangement invites acceptance through performing a specified act rather than through a return promise alone. Nepal's section 511 deals specifically with public advertisements offering remuneration. A person who knows the reward offer and completes the required act may become entitled under that section. This example demonstrates that the textbook distinction has practical significance, but the statutory conditions must still be applied carefully.

    The distinction must not be confused with the number of persons involved in a transaction. A unilateral contract is not a contract with only one human being and no other party. It describes the way acceptance and obligation operate. Equally, a bilateral agreement can involve several persons on one side. What matters is the exchange of commitments. English law's famous reward case, Carlill, is useful for comparison. It does not replace Nepal's section 511, including its rules about knowledge, multiple performers, expiry and revocation of a publicly advertised reward.

    **Classification by performance and event**

    An executed obligation has already been performed. An executory obligation remains to be performed. If the painter has finished the shop but payment is due next week, performance is complete on one side and outstanding on the other. These terms describe the state of performance. They do not decide whether the original agreement was valid. A party who has received performance may still dispute its quality or whether payment is due. The applicable performance and remedy provisions must answer those further questions on their own terms.

    A contingent contract depends on a future event. Section 513 regulates when obligations arise or fail in several event-dependent situations. An agreement to do work if a specified permit is obtained provides a simple illustration. The event is not necessarily an unlawful wager. Classification requires attention to its purpose and legal setting. Specific contracts such as sale, bailment, guarantee and agency also have separate chapters, supplemented by general rules under section 514. In conclusion, classification is valuable when it identifies the exact legal question. A complete answer connects each label with its consequence instead of memorising disconnected names.

??? example "Q3. Explain the nature, development and main features of Nepal's contract law."
    !!! warning "Unverified"
        This answer uses the checked Nepal text through Jestha 2076. Later amendments remain incompletely verified.

    Contract law helps people organise their affairs through commitments that the legal system will recognise. Farmers buy equipment, businesses obtain supplies, and households hire services because they can agree on what each side must do. The law supplies conditions for recognising those agreements and responses when performance fails. Nepal's present teaching framework centres on the Muluki Civil Code 2074. To understand that framework, an answer should connect the nature of contractual obligation, the supported modern legislative sequence and the Code's main features. Historical detail must rest on evidence rather than a story invented to fill a syllabus heading.

    **Nature of the subject**

    Contractual obligation generally arises from a voluntary agreement. Section 504 defines the relevant agreement as one enforceable by law between two or more persons to do or abstain from doing something. This definition gives the subject a private-law character. The parties choose a transaction and undertake duties towards each other. However, choice alone does not determine enforceability. Section 505 requires binding consent, capacity, a definite subject and lawful obligation. Contract law therefore combines private decision with public legal standards. It does not treat every assurance or expectation as a legal duty.

    The distinction between contract and other obligations is equally important. Section 494 identifies several sources of obligation, including law, contract, quasi-contract, unjust enrichment and wrongful conduct. A person may owe compensation for harm even though they made no promise to the victim. Someone may have to return an unjustified benefit without an agreed exchange. These examples show that the law of obligations is wider than contract law. Studying the subject inside a civil code helps explain its relationship with neighbouring doctrines without collapsing them into a single category of voluntary agreement.

    **The supported modern development**

    The Nepal Law Commission's annual report for 2067/68, at printed page 25, records that the Contract Act 2023 operated before the Contract Act 2056 replaced it. This is a dated official source for the modern sequence. It shows that Nepal had a separate contract statute before the present Code and that the 2056 Act represented a further legislative stage. The source should be attributed accurately. The report's financial year is its historical setting. A later website upload date does not turn the report into a description of today's law.

    The Civil Code 2074 was authenticated on 2074/6/30. Section 1(2) provides commencement on 2075 Bhadra 1. Its preamble describes amendment and consolidation of civil provisions previously found in the Muluki Ain and other laws. The change therefore places contract within a larger organised statement of civil law. The supported history is a movement through separate contract legislation into civil codification. Detailed assertions about ancient practice or exact contract provisions in early Muluki Ain versions require further sources. They should not be offered as established facts merely because an examination question asks about development.

    **A general framework with specific transactions**

    Part 5 contains eighteen chapters, extending from section 493 to section 691. The early chapters address obligations, formation, validity, performance and remedies. Later chapters deal with transactions such as sale of goods, guarantee, bailment, pledge, agency, carriage, lease and hire-purchase. This organisation gives students a practical method. First identify the general issue, such as whether competent parties agreed. Then identify any special rule for the transaction. Section 514 generally applies the formation, validity, performance and remedy framework to other contracts under the Code or law.

    The framework also includes quasi-contract and unjust enrichment. Their inclusion acknowledges that some duties arise without an ordinary agreement. The final Part 5 chapters concern torts and defective products, demonstrating the wider scope of obligations. Those subjects should not be expanded into this contract paper's substantive unit sequence, but their place in the structure matters. Section 692 begins Part 6, so it should not be included as the last contract-part section. Accurate boundaries prevent an organisational mistake from spreading into the overview, sources and examination answers.

    **Autonomy, consent and interpretation**

    Section 507 recognises party autonomy over subject matter, terms, remedies and methods of dispute resolution, subject to the Code. This allows people to tailor agreements to their needs. A supplier and buyer may specify quantity, delivery stages and responses to delay. Freedom remains limited by capacity, legality and other mandatory requirements. The Code does not permit the parties to defeat public safeguards merely by calling their arrangement private. This balance allows commerce and cooperation while preserving legal control over arrangements that threaten protected interests or exploit a defective process of agreement.

    Section 515 provides an approach to interpretation centred on the parties' common intention. Where that intention cannot be established, the reasonable meaning in context matters. The whole agreement should be considered rather than isolated words selected to favour one side. Section 499 also requires good faith in fulfilling obligations. Together, these provisions help explain why contract law is more than literal enforcement of a sentence. The nature of the commitment, the surrounding context and the statutory framework all contribute to determining what the parties must actually do.

    **Distinctive features and critical assessment**

    Section 505 does not separately list consideration among its four conditions. This differs from the familiar Indian statutory rule and English simple-contract doctrine. The difference must be taught carefully, without inferring that every gratuitous promise is enforceable. Section 530 also states an exception allowing an intended beneficiary to demand performance although not a contracting party. These provisions show why foreign textbooks must be read comparatively. Their concepts can help explain problems, but their conclusions cannot automatically be presented as Nepal's law where the Code provides a different rule.

    The Code further distinguishes void, voidable and unenforceable contracts in sections 517 to 519. This improves analysis by connecting different defects with different consequences. Its practical value depends on accurate application, accessible language and proof of the relevant facts. Codification does not eliminate disputes about consent, meaning or performance. In conclusion, Nepal's contract law supports voluntary legal cooperation within statutory limits and now sits in a wider civil framework. A strong historical answer combines the verified legislative sequence with the Code's organisation and principles, while openly excluding unsupported claims about earlier periods.
