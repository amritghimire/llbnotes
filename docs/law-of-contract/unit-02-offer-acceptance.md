# Unit 2: Offer and acceptance

!!! abstract "In one line"
    An offer proposes a binding deal; acceptance agrees to that deal in the same meaning. Communication and timing decide whether the parties have actually contracted.

## 2.1 Offer

### 2.1.1 Meaning

Section 504 explains a proposal made to obtain another person's agreement to do or not do an act. “I will sell this bicycle to you for Rs 8,000 if you agree by Friday” identifies a possible bargain. “What might you pay for my bicycle?” seeks information.

### 2.1.2 Rules of a valid offer

The proposal must express a commitment sufficiently definite to be accepted. Identify the subject, promised act and relevant conditions. Read it with section 505's certainty and binding-consent requirements. A price list or invitation to negotiate is not automatically an offer. Its wording and context matter. An unlawful proposal cannot produce an enforceable obligation merely through acceptance.

### 2.1.3 Types of offer

Specific offers address an identified person. General offers invite anyone meeting the conditions, such as a public reward. Express offers use words; conduct may also communicate a proposal. A counter-offer changes proposed terms. A continuing supply proposal may invite individual orders, but whether an order creates a contract depends on its terms. These are descriptive categories. Section 511 gives special rules for public rewards.

### 2.1.4 Rules on communication

Under section 508(1), sending an offer is complete when the intended recipient knows of it. A reward cannot be accepted through an act performed without knowledge of the advertisement, section 511(1). Distinguish sending a message from communicating its content.

### 2.1.5 Communication and revocation

Section 509(1) permits notice of withdrawal. It bars withdrawal once the offeror has already received acceptance before the relevant withdrawal notice is received. Write a timeline identifying sender, recipient and receipt of each notice. Do not import India's dispatch-based section 4 rule as Nepal's rule.

### 2.1.6 Termination

Section 510 covers expiry of the fixed or reasonable acceptance period, death or loss of soundness in its specified circumstances, withdrawal under 509, counter-offer, and failure to fulfil a required preliminary act or condition. A proposal does not remain available indefinitely merely because no written cancellation exists.

## 2.2 Acceptance

### 2.2.1 Meaning

Acceptance is assent in the same meaning as the offer, section 504. “Yes, at Rs 8,000” differs from “Yes, but only at Rs 6,000.” The second changes a term and is a counter-offer under 509(5).

### 2.2.2 Rules of valid acceptance

Acceptance must respond to the offer and meet its conditions. Section 508(3) recognises conduct, performance of a condition, or acceptance of a benefit or service as possible assent. Mere silence cannot be imposed as assent by an offeror declaring that failure to refuse will count as acceptance, section 508(4). Distinguish silence from meaningful conduct.

### 2.2.3 Communication and revocation

Section 508(2) uses receipt by the offeror for completion as against the offeror, and the offeror's knowledge that assent was given for completion as against the offeree. Do not replace that wording with “posting always binds both sides.” Section 509(2) permits withdrawal of an acceptance by notice, but not if the offeror already received acceptance before its withdrawal notice. Under 509(3), where refusal and later acceptance compete, their order of arrival matters. Under 509(4), the specified competing notices received simultaneously result in no contract.

### 2.2.4 Termination

Before effective acceptance, its withdrawal or expiry of the underlying offer may prevent formation. A purported acceptance after an offer has ended does not revive it automatically. Once a contract exists, ending that contract is a separate performance or termination question. Do not use “termination of acceptance” to suggest that an already binding contract can always be cancelled by changing one's mind.

## Nepal's law

Section 511 adds useful detail. A knowing performer of the advertised act can claim the reward. The first performer has priority, and simultaneous performers share. Expiry of the advertised period ends the offer. Withdrawal uses the same medium. Completed performance before publication of withdrawal remains protected, subject to prompt notice. A person who notified the advertiser and began work has a right to appropriate remuneration for work done until withdrawal under subsection (6).

Section 512 determines the place of formation. The place specified by the parties prevails under subsection (3). Otherwise read the place for receiving acceptance under subsection (1) and the receipt fallback under subsection (2).

**England:** *Carlill v Carbolic Smoke Ball Co* [1893] 1 QB 256 illustrates acceptance of a serious public reward through performance. It helps explain unilateral offers. Nepal's answer must still apply section 511 rather than assuming every English detail governs Nepal.

## Compare

| Issue | Nepal | India | England |

| Changed assent | Counter-offer, 509(5) | Section 7 requires absolute acceptance | Agreement must correspond to the offer |
| Silence | Offeror cannot impose it under 508(4) | Apply communication and acceptance rules | Silence alone is not normally acceptance |
| Public reward | Detailed section 511 | Section 8 recognises acceptance by performance | *Carlill* provides a leading illustration |

## Model answers

??? question "2078 Q3: rules on valid offer (topic paraphrase)"
    Define offer under 504. Explain certainty and binding intention under 505. Apply communication under 508, withdrawal under 509, expiry under 510 and public reward under 511. Finish with a timed example.

??? question "Practice short note: counter-offer and mere inquiry"
    A counter-offer changes proposed terms and engages 509(5) and 510. A genuine request for information need not reject the offer. Decide from the actual words. Explain why “Will you accept Rs 6,000 instead?” needs context, while “I accept only at Rs 6,000” clearly changes the terms.

## Full 20-mark answers

??? example "Q1. Explain offer, acceptance and their communication under Nepal's Civil Code."
    !!! warning "Unverified"
        This answer uses the checked Nepal text through Jestha 2076. Later amendments remain incompletely verified.

    Agreement is the starting point of a contract. Section 504 of the Muluki Civil Code 2074 connects agreement with a proposal and assent to that proposal in the same meaning. These ideas are commonly called offer and acceptance. Their purpose is practical. Before ordering a person to perform, a court must be able to identify what was proposed, what was accepted and whether the parties became bound. Communication rules answer a further question about timing. Negotiation may occur through letters, messages, spoken words or conduct, so sending, receiving and knowing must be distinguished carefully.

    **Meaning and quality of an offer**

    An offer proposes doing or abstaining from an act with the intention of obtaining the recipient's assent. Suppose Maya tells a competent adult buyer that she will sell a specified bicycle for Rs 8,000 if the buyer agrees by Friday. The proposal identifies a subject, a price and an acceptance period. It differs from asking what the bicycle might be worth. The latter may invite discussion without committing Maya to sell. The distinction is drawn from the words and context, not from the mere appearance of a figure in a conversation.

    An offer must also fit the requirements of section 505. There must be a binding commitment and a definite subject capable of creating an obligation. A proposal so uncertain that no reasonable meaning can be given to it cannot create enforceable duties through a simple affirmative response. Its subject must be lawful. An offer to carry out prohibited conduct does not become enforceable because the recipient agrees enthusiastically. Formation analysis therefore operates together with capacity, certainty and legality. It cannot be isolated from the other requirements that make an agreement a legally recognised contract.

    **Acceptance in the meaning offered**

    Acceptance means assent to the proposal as made. If the bicycle buyer agrees to the stated bicycle at Rs 8,000 within the acceptance period, the response corresponds to the offer. If the buyer says that they accept only at Rs 6,000, the response changes the proposal. Section 509(5) treats conditional or altered acceptance as a counter-offer. This protects each party from being bound to terms they did not accept. A request for clarification, however, must be interpreted in context rather than automatically treated as a rejection of the offer.

    Acceptance need not always appear in a signed letter. Section 508(3) recognises conduct, compliance with a condition or acceptance of an offered benefit or service as possible ways of assenting. The conduct must still express agreement in the circumstances. A person receiving a service without any relevant choice or knowledge should not automatically be treated as accepting every term later asserted by the supplier. Section 508(4) makes a related limit explicit. An offeror cannot make silence acceptance simply by stating that failure to reject within a period will count as assent.

    **Communication of the proposal**

    Section 508(1) regards the sending of an offer as complete when the person addressed knows about it. This prevents a proposal remaining only in the offeror's mind from creating an agreement. Maya's unsent draft message is not the same as a proposal known to the buyer. Evidence of delivery may help establish communication, but the legal language should be stated accurately. The student must identify the intended recipient and what that recipient knew. An unsupported assumption that all electronic messages were read immediately would not answer a disputed factual problem.

    The knowledge requirement is particularly clear in public rewards. Under section 511(1), someone who performs the advertised act without knowing of the advertisement is not entitled to its remuneration under that provision. A person who finds a lost bag by chance and only later hears about the reward differs from someone who acts knowing the reward proposal. The distinction shows why a beneficial act alone does not resolve every formation question. The law connects the act with a communicated proposal and applies the particular conditions attached to that kind of offer.

    **Communication of acceptance**

    Section 508(2) must be explained in Nepal's own terms. Acceptance is complete against the offeror when the offeror receives the communicated acceptance. For the offeree, the text speaks of the offeror knowing that the offeree has accepted. The provision should not be rewritten as a universal rule that posting binds both parties. India's section 4 contains a different distinction concerning dispatch and receipt. English cases also distinguish forms of communication in their own doctrinal setting. Those rules can be compared, but the Code controls a question framed under Nepal law.

    Timing becomes important when messages cross. A buyer may send acceptance and then a faster withdrawal, or an offeror may send revocation while awaiting a reply. Section 509 supplies specific rules. Acceptance cannot be withdrawn if the offeror has already received it before the withdrawal notice. Likewise, the offeror cannot revoke in the circumstances where acceptance has already reached them before the relevant withdrawal notice is received. A good factual answer lists each message, its sender, its recipient and the order of receipt before applying the statutory rule to the sequence.

    **Public proposals and the place of formation**

    Section 511 provides a detailed scheme for publicly advertised remuneration. It addresses knowledge, first and simultaneous performers, expiry, withdrawal through the same medium, completed acts and notified work already begun. These details prevent the law from treating a public reward exactly like every private negotiation. Carlill is a useful English illustration of a serious advertisement accepted through performance. It is not the legal source of Nepal's statutory protections. A Nepal answer should explain the comparison and then return to the relevant subsection of the Code for its actual conclusion.

    Section 512 addresses where the contract is formed, including the parties' own specification and the statutory rules connected with receipt of acceptance. This can matter to later procedural analysis, although formation place alone does not answer every jurisdiction question. In conclusion, offer and acceptance identify the agreed commitment, while communication rules determine whether and when it became binding. Clear analysis moves through proposal, matching assent, knowledge, receipt and any competing notice. It also checks the other section 505 conditions, because communication of an agreement does not by itself make an unlawful or incompetent transaction valid.

??? example "Q2. Explain revocation and lapse of offers, with special reference to public rewards."
    !!! warning "Unverified"
        This answer uses the checked Nepal text through Jestha 2076. Later amendments remain incompletely verified.

    An offer gives its recipient an opportunity to accept a proposed commitment, but that opportunity does not necessarily last forever. It may end through withdrawal, expiry, counter-offer or another statutory event. An acceptance may also be withdrawn before the relevant legal point is reached. Nepal's Civil Code addresses these matters in sections 509 to 511. The rules protect both freedom to withdraw before commitment and the security of a bargain already completed. Public rewards require special attention because the person accepting may act through performance rather than by sending an ordinary return promise to the advertiser.

    **Revocation by notice**

    Section 509(1) permits an offeror to withdraw an offer by notice. The fact that the offeror privately changes their mind is not enough to answer the communication issue. The notice must be considered in relation to the recipient and any acceptance already communicated. The section prevents revocation where acceptance has already been received by the offeror before the relevant revocation notice is received. A trader should therefore not assume that an unsent cancellation or a delayed message can undo an agreement already reached under the Code. The statutory timing conditions must be applied to the evidence.

    Suppose a furniture maker offers to supply specified tables. The buyer sends an acceptance, while the maker sends withdrawal. The answer must establish the order in which the legally relevant notices arrived. Saying that the maker posted first or changed their mind first does not supply the whole analysis. The Code's rules concern communication, and section 508 provides the foundation. Exam answers are clearer when they identify each event separately. The purpose is to decide whether the offer remained available when acceptance became effective, rather than to reward whichever side tells the more sympathetic story.

    **Withdrawal of acceptance and competing notices**

    Section 509(2) permits the offeree to withdraw an acceptance by notice. However, if the offeror has already received the acceptance before the withdrawal notice, the acceptance cannot be revoked under that provision. A later regret therefore differs from a timely withdrawal that reaches the relevant person first. This gives the recipient of a completed acceptance a measure of security. It also shows why a student should distinguish withdrawal of acceptance before formation from cancellation of a contract after formation. The latter requires its own legal basis and consequences.

    Subsection (3) addresses a person who sends refusal and then acceptance. The first notice to arrive determines whether a contract is concluded in the stated situation. Subsection (4) addresses the specified competing notices received simultaneously and provides that no contract is concluded. These are useful examination details because they resist a broad slogan such as the last message always wins. The relevant question is arrival under the statutory scheme, not merely chronological drafting. A careful answer states the particular subsection and avoids extending its simultaneous-receipt rule to situations the text does not describe.

    **Lapse under section 510**

    Section 510 identifies circumstances in which an offer ends. If an offer specifies a time for the offeror to receive acceptance, failure to receive it within that time matters. Where no time is specified, acceptance must still be communicated within a reasonable period. Reasonableness depends on the transaction. A proposal about rapidly changing goods may not remain open as long as one concerning a less urgent service. The Code therefore allows the factual setting to matter while preventing the recipient from treating every offer without a printed deadline as available indefinitely.

    Death or loss of soundness also matters in the particular circumstances stated in section 510. The provision addresses the offeror before receipt of acceptance and the offeree before the offeror receives acceptance. It also covers revocation under section 509, counter-offer and purported acceptance without performing an act or condition required beforehand. These grounds should be explained separately. A counter-offer changes the proposed bargain, whereas expiry ends the opportunity by passage of time. Failure to meet a preliminary condition prevents the attempted acceptance from satisfying the offer as made. Different facts lead through different legal routes.

    **Public reward offers**

    Section 511 deals with an advertised promise to pay remuneration for a specified act. A familiar example is a public reward for returning a lost document. The provision requires knowledge of the advertisement. Someone who performs the act without knowing about it cannot claim the remuneration under subsection (1). If several persons perform, priority belongs to the first performer. If performance is simultaneous, the reward is shared, with sale and division of proceeds where the reward cannot itself be divided. The precise requirements should be distinguished from a loose assertion that everyone who helps must receive the entire reward.

    A reward offer with a stated performance period ends when that period expires. Under subsection (4), revocation uses the same medium as publication. This is a practical way to communicate withdrawal to the audience previously invited to act. The rule is nevertheless qualified. Subsection (5) protects a person who completed the specified act before publication of the revocation, subject to notifying the advertiser as soon as possible. Withdrawal should not become a device for avoiding a reward after the advertised task has already been completed in the protected circumstances. The timing of completion and publication therefore needs proof.

    **Work begun and the comparative position**

    Subsection (6) gives another important protection. Where a person notified the advertiser and began the specified work, appropriate remuneration is payable for work done until revocation. This is not necessarily the whole advertised reward. It is a statutory response to work undertaken in the stated circumstances. For example, someone who notified a search organiser and began the requested search may have a different claim from a stranger who acted without knowledge of the offer. The answer must identify the relevant condition rather than assume all efforts produce identical entitlements.

    English law's Carlill case helps explain why an advertisement can contain a real offer accepted through performance. Nepal's section 511, however, is the immediate authority for a Nepal public-reward problem. In conclusion, the Code balances withdrawal before commitment with protection for communicated acceptance and specified performance. The correct method is to construct a timeline, identify whether the offer was private or public, apply sections 508 to 511 and distinguish lapse from post-contract termination. That method produces a reasoned answer instead of treating every changed intention as either automatically effective or automatically irrelevant to the legal result.

## Test yourself

??? question "A says silence for two days will count as acceptance. B says nothing. Contract?"
    That imposed silence does not amount to acceptance under 508(4). Check whether there is any separate conduct expressing assent.

??? question "B accepts only at a lower price. What is the response?"
    A counter-offer under 509(5), not acceptance of the original price.

??? question "Does posting acceptance always bind the offeror in Nepal?"
    No. Apply 508(2), not India's dispatch rule.

??? question "Who receives an advertised reward when two people perform simultaneously?"
    They share under 511(2), subject to its rules for an indivisible reward.

??? question "Can completed reward performance be defeated by later withdrawal?"
    Section 511(5) protects completion before publication of withdrawal, with prompt notification required.

??? question "Does an offer without a deadline last forever?"
    No. Section 510 requires acceptance within a reasonable time.

## One-minute revision

- Section 504 defines proposal and matching assent.
- Section 508 deals with knowledge, receipt, conduct and silence.
- Section 509 covers withdrawal and competing notices.
- Section 510 covers lapse and other ending events.
- Section 511 is the public-reward scheme.
- Section 512 deals with formation place.
