# Unit 17: Arbitration

!!! abstract "In one line"
    Arbitration is agreed private adjudication: the arbitrator decides, while courts assist, supervise within limited grounds and enforce the award.

## 17.1 Meaning and importance

An arbitrator decides; a mediator helps parties reach their own settlement. Arbitration can offer selected expertise and a tailored procedure, but speed, low cost and secrecy are not guaranteed merely by its name. The Arbitration Act 2055 is the main Nepal statute. It bears the year 2055 in its title but is Act No. 1 of 2056. Section 44 repealed the earlier Arbitration Act 2038.

## 17.2 Essentials

Sections 2(क) and 3 require the relevant written arbitration agreement about a defined legal relationship, contractual or otherwise, and an arbitrable dispute. The definition includes an arbitration clause, a separate agreement and the stated recorded communications or pleading situation. The tribunal must have jurisdiction, be properly constituted and give each side fair opportunity. Parties cannot make a non-arbitrable matter arbitrable simply by writing a clause. Section 4 addresses a court proceeding brought despite the relevant agreement.

## 17.3 Arbitrators: number, qualifications, appointment and removal

Sections 5–8 concern number, appointment and vacancy. The default is three; an even chosen number requires the additional arbitrator to make the panel odd. Follow the agreed appointment procedure; the statutory fallback includes each side's nominee and the presiding arbitrator. Section 7 allows the High Court to assist when the method fails. Appointment is not the same as deciding the dispute.

Section 9 requires impartiality, honesty and disclosure of circumstances creating reasonable doubt about independence. Section 10 disqualifies persons lacking contractual capacity, convicted of the stated moral-turpitude offence, insolvent, interested in the subject, or lacking an agreed special qualification. The Act does not impose a universal law-degree requirement. Other legal restrictions still matter: Constitution Article 135 restricts post-retirement work of Supreme Court judges, and Mediation Act 2068 s 19 prevents the mediator hearing the same dispute as arbitrator.

Section 11 follows the agreement's challenge process first; its fallback addresses bias, misconduct/fraud, repeated irregularity, unjustified delaying absence, natural-justice breach and lack/loss of qualification. Under that fallback, raise the challenge within 15 days of learning of appointment or conduct; the arbitrator addresses it within the stated 30-day period, with High Court review as provided. A party-appointed arbitrator is not an advocate free to favour the appointing party.

## 17.4 Award: validity, setting aside and execution

Sections 26–28 concern decision, signatures, contents and communication. The award must address the referred dispute, give findings and reasons and specify relief, relevant interest, place and date. Section 29 has limited correction, interpretation and additional-award routes; it does not authorise an unrestricted second merits decision.

Section 30 permits a High Court set-aside petition within **35 days** of hearing the award or receiving notice. Grounds include incapacity/invalid agreement, notice failure, excess of reference and defective tribunal composition/procedure; non-arbitrability and public policy are also grounds. Section 30(4) bars re-examination of the evidence on which the arbitrator relied. This is not an ordinary appeal because the party dislikes the result.

For the amended position recorded in the finished Procedural Law paper, s 31 provides **45 days** for voluntary compliance after receiving the copy. Section 32 provides the subsequent **30-day** enforcement-application window in the District Court. Filing a s 30 petition does **not automatically stay enforcement** under amended s 32(2); s 32(3) provides a separate High Court stay route for the stated prima facie corruption/fraud or irreparable-loss circumstances. Do not merge this application with setting aside.

## 17.5 Main provisions of the Arbitration Act

| Sections | Subject | Beginner's distinction |
|---|---|---|
| 2–4 | Agreement, reference and court action | Consent and arbitrability come first |
| 5–11 | Tribunal constitution and challenge | Appointment does not waive impartiality |
| 12–14 | Place, language, pleadings; s 13क fast track | Agreed procedure still subject to fairness |
| 17–23 | Hearing, applicable law, confidentiality, powers and evidence | Both sides must have an adequate opportunity |
| 24–29 | Time, scope, award and limited corrections | Award is a decision, not a settlement suggestion |
| 30–33 | Review, compliance, enforcement and interest | Challenge and stay are distinct |
| 34 | Foreign award route | Do not use the domestic enforcement route without checking |
| 39–40, 44 | Limited court role, consent award and repeal | Limited intervention does not mean no court role |

Section 22 requires equal and adequate opportunity. Section 19 concerns confidentiality subject to the parties' wishes. Section 23 permits court assistance with evidence. Section 34 governs foreign-award recognition and enforcement with its conditions; specific treaty route, documents and deadline must be checked for the award. Section 33's interest rule was amended: do not repeat an old interest formula without checking its exclusions.

## Full 20-mark answers

These are full practice answers. Adapt the emphasis to the question; the paragraph headings help you organise a 35-minute answer.

??? example "Q1. Explain arbitration and the appointment, qualifications and removal of arbitrators. (20 marks)"
    !!! warning "Unverified"

    **Introduction**

    Arbitration is a method of deciding disputes through an agreed private adjudicator. Unlike a mediator, an arbitrator hears the parties and makes a determination rather than merely helping them negotiate. The determination is an award that can receive statutory enforcement. Nepal's Arbitration Act 2055 supplies the main framework. Its title year should not be confused with its enactment designation as Act No. 1 of 2056. The Act replaced the earlier Arbitration Act 2038. A full answer begins with agreement and arbitrability, then explains how a lawful and impartial tribunal is constituted.

    **Agreement and scope**

    Sections 2(क) and 3 concern the relevant written agreement to submit disputes about a defined legal relationship to arbitration. The agreement can take the form of a clause in the main contract or a separate instrument, and the definition includes the stated recorded communications and pleading situation. The underlying relationship need not always be contractual. However, the particular dispute must be legally capable of arbitration. Parties cannot manufacture jurisdiction over a non-arbitrable matter merely by agreeing that an arbitrator should decide it. Consent and legal scope are therefore separate requirements.

    The agreement can define the disputes, procedure and qualifications required. This makes arbitration flexible, but not free from legal limits. Equal opportunity, independence and the statutory grounds of supervision continue to matter. A construction dispute may benefit from an engineer's expertise, while a legal interpretation dispute may benefit from legal knowledge. The parties should select competence suited to the task. The fact that an institution administers the proceedings does not remove the need for a valid agreement or a properly appointed tribunal. Ad hoc and institutional forms differ in administration, not in permission to ignore the law.

    **Number and appointment**

    Sections 5 to 8 address the number of arbitrators, appointment and vacancies. The default is three, and an even chosen number requires the additional appointment making the panel odd. The agreed method operates first; the fallback structure includes party nominees and the presiding arbitrator. This promotes a workable decision-making body. However, a party's power to nominate does not turn the nominee into that party's legal advocate. Every arbitrator must undertake the adjudicative role honestly and impartially, including where one party selected the person because of technical expertise or confidence in professional judgment.

    Section 7 provides High Court assistance when the agreed appointment method fails or the statutory conditions for court appointment arise. The court's appointment role helps prevent one party from defeating arbitration through refusal to cooperate. It is not a decision on the merits of the contract dispute. Similarly, replacing a vacant position under section 8 is a tribunal-constitution question. A student should distinguish the legal source of appointment, the point at which the tribunal can act and the separate question whether the claim ultimately succeeds. Conflating appointment with liability produces unnecessary confusion.

    **Independence and qualifications**

    Section 9 requires the oath of impartiality and honesty and disclosure of circumstances creating reasonable doubt about independence or impartiality. Disclosure allows the parties to consider conflicts before the hearing is compromised. A person should not conceal a relevant relationship and assume that being technically skilled cures every concern. The legal standard protects confidence in the process as well as the quality of the final reasoning. It is important to explain this duty as continuing adjudicative responsibility, rather than a ceremonial signature having no connection to later conduct.

    Section 10 identifies disqualifications. They include inability to contract, the specified conviction involving moral turpitude, insolvency, an interest in the dispute's subject and absence of a special qualification required by the agreement. The Act does not create a universal requirement that every arbitrator hold a law degree. Nevertheless, other law may restrict particular persons or roles. Constitution Article 135 concerns work by retired Supreme Court judges, and Mediation Act section 19 prevents the mediator from arbitrating the same dispute. Selection under the agreement must therefore remain consistent with these additional legal limits.

    **Removal and procedural safeguards**

    Section 11 first respects the challenge procedure in the agreement. Its statutory fallback addresses clear bias, misconduct or fraud, repeated irregularity, unjustified delaying absence, natural-justice breach and lack or loss of qualification. The challenge must be raised within the applicable fifteen-day period after learning of the appointment or conduct, and the arbitrator deals with it within the stated thirty-day period. The Act also provides High Court review. A party should not simply abandon the process on the assumption that disagreement with a procedural decision automatically removes the arbitrator.

    Natural justice requires an adequate and equal opportunity to present the case. Section 22 states the participation safeguard, while related provisions concern notice, documents, evidence and assistance. An arbitrator cannot fairly decide by receiving one side's undisclosed material and refusing the other side a chance to answer. Equally, a party's deliberate absence after proper notice does not necessarily prevent every decision. The procedural framework distinguishes genuine denial of opportunity from a party choosing not to use an available opportunity. The answer should explain the principle before alleging that any adverse result demonstrates bias.

    **Importance and limits**

    Arbitration can provide expertise, procedural choice and a binding result, but speed and low cost depend on actual administration. Appointment disputes, complex evidence and court challenges can add delay. The court has an assisting and limited supervisory role, rather than unlimited authority to retry the merits. The finished Procedural Law unit records amendments through 2082, but the fresh Commission PDF could not be downloaded for this unit; amendment-sensitive details and later currency remain specifically warned. This source limitation should be acknowledged rather than hidden behind confident statements of current law.

    In conclusion, a sound arbitration begins with a valid recorded agreement, an arbitrable dispute and a properly constituted independent tribunal. Appointment rules make the process workable; qualifications and disclosure protect trust; removal rules address proven defects through the designated process. A strong examination answer follows this sequence and keeps tribunal formation separate from award review and enforcement. The decisive point is that private selection does not mean private favour: the arbitrator's authority comes from agreement and law and must be exercised through impartial adjudication.

??? example "Q2. Explain a valid award, setting aside and enforcement in Nepal. (20 marks)"

        The Arbitration Act rules are cross-checked with the finished Procedural Law unit, which records a Law Commission text through 2082.     **Introduction**

    An arbitral award is the tribunal's decision on the dispute referred to it. Its importance lies in combining a private adjudicative process with legal enforceability. The award is not merely a recommendation that the losing party may disregard. At the same time, enforceability depends on a lawful tribunal, proper scope and fair procedure. Nepal's Arbitration Act 2055 separates making and communicating the award, limited correction, setting aside and enforcement. A complete answer should preserve that sequence because each stage asks a different legal question and uses a different procedural route.

    **Scope and decision**

    The arbitrator must decide the dispute actually referred, not an unrelated controversy. Section 25 addresses a non-arbitrable matter, while sections 26 to 28 concern decision-making, signatures, contents and communication. The award should explain findings and reasons, identify the relief and include the relevant place and date. Where jurisdiction was challenged, the statutory content requirements also matter. These elements allow the parties and supervising court to understand what was decided and on what basis. A document giving an unexplained number without the required reasoning cannot simply be assumed sufficient because the arbitrator signed it.

    Section 26 deals with the panel's decision and signatures, including the stated position on disagreement and a missing signature. The process should produce an identifiable award rather than several competing informal opinions. Section 28 concerns announcement and delivery of copies. Communication is important because later statutory periods depend on hearing or receiving notice or a copy as specified. A student should not select one arbitrary calendar date for every deadline. The event triggering the particular remedy must be identified from the provision, and proof of communication can become important in a practical dispute.

    **Limited correction rather than a second trial**

    Section 29 permits specified correction, interpretation and additional-award routes. Clerical or arithmetical mistakes are different from changing the merits because the arbitrator has reconsidered the evidence. An undecided referred claim also raises a different issue from a wholly new claim. The statutory conditions and time limits must be met. The general point is that the tribunal does not possess an unrestricted power to decide the same matter again whenever a party is dissatisfied. A request should therefore identify the precise defect and the relevant limited power rather than ask for an informal complete rehearing.

    **Setting aside in the High Court**

    Section 30 permits an application to the High Court within thirty-five days of hearing the award or receiving notice of it. The applicant must identify a statutory ground, not simply restate arguments lost before the tribunal. The grounds include incapacity or invalidity of the agreement, failure of proper notice, an award beyond the reference and composition or procedure inconsistent with the governing agreement or law. The statute also recognises non-arbitrability and public policy. Each ground focuses on the legitimacy of the process, authority or result within the statutory framework.

    Section 30(4) bars re-examination of the evidence relied on by the arbitrator. Setting aside is therefore not an ordinary merits appeal. If a party merely believes a witness should have been believed differently, the court's limited role cannot be ignored. Conversely, serious absence of notice or decision outside the reference is not cured merely because arbitration is described as final. Finality and lawful supervision operate together. The student should state both: arbitration avoids a general retrial, but the Act retains defined safeguards against invalid agreements, unfair process and excess of jurisdiction.

    **Compliance and domestic enforcement**

    The amended position recorded in the finished Procedural Law paper provides forty-five days for compliance after receiving the award copy under section 31. If compliance does not occur, section 32 supplies the subsequent thirty-day application window in the District Court, which enforces through the statutory route as a court judgment. Voluntary compliance and compulsory execution are different stages. The creditor should not assume that an award alone authorises any private seizure of the debtor's property. The statutory enforcement mechanism identifies the responsible court and the process for converting the award into effective recovery.

    Amended section 32(2), as recorded in that source, means a section 30 petition does not automatically stop enforcement. Section 32(3) gives a separate High Court stay route involving the stated prima facie corruption or fraud circumstances or irreparable loss. Filing a challenge and obtaining a stay are therefore distinct acts. This distinction is particularly important when studying an older prescribed decision. A historical judgment may explain the law and procedural position then applicable without settling the effect of a later amendment. Students should state the period and source of the proposition they use.

    **The prescribed case and source caution**

    The saved DN 7836 judgment concerns historical enforcement and finality. Its public record identifies Anil Kumar Pokharel, while the syllabus transcription uses Anil Krishna and also differs on issue metadata. Those discrepancies must be stated rather than quietly corrected through invention. More importantly, its old enforcement reasoning should not be repeated as a verified current rule automatically staying all execution. The Commission landing page was available for this unit, but the fresh consolidated PDF download failed. The later-amendment position is therefore cross-referenced to the finished ADR paper and expressly marked for independent fresh verification.

    **Foreign awards and conclusion**

    Section 34 provides a distinct recognition and enforcement route for foreign awards, with its own documentation and conditions. It should not be replaced by the domestic section 32 process merely because the creditor is in Nepal. Likewise, section 33's interest treatment has been amended, so an old general interest formula requires care. The applicable source version and later changes must be established before giving definitive practical advice about a particular award. An examination answer can explain the framework clearly while acknowledging a source gap in a disputed current detail.

    In conclusion, an award requires lawful authority, proper procedure, identifiable findings and communication. Correction, setting aside, stay and enforcement serve different functions and should be analysed separately. The High Court's limited review does not invite a complete retrial, and an enforcement application belongs to the statutory court process. A strong answer also distinguishes the historical prescribed case from amendment-sensitive current rules. This keeps the discussion accurate, gives the reader a usable procedural sequence and avoids treating either finality or a pending challenge as an unlimited answer to every enforcement problem.
