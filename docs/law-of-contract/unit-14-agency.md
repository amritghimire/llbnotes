# Unit 14: Agency

!!! abstract "In one line"
    An agent can create legal relations for a principal, but the scope of authority decides who is bound and who must answer for an excess.

## 14.1 Meaning

Section 591 permits appointment to act, transact, manage or create legal relations for another, except work requiring personal skill. The principal is represented; the agent represents; the third party deals with that representation. A shop owner authorising a manager to purchase stock illustrates agency. An employee may also be an agent for particular transactions, but employment alone does not establish unlimited authority.

## 14.2 Features

Agency is a three-person legal setting built on authority and representation. An authorised transaction is attributed to the principal under s 592. Authority may be narrow: permission to buy ten sacks does not necessarily permit a loan or a purchase of one hundred sacks. A legally required personal appearance cannot simply be delegated under s 592(1).

## 14.3 Creation

Express appointment uses spoken or written instructions, subject to applicable formalities. Implied appointment can be inferred from conduct and the actual arrangement. Section 592(2) recognises acceptance of an excess; s 596 protects the specified outsider's reasonable reliance on the principal's representation. Do not collapse these into one rule: actual authority concerns what was authorised, ratification concerns later acceptance, and apparent authority concerns what the principal held out to the third party.

India separately codifies express/implied authority (ss 186–187), emergency authority (s 189) and ratification (ss 196–200). These are useful textbook categories. Nepal ss 591–596 must support a Nepal conclusion; do not assume that every foreign doctrine of agency by necessity applies in full without checking its basis.

## 14.4 Types of agent

A special agent handles a particular task; a general agent handles an identified business sphere; a broad or universal agency claims wider powers but remains subject to law and the actual instrument. Brokers arrange deals; commission agents transact for remuneration; a selling agent sells within authority; a commercial manager may make business contracts. These descriptions do not independently prove the power to borrow, give guarantees or dispose of property. Determine the actual authority for the disputed act.

## 14.5 Delegation: meaning and exceptions

The textbook maxim *delegatus non potest delegare* expresses the starting concern: a principal chose this representative. Nepal s 593 permits a sub-agent where the transaction's nature, trade custom or contract permits, but requires the principal's approval unless the contract otherwise provides. Personally entrusted work cannot simply be transferred. Notify the principal. Do not recite the textbook exceptions and omit Nepal's approval requirement.

## 14.6 Agent's rights, duties and personal liability

Section 594 requires instructions to be followed. Without agreed instructions, the agent must act according to the nature of the work, local law and custom, in good faith with diligence and skill. The statutory qualification matters where the principal knew of the agent's lack of skill at the time of contracting. Accounts must be given when demanded; difficulties must be reported promptly for instructions; work must stop after removal. Loss from the stated breaches attracts responsibility.

Remuneration and reimbursement must be established from the lawful agreement and applicable general rules; ss 591–601 do not supply a verbatim Nepal version of India's general agent lien and indemnity list. Explain the agent's contractual payment claim without inventing a free-standing lien over all the principal's property.

Under s 595, unless otherwise arranged, personal liability arises in the listed cases: agreement to be personally liable, undisclosed principal, principal not suable, contracting in one's own name, excess authority, fraud/deception, the transaction's nature, or the agent's own interest. Subsection (2) preserves the stated claim against the principal for an amount not recovered from the personally liable agent. It does not authorise double recovery of the same debt. Subsection (3) has its own limitation-start rule connected to final recovery from the agent; distinguish it from s 601's general two-year period.

## 14.7 Sub-agent and substituted agent

| Question | Textbook sub-agent | Textbook substituted agent |
|---|---|---|
| Selection | Agent appoints someone to assist in the agency | Agent authorised to name a person selects one for the principal |
| Relationship | Often works under original agent | Direct representative of principal for the selected work |
| Indian source | Contract Act ss 191–193 | Contract Act ss 194–195 |
| Nepal caution | Apply approval, notification and liability under s 593 | Do not presume the foreign label creates a separate Nepal exception |

An approved Nepal sub-agent has the rights and duties contemplated by s 593 as if appointed by the principal. Without the required approval, the principal is not liable for that appointment and the appointing agent is personally responsible as provided. Labels alone do not answer the question.

## 14.8 Principal's rights and duties

The principal may demand proper performance, accounts and communication under s 594, and recover proven loss for the specified breaches. The principal must honour authorised transactions under s 592 and the agreement's lawful remuneration and expense obligations. Section 596 may bind a principal who represented that the agent had authority where the third party reasonably relied; it excludes a third party who knew or should have known the limit. Under s 592(3), a separable authorised portion of an excessive transaction can bind the principal. Analyse separability rather than declaring the whole transaction automatically valid or invalid.

## 14.9 Termination

Section 597 lists notice by the agent, revocation/impossibility, completion, expiry, death or unsoundness of either person, principal's insolvency, destruction of the subject and dissolution of the entity. Revocation of unused authority requires immediate notification; completed authorised acts are not undone. The property-interest protection in s 597(3) prevents removal prejudicing the agent's protected share.

Section 598 restricts premature removal in fixed-period/task arrangements unless otherwise agreed, and requires proper cause and prior notice in the indefinite setting, with reasonable compensation for breach. Section 599 places corresponding obligations on the agent withdrawing. Section 600 ends sub-agency with the main agency. Keep power to revoke, contractual liability for doing so and protection of an existing property interest separate.

## 14.10 Nepal's agency law

The core study source is Civil Code Part 5, chapter 10, ss 591–601. General capacity, consent, legality and breach rules still apply. Sector-specific registration, professional permission or formal powers to represent someone in court may require additional law; appointment under s 591 is not a licence to ignore those requirements.

## Full 20-mark answers

These are full practice answers. Adapt the emphasis to the question; the paragraph headings help you organise a 35-minute answer.

??? example "Q1. Explain agency, its creation and the extent of authority. (20 marks)"
    !!! warning "Unverified"

    **Introduction**

    Agency allows one person to participate in legal dealings through another. The person represented is the principal, the representative is the agent, and the person dealing with the representative is the third party. If a shop owner authorises a manager to buy stock, the manager's authorised purchase can create an obligation for the owner. This makes business possible without requiring the owner to perform every transaction personally. It also creates a risk: an agent may act beyond the permission actually given. Nepal's Civil Code sections 591 to 601 regulate that relationship and its consequences.

    **The basis of appointment**

    Section 591 permits appointment to act, transact, manage or establish legal relations for another, subject to the exclusion concerning personally skilled work. The principal must identify what representation is being entrusted. A general description such as manager or broker can help explain the relationship, but it does not prove every disputed power. Authority to negotiate a purchase need not include authority to borrow money or pledge the principal's assets. The first step in an agency problem is therefore to identify the transaction and the actual appointment rather than rely only on the person's job title.

    Section 592 also preserves legally personal acts. Where the law requires the person's own appearance or action, ordinary appointment cannot simply remove that requirement. This shows the limit of freedom to use representatives. Agency facilitates lawful dealings, but does not override mandatory rules concerning personal participation, professional qualifications or the special form required for particular transactions. A student should distinguish the general possibility of agency from any additional legal requirement attached to the specific act. The two questions operate together rather than one automatically replacing the other.

    **Express and implied creation**

    Express appointment uses direct words, whether spoken or written, subject to applicable formalities. The owner may instruct a manager to buy a specified quantity at a stated price. Implied appointment is inferred from conduct and the actual business arrangement. A person regularly allowed to order ordinary stock may have authority within that established activity. The inference must be supported by facts. An isolated personal friendship or mere presence in a shop is not enough to demonstrate that the owner authorised binding transactions with suppliers.

    These categories explain how authority may be shown, but the conclusion in Nepal must remain grounded in sections 591 and 592 and the relevant agreement. India's Contract Act expressly addresses express and implied authority in sections 186 and 187. Those provisions are useful comparisons, not substitutes for Nepal's Code. Similarly, broad textbook categories such as agency by necessity require care. A difficult situation does not automatically create unlimited authority. The answer must establish the relevant Nepal basis and the scope of any emergency action rather than simply attach a foreign label.

    **Later acceptance and ratification**

    Section 592 addresses acts beyond authority and acceptance by the principal. Later approval is conceptually different from permission existing before the transaction. Suppose the manager orders more goods than permitted and the owner subsequently accepts the excessive transaction. The legal effect depends on the statutory rule and the acceptance actually established. The student should explain what the principal knew and accepted, rather than assume that every silent response is approval. The foreign doctrine of ratification can aid explanation, but India's detailed sections 196 to 200 should not be presented as Nepal's numbered provisions.

    **Apparent authority and the outsider**

    Section 596 addresses another distinct situation. A principal may represent that an agent has authority and thereby lead a third party reasonably to believe the transaction is authorised. If the statutory requirements are met, the principal may be bound despite a private limit on actual authority. The representation must be connected to the principal. An agent's unsupported assertion that all powers exist cannot necessarily manufacture the principal's consent. The provision also excludes the third party who knew or should have known the limitation. Outsider protection depends on reasonable reliance, not merely convenience.

    For example, an owner who presents a manager as the person authorised to place ordinary stock orders may create a different position from an owner who clearly informs the supplier that approval is needed for orders above a stated value. In the second situation, the supplier's knowledge matters. In both situations the answer should identify the principal's conduct, the third party's knowledge, the scope of the proposed deal and the reasonableness of reliance. This factual structure is more reliable than saying that principals are always responsible for everything their employees do.

    **Extent, excess and separability**

    Section 592 attributes authorised acts to the principal. Where the agent exceeds authority, the excess does not automatically bind without the relevant acceptance or another applicable basis. Subsection (3) requires attention to a separable authorised portion. If a lawful authorised transaction can be separated from an unauthorised addition, the Code may bind the principal to that part. This is different from assuming either that the whole deal is valid or that the whole deal disappears. The terms and practical divisibility of the transaction determine the appropriate analysis.

    The agent may also face personal responsibility under section 595. Own-name contracting, excess authority, an undisclosed principal and the other listed situations require separate consideration. Thus a finding that the principal is not bound does not necessarily mean the third party has no remedy against anyone. Conversely, personal liability of the agent does not establish unlimited double recovery. The Code's stated principal claim for an unrecovered amount must be read alongside the facts and the sums already received. Each person's obligation should be identified rather than merged into a vague common liability.

    **Why distinctions matter and conclusion**

    Actual authority, later acceptance and apparent authority answer different questions: what was permitted, what was subsequently accepted and what the principal reasonably represented to the outsider. Keeping them separate protects legitimate business reliance while respecting the principal's lawful limits. The agent's duties of instructions, care and accounting under section 594 reinforce this structure. In conclusion, agency is a practical method of representation governed by authority, attribution and accountability. A complete Nepal answer identifies the three persons, the source and scope of authority, the disputed excess, the outsider's knowledge and the applicable Code provision before deciding who is bound.

??? example "Q2. Discuss the rights, duties and personal liability of an agent and principal. (20 marks)"
    !!! warning "Unverified"

    **Introduction**

    Agency gives one person power to affect another person's legal position. That power requires accountability. The principal must be able to rely on authorised work, accurate accounts and timely communication, while the agent must be able to rely on the lawful payment and expense arrangement. Third parties also need to know whether they are dealing with the principal or with an agent personally. Sections 592 to 596 of Nepal's Civil Code address these questions, with section 594 governing conduct and section 595 identifying important cases of personal liability. Their rules should be explained together but not confused.

    **Instructions and proper performance**

    Section 594 requires the agent to follow the principal's instructions. The instruction defines the entrusted task and the lawful limits of performance. If a principal authorises purchase of a particular machine within an agreed budget, the agent should not substitute a materially different transaction for personal convenience. Instructions must themselves be read with the law; agency does not authorise unlawful conduct. In a problem question, the answer should set out the actual instruction and the agent's actual act, then identify the departure. This is stronger than making a general accusation that the agent acted badly.

    Where the agreement or instructions do not settle the matter, the section looks to the nature of the work, local law and custom, good faith, diligence and skill. Good faith concerns honest performance for the entrusted purpose; diligence concerns the care and effort reasonably required; skill concerns the competence expected in that work. The Code includes a qualification where the principal knew of the agent's lack of skill when contracting. That qualification should not be omitted or turned into a general permission for deception, disobedience or careless conduct unrelated to the known limitation.

    **Communication, accounts and stopping after removal**

    An agent often has information that the principal cannot directly observe. Section 594 therefore requires accounts when demanded and prompt reporting of difficulties so that instructions can be obtained. If a supplier changes the essential terms, the agent should not conceal the change and later claim that any decision was acceptable. Accounts allow the principal to trace payments, receipts and the handling of property. The duty to stop acting after removal also matters: a former agent cannot treat an ended appointment as continuing permission merely because the person once represented the principal.

    The section attaches responsibility to loss from the specified breaches. Liability should therefore be related to the actual breach and loss, rather than treated as punishment for an unpopular outcome. A transaction may lose money despite proper authorised conduct. On the other hand, an agent who disobeys a clear limit and causes avoidable loss cannot escape simply by describing the result as a business risk. The answer should distinguish proof of authority, proof of breach, causation and the amount claimed. These are connected but separate steps in the legal reasoning.

    **Agent's contractual rights**

    The agent's remuneration and reimbursement depend on the lawful agreement and the applicable general rules. The principal cannot demand performance of an agreed paid task and simply disregard the agreed payment obligation. The terms should establish the amount, trigger and expenses covered. However, sections 591 to 601 do not contain an exact reproduction of India's detailed list of agent lien, indemnity and remuneration provisions. A Nepal answer should not invent a general right to hold every item of the principal's property until all unrelated debts are paid.

    The distinction between a payment claim and a right over property is important. A person may be entitled to remuneration without having a statutory power to retain or sell goods. Likewise, the existence of an expense does not automatically prove that the principal agreed to bear it. Identify the instruction, scope, payment clause and legal basis. India's Contract Act sections 217 to 225 provide useful comparative material, but the student must clearly label them as Indian law. Comparative study should help clarify the issue rather than supply an unsupported Nepal remedy.

    **Personal liability under section 595**

    Section 595 lists circumstances in which the agent is personally liable unless the arrangement provides otherwise. They include an agreement to undertake personal liability, an undisclosed principal, a principal who cannot be sued, contracting in the agent's own name, exceeding authority, deception or fraud, the nature of the transaction and the agent's own interest. These categories should be connected to examples. An agent who contracts in a personal name without disclosing the representative setting creates a different issue from one who clearly enters an authorised transaction solely for an identified principal.

    Excess authority also requires attention to section 592. A separable authorised part may bind the principal, while the excess raises the agent's responsibility and possible later acceptance. Fraud is not excused merely because the person calls himself an agent. At the same time, personal liability should not be asserted mechanically whenever a transaction fails. The relevant statutory category, agreement and facts must be shown. A student should distinguish a contractual promise to pay personally from a claim arising because the agent exceeded authority or misrepresented the transaction.

    **The principal's position and third-party recovery**

    The principal has the right to proper instructions-based performance, communication and accounts, and to recovery of established loss under section 594. The principal must honour authorised acts under section 592. Section 596 may also bind a principal who represented authority where the third party reasonably relied, subject to the exception for knowledge or constructive knowledge of the limit. A private restriction and an external representation can therefore produce different questions. The principal's own conduct must be examined, not only the agent's description of the powers claimed.

    Section 595(2) preserves the stated claim against the principal for an amount not recovered from the personally liable agent. This should not be described as permission to collect the same debt twice. Amounts already recovered must be accounted for. Subsection (3) contains a specific limitation-start rule linked to final recovery from the agent; section 601 gives the general two-year cause-of-action period for the chapter. In conclusion, agency law balances delegated power with duties, payment and outsider protection. A complete answer identifies the particular obligation of each person and supports every claimed remedy with the relevant Nepal provision.

??? example "Q3. Explain delegation, sub-agency and termination of agency. (20 marks)"
    !!! warning "Unverified"

    **Introduction**

    A principal chooses an agent because the principal expects a particular person to perform entrusted work. The agent may need help, but transferring the work can change the risk, competence and accountability that the principal accepted. Agency must also have an end: the task may be completed, the period may expire or a party may seek to withdraw. Nepal's Civil Code addresses permitted sub-agency in section 593 and termination in sections 597 to 600. These rules protect both the principal's choice and legitimate interests that arise while the agency continues.

    **The starting principle concerning delegation**

    The textbook maxim delegatus non potest delegare means that a delegate generally cannot hand the entrusted authority to another. Its purpose is to preserve the personal confidence underlying the appointment. If an owner selects a person for particular judgment or skill, that person cannot assume permission to replace the selected performer with someone else. However, the maxim is not an absolute description of every business arrangement. Some transactions, established customs or express contracts contemplate assistance. The question is which delegation the law and the principal's arrangement actually permit.

    Section 593 is the Nepal starting point. It permits a sub-agent where the nature of the transaction, trade custom or contract allows, but requires the principal's approval unless the contract provides otherwise. The approval element is important. A student should not list foreign textbook exceptions and conclude that no further Nepal condition exists. Personally entrusted work cannot simply be passed on. The original agent must notify the principal as required. These rules make it possible to distinguish authorised business assistance from an unauthorised substitution of the person entrusted with responsibility.

    **Consequences of authorised and unauthorised appointment**

    Where the required approval exists, section 593 gives the sub-agent the contemplated rights and duties as if appointed by the principal. This creates a legal route through which the principal can be represented by the approved person. Without the required approval, the principal is not made liable merely by the original agent's unilateral choice, and the appointing agent bears personal responsibility as provided. The answer should therefore identify the permission to delegate, approval or contractual exception, notification and the actual acts performed. Each issue contributes to the eventual allocation of responsibility.

    Suppose an agent authorised to arrange transport appoints another person. The appointment's validity cannot be decided from the word transport alone. The nature of the original task, the trade custom, the contract and the principal's approval must be considered. If the original appointment depended on the agent's personal skill, that fact points against free substitution. If the contract expressly provides the relevant delegation arrangement, its lawful terms matter. A reasoned answer explains these facts before deciding whether the principal or appointing agent answers for the subsequent representative's conduct.

    **Sub-agent and substituted agent in textbooks**

    Indian law distinguishes a sub-agent, appointed to assist within the agency, from a person whom the agent is authorised to select to act directly for the principal. Sections 191 to 193 address the former and sections 194 to 195 the latter. Textbooks call the second person a substituted agent. The distinction helps explain who chooses the person and for whom that person acts. However, Nepal's section 593 has its own wording concerning approval and the resulting rights and duties. The foreign label does not independently remove Nepal's statutory conditions.

    **Events ending authority**

    Section 597 identifies several routes to termination: notice or withdrawal, revocation or impossibility, completion of the task, expiry of time, death or unsoundness of either party, insolvency of the principal, destruction of the subject and dissolution of the relevant entity. The answer should organise these as acts of parties and events affecting the relationship. Completion is easy to illustrate: authority to buy one specified item does not necessarily continue after the authorised purchase is complete. A fixed period similarly requires attention to whether the appointment has expired or has been lawfully extended.

    Revocation of unused authority requires immediate notification under section 597. It does not retrospectively undo authorised acts already completed. If a principal lawfully committed to a purchase through the agent yesterday, removing the agent today does not by itself cancel the purchaser's existing obligation. This distinction protects transaction security. It also prevents a principal from using termination as a device to avoid an unfavourable but properly authorised deal. The existence and timing of each act must therefore be established before a conclusion about termination's effect is reached.

    **Property interest and compensation**

    Section 597(3) protects an agency connected with the agent's own interest in the relevant property against removal prejudicing that share. This is more precise than saying every agency called irrevocable can never end. The agent must have the protected interest contemplated by the section. A mere expectation of future fees is not automatically identical to an existing share in property. The answer should identify the property, the interest and the prejudice caused by the proposed removal, rather than rely on the wording of a label alone.

    Section 598 addresses the principal's premature removal of an agent in a fixed-period or fixed-task arrangement, subject to the contract, and the cause and notice required in the indefinite setting. Breach can require reasonable compensation. The power to end authority and the freedom to do so without contractual consequences are not the same question. A principal may seek to stop further representation, yet still owe compensation for violating the promised arrangement. The student should explain both issues instead of treating termination as either wholly impossible or entirely consequence-free.

    **The agent's withdrawal and conclusion**

    Section 599 places corresponding obligations on an agent leaving the relationship. A fixed task or period cannot simply be abandoned without the applicable proper and adequate reason; the indefinite setting requires the relevant cause and prior notice. Breach may expose the agent to compensation. Section 600 links sub-agency to the principal agency's end. Section 601 generally supplies two years from accrual for chapter claims. In conclusion, delegation and termination regulate who may act and when that authority stops. A strong Nepal answer states approval, notification, protected interests and compensation separately, while ensuring that completed authorised dealings are not incorrectly erased.

## Test yourself

??? question "Who supplies the representation for apparent authority?"
    The principal's representation under s 596, coupled with the required reasonable belief; the agent's unsupported assertion alone is insufficient.

??? question "Does the textbook delegation exception remove Nepal's approval condition?"
    No. Apply s 593 and any contractual exception to its approval requirement.

??? question "Does personal agent liability permit double recovery?"
    No. Account for sums recovered; s 595(2) concerns the stated unrecovered amount.

??? question "Can termination undo yesterday's completed authorised purchase?"
    Not merely by ending future authority; s 597 preserves completed authorised acts.

??? question "Is every expectation of commission an agency coupled with property interest?"
    No. Establish the protected property interest under s 597(3).
