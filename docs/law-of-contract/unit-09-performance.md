# Unit 9: Performance of contract

!!! abstract "In one line"
    Performance means doing what the contract requires, by the proper person, at the proper time and place. Offering performance and transferring a right raise separate questions.

## 9.1 Meaning and importance

Section 521 requires each party to fulfil their contractual obligations. A carpenter must supply the agreed desks, not simply something of approximately the same value. Performance gives the agreement its practical value and determines whether payment, release or remedies become relevant.

Distinguish actual completion from an offer to perform. A delivery attempt may fail because the customer obstructs access. That fact matters, but it does not justify treating every obligation as automatically extinguished without examining the Code and the contract.

## 9.2 Types of performance and requisites of valid tender

Actual performance fulfils the duty. Attempted performance, often called tender, offers the performance but does not complete it because it is not accepted or cannot proceed in the circumstances. Performance may be simultaneous or sequential, and personal or through another authorised person.

A practical tender checklist asks whether the offer is unconditional as to the performance due, complete rather than an unauthorised substitute, made by a ready and able person, addressed to the proper recipient, at the required time and place, and accompanied by a reasonable opportunity to check the offered goods. “Unconditional” does not forbid requiring the reciprocal payment which the agreement actually makes due on delivery.

**India:** Contract Act 38 expressly regulates refusal of a valid offer of performance and its stated conditions. Its inspection rule is an Indian statutory rule. **Nepal:** apply 521–524 to what is due and 522 and 532 to cooperation or prevention. Do not cite an imaginary Nepal tender section or assume refusal cancels every debt.

## 9.3 Rules of performance

| Nepal provision | Rule |
|---|---|
| 522 | Simultaneous and ordered reciprocal obligations; consequences of a party preventing the other's performance |
| 523 | Agreed time and manner, inherently required time or manner, otherwise reasonableness |
| 524 | Agreed place; goods' location and nature or usage rules; notification to specify a reasonable place in the remaining case |
| 525 | Late performance, reasonable additional-time notice and possible cancellation following noncompliance |
| 526 | Where timely performance is essential to the purpose, failure permits immediate cancellation under its conditions |
| 528 | Rights pass to the heir succeeding to property; obligations limited to that property, with a personal-skill exception |
| 529 | Other performers, transfer, joint promisors and contribution |
| 530 | Parties and intended beneficiaries may demand performance as provided |
| 532 | Necessary facilities must be provided; the relevant prevented party is not liable for failure caused by their absence |
| 533–534 | Agreed alteration or substitution and the chapter's two-year limitation period |

Example: a venue owner denies agreed access to the repair crew, then complains the roof was not repaired. Establish the promised access, the crew's readiness and the actual causal effect. Section 532 is not an excuse for unrelated defects in the crew's own work.

## 9.4 Assignment

Under 529(1), another person may perform unless personal performance is required. Transferring an obligation requires the other party's consent. Section 529(2) requires writing unless otherwise agreed, unconditional transfer, no prohibition by law or contract, and the stated notice to the other party.

Delegating a task is not automatically releasing the original debtor. Assignment of a payment right differs from substituting a new debtor by agreement under 533. A portrait requiring a named artist's skill differs from a routine delivery which an authorised assistant can perform.

Under 529(3), accepting a third person's performance prevents a later demand for the original party's personal performance, subject to contrary terms. Subsections (4)–(6) address joint promisors, proportionate recovery between them and release of one without automatically releasing the rest. Keep these rules distinct from joint enforcement under 530(2).

## Full 20-mark answers

??? example "Q1. Explain performance and the rules governing it under Nepal's Civil Code."
    !!! warning "Unverified"
        This answer uses the checked Nepal text through Jestha 2076. Complete later-amendment currency remains unverified.

    **Meaning and practical importance**

    Performance means fulfilling the obligation which the contract legally requires. Section 521 of Nepal's Civil Code directs each party to perform their contractual duty. This gives practical effect to the agreement. A school's purchase of desks matters because the desks are delivered as promised, not merely because a document exists. The performance inquiry identifies what must be done, by whom, when, where and in what manner. It also considers cooperation and linked promises. Without those details, an allegation that a contract was not fulfilled may conceal disagreement about the obligation itself rather than proof of a legally established failure.

    Actual performance differs from an attempt to perform. A supplier may arrive with the goods but find that the buyer refuses access. That situation needs legal analysis, but it should not be described as automatic completion of every obligation. The contract and the applicable Code provisions determine the consequence. Similarly, offering a different item does not necessarily perform a promise to deliver the identified item. The law connects performance with the agreed duty, subject to valid alteration or other relevant rules. A general willingness to help is not always equivalent to readiness and ability to supply the performance actually owed.

    **Reciprocal promises and sequence**

    Section 522 deals with reciprocal obligations. Where the parties must perform simultaneously and one materially demonstrates unwillingness to fulfil their side, the other need not fulfil the corresponding promise in the statutory circumstances. A cash-on-delivery transaction illustrates why the duties cannot always be considered separately. The buyer's payment and seller's delivery may be linked. However, the terms must establish that relationship. A party cannot impose a new sequence after contracting simply because it would now be more convenient or provide greater security. The agreed structure and the nature of the transaction remain important to deciding what performance is due first.

    Where an order of performance is fixed, section 522 respects that order. Where none is fixed, the nature of the contract can indicate which promise must be performed first. The section also addresses loss caused when one party's failure prevents the other's performance, and prevention in a simultaneous-performance arrangement. These provisions discourage a person from creating the obstacle and then treating the other side as the defaulter. The facts still need to show the relevant connection. Failure by one party does not justify withholding every unrelated obligation merely by invoking the broad description reciprocal promises without examining the actual agreement.

    **Time and manner**

    Section 523 starts with the time and manner specified by the contract. If the act inherently requires a particular time or method, that feature matters even without an express term. In the remaining situation, performance must occur within a reasonable time and in a reasonable manner. Reasonableness is connected with the transaction rather than the debtor's unrestricted preference. Repairing an urgent leaking roof and making an ordinary long-term supply may involve different practical expectations. A factual answer should explain why the suggested time or manner fits the relevant circumstances instead of treating the word reasonable as a conclusion requiring no support.

    Sections 525 and 526 distinguish ordinary delay from timing essential to the contract's purpose. Section 525 provides a route involving reasonable additional-time notice where later performance remains possible and cancellation after the stated noncompliance. Section 526 permits immediate cancellation where timely performance is essential in its terms. Missing a delivery date for equipment needed only for a particular event can differ from a short delay which leaves the purpose achievable. The point is not that every printed date is automatically essential, but that nature and the parties' intention determine the statutory timing question and the appropriate response to failure.

    **Place and cooperation**

    Section 524 gives priority to the agreed place of performance. It then addresses goods' location, the nature or usage of the act and a remaining situation requiring notice to specify a reasonable place. These rules prevent avoidable uncertainty. A supplier should not assume that any place chosen after the event counts as proper delivery. Equally, a recipient cannot insist on a newly invented location contrary to the agreement. Identifying the relevant place helps distinguish a valid attempt to perform from an incomplete or misdirected act. It also helps establish which party actually caused a failed delivery or missed performance.

    Section 532 requires the parties to provide necessary facilities from their respective sides. If performance fails because those facilities were not provided, the relevant prevented party is not liable for failure caused by that omission. Suppose a venue owner denies agreed access to a ready repair team. The owner cannot simply ignore that obstruction when alleging delay. The defence must nevertheless be connected with the actual prevention. It does not excuse separate defects in work the team could perform or an inability existing before access was refused. The inquiry remains factual, causal and tied to the obligation in question.

    **Who performs and the consequences**

    Section 529 generally allows performance through an agent or another person unless the contract requires the party's own performance. Personal skill matters where, for example, a named artist's work is promised. Routine delivery may permit a different arrangement. Transferring the obligation to another person requires the other party's consent, and the section supplies further conditions for transfer. Section 528 separately addresses succession on death or loss of soundness, limiting the heir's obligation to inherited property and excluding personal-skill duties. These provisions should not be merged into a universal rule that anyone can always replace any contracting party without consequences.

    Finally, section 533 permits agreed alteration, suspension or substitution, while section 534 supplies the chapter's checked two-year limitation period. Performance disputes can lead to remedies, but the remedy depends on an established duty and the nature of its failure. In conclusion, Nepal's framework treats performance as a coordinated legal obligation, not merely an isolated act. A careful analysis reads the agreement, identifies linked promises, applies time and place rules, examines cooperation and determines the proper performer. That sequence supports reliable fulfilment and prevents a party from claiming either completion or default without proving the conditions relevant to the transaction.

??? example "Q2. Explain tender of performance and assignment, distinguishing them from discharge."
    !!! warning "Unverified"
        This answer uses the checked Nepal text through Jestha 2076. Complete later-amendment currency remains unverified.

    **Three different questions**

    Tender, assignment and discharge concern different stages or aspects of contractual obligation. Tender means offering the performance due. Assignment concerns transfer of a contractual entitlement under the governing rules, while transfer or substitution of obligations requires further attention. Discharge concerns release from an obligation which no longer needs performance. Confusing these ideas can lead to serious errors. A refused delivery does not necessarily extinguish every debt, and assigning a payment right does not automatically remove the debtor's obligations or replace a contractor. Nepal's Civil Code provides the performance, transfer and alteration framework through which these questions should be examined separately.

    Consider a supplier who sends goods, then transfers the payment entitlement to a financier. The delivery attempt raises a tender or performance question. The financier's claim raises a transfer question. Whether the supplier is released from duties concerning the goods raises another issue. None is answered merely by the fact that someone else is now involved. The student should identify the actual obligation and each person's legal role. This method avoids treating practical delegation, legal transfer and complete release as interchangeable descriptions of any commercial arrangement in which a third person handles work or money connected with the contract.

    **Tender and readiness to perform**

    A practical tender analysis asks whether the offered performance is what the contract requires, offered by a ready and able person to the proper recipient at the required time and place. It should not impose an unauthorised new condition or substitute a different obligation. A person offering ten agreed desks differs from someone offering five unrelated tables and insisting the debt is satisfied. At the same time, requiring the reciprocal payment actually due on delivery is not necessarily an improper condition. The contract defines the exchange, so a checklist must be applied to the bargain rather than detached from its lawful terms.

    Indian Contract Act section 38 gives an express statutory framework for refusal of an offer of performance and its stated conditions, including a reasonable opportunity to inspect offered goods. That section is comparative material, not a Nepal provision. Nepal's relevant rules include sections 521 to 524 on performance and sections 522 and 532 on prevention or necessary cooperation. An answer should therefore avoid inventing a Nepal section identical to section 38. It should also avoid assuming that the foreign rule settles every consequence of refusing a Nepal payment or delivery attempt, regardless of the type of obligation or its remaining conditions.

    **Refusal, prevention and discharge**

    Where a recipient prevents performance, the precise effect depends on the applicable rule and facts. Section 532 addresses failure caused by lack of required facilities. Section 522 addresses linked promises and prevention in the circumstances it describes. Suppose a buyer refuses the agreed access necessary for delivery when the supplier is ready with conforming goods. That obstruction matters to responsibility for the resulting failure. It does not prove that every unrelated duty disappeared or that the supplier may keep every advance without accounting. A complete answer follows the established statutory consequence instead of converting a failed attempt into an unlimited release.

    Discharge may result from actual fulfilment, agreed release or another recognised ground. Tender alone is a different fact. Particularly with money obligations, one should not announce automatic extinction simply because the creditor declined an attempted payment. The relevant law and transaction must be examined. This distinction protects both sides: a recipient cannot manufacture a default through wrongful obstruction, while a debtor cannot use an inadequate or misdirected offer as a device to escape all responsibility. The legal analysis needs evidence of what was offered, what was required and why completion failed, not merely the word tender in the claim.

    **Transfer under section 529**

    Section 529 allows performance by an agent, appointed person or another person acting on the party's behalf unless the obligation requires personal performance. It also recognises transfer within its conditions. Transfer of obligations requires the other party's consent. Subsection (2) addresses writing unless otherwise agreed, unconditional transfer, absence of a prohibition by law or contract, and the stated notice to the other party. These requirements matter because the identity of the person bearing a duty can affect risk. A customer hiring a named specialist has not necessarily agreed to rely on any replacement chosen unilaterally by that specialist after contracting.

    Assignment of a right to payment is conceptually different from shifting the duty to build or repair. Likewise, delegation of work does not itself establish release of the original debtor. Section 533 provides a separate framework for agreed alteration or substitution of a new contract and its effect on the original obligation. These distinctions explain why consent and careful terms matter. A supplier's arrangement with a financier should not be assumed to change every promise made to the customer. The agreement and relevant statute determine which right moved, which duty remains and whether a genuine substitution was made by the persons concerned.

    **Succession, third performance and conclusion**

    Section 528 concerns another mechanism, succession following death or loss of soundness. Rights pass to the heir succeeding to property, obligations are limited to that property, and personal skill or qualification duties are excepted. This is not simply a voluntary assignment under section 529. Section 529(3) separately addresses acceptance of a third person's performance, preventing the stated later demand for personal performance subject to contrary terms. Each provision addresses its own factual situation. Their differences should be retained rather than collected under a vague proposition that obligations always pass freely from one person to another whenever circumstances change.

    In conclusion, tender offers performance, transfer changes a legal entitlement or duty under conditions, and discharge releases the duty through a recognised ground. A practical answer identifies the obligation, checks conformity and cooperation, distinguishes rights from duties and asks whether any release or substitution was actually agreed or legally supplied. Nepal's sections 521 to 534 provide the foundation. The comparative tender checklist helps organise facts but does not replace those provisions. Keeping the concepts distinct allows justified protection against obstruction while preserving consent, personal commitments and the continuing obligations which an attempted performance or transfer alone has not lawfully removed.

## Test yourself

??? question "Is a refused attempt always complete performance?"
    No. Apply the contract and the relevant prevention or cooperation rules.

??? question "Does section 528 govern voluntary assignment?"
    No. It governs succession; section 529 is the transfer provision.

??? question "Can a party transfer obligations without the other party's consent?"
    Section 529(1) requires that consent.

??? question "Does every missed date permit immediate cancellation?"
    No. Distinguish 525 from the essential-time conditions in 526.

??? question "Can the owner deny required access and ignore that fact when alleging delay?"
    No. Examine 522 and 532 and the actual cause of nonperformance.

??? question "Does personal skill matter?"
    Yes. Personal performance can limit substitution and succession.

## One-minute revision

- Performance must match the obligation.
- Tender and completed performance differ.
- Respect reciprocal order, time, manner and place.
- Prevention requires causal analysis.
- Succession is 528; transfer is 529.
- Transfer of obligations requires consent.
