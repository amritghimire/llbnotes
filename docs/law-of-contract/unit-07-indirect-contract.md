# Unit 7: Indirect contract and unjust enrichment

!!! abstract "In one line"
    Some duties arise because someone lawfully manages another's affairs, supplies a benefit or receives something without justification, even without an ordinary agreement.

!!! warning "Unverified"
    Nepal provisions were checked against the Nepali government Code through Jestha 2076 and Law Commission repository text. Complete later-amendment currency remains unverified. Comparative doctrines do not replace the Code's individual conditions.

## Why it matters for the exam

- No standalone Unit 7 question appears in the available 2078 and 2080 papers. It remains a required unit and supports answers on remedies.
- The syllabus prints “Quantum Merit.” The conventional legal term is **quantum meruit**.
- Keep three ideas separate: a contractual price, payment for completed work when a contract ends, and repayment of an unjustified benefit.

## 7.1 Meaning of indirect contract

Section 648 recognises a definite, lawful, voluntary or unilateral act capable of creating a legal relationship, despite the ordinary formation chapter. The obligation follows from the legally relevant facts. Do not invent a promise which the parties never made.

Example: during a flood, a neighbour spends reasonable money protecting another person's stored grain without first reaching them. Section 652 supplies a specific reimbursement rule. The rescuer need not pretend that the absent owner accepted a price beforehand.

## 7.2 Rules governing indirect contracts

| Sections | Rule and ordinary example |
|---|---|
| 649–650 | A voluntary manager must not simply abandon another's affairs and must care for property in good faith; bad-faith or reckless damage attracts liability |
| 651 | Delegating an entrusted task does not by itself remove the original obligation |
| 652–653 | Reasonable disaster-protection expenses and the stated voluntary-performance benefits can support reimbursement; 653 excludes an unlawful purpose |
| 654–657 | Maintenance, funeral and urgent treatment expenses have individual conditions. Gratuitous assistance is excluded where the provision says so |
| 658 | Specified public health or safety expenditure can be recovered despite the person's refusal to take required measures |
| 659 | Where someone promised to pay a legally payable sum but failed and the legally liable person paid, the promisor must reimburse that person |
| 660–661 | Payment for supplied goods or work, including the stated rule for goods or services supplied to persons incapable of contracting or those they must support |
| 662–663 | Lawfully held property attracts bailment-like care; the chapter's claim period is two years from the cause of action |

Section 653's Nepali text says the actor was **not legally compelled** to undertake the obligation. The English translation's wording can obscure this. Section 661 also must not be rewritten as India's section 68 property-only necessaries rule. Apply Nepal's actual wording and keep it separate from enforcing an incapable person's own contract.

## 7.3 Quantum meruit and its rules

The Latin expression means payment for what is deserved. It is a measure or route of recovery in a particular legal setting, not a promise that every volunteer can submit a bill.

Nepal's section 539 permits the injured party, where breach or another reason ends the contract, to claim the contractual amount proportionate to work or performance completed. Section 538(2) addresses a reasonable amount for services or benefits in the cancellation or invalidity situations it covers. Section 660 supplies a further statutory payment rule. Identify which applies before calculating anything.

Practical rules are to establish the legal basis, prove the work or benefit, check agreed price and proportions where relevant, account for earlier payment and distinguish payment from breach damages. An invented full-contract profit is not automatically reasonable remuneration. English restitutionary quantum meruit, illustrated by *Benedetti v Sawiris*, concerns valuation of services in its own setting. It is not the source of section 539.

## 7.4 Unjust enrichment

Section 664 covers benefit without the lawful basis described there. A benefit can increase property, reduce liability, provide a service or allow use of another's property. A lawful entitlement under law or contract, or freely agreeing not to seek return or reimbursement, affects the analysis under subsection (3). Not every unequal gain is unjust.

Sections 665–666 require return of mistaken deliveries and payment of a debt proved not to be owed. Section 667 adds interest or returns and damage consequences for bad-faith receipt. Sections 668–669 concern payment of another's debt or tax. Section 670 regulates found goods through police notification, care, expenses and its conditions. Section 671 provides the two-year claim period. A mistaken duplicate payment is an easier example than claiming all profitable transactions are unjust.

## Nepal's law

Use Part 5 chapters 15–16, sections 648–671, with 538–539 where an existing contract has ended. Read [remedies](unit-11-breach-remedies.md) for the distinction between payment, return and damages.

## Full 20-mark answers

??? example "Q1. Explain indirect contract and unjust enrichment under Nepal's Civil Code."
    !!! warning "Unverified"
        This answer uses the checked Nepal text through Jestha 2076. Complete later-amendment currency remains unverified.

    **Obligations without an ordinary bargain**

    An enforceable agreement usually begins with offer and acceptance, but not every civil duty rests on that process. A person may save another's property during a disaster, pay an amount not actually owed or receive goods by mistake. The law needs rules for these situations even where nobody agreed a price or promised repayment beforehand. Nepal's Civil Code recognises indirect or quasi-contracts in sections 648 to 663 and unjust enrichment in sections 664 to 671. The two chapters should be explained through their particular provisions, rather than through a fictional promise which the facts do not support.

    Section 648 recognises a definite, lawful, voluntary or unilateral act capable of producing a legal relationship. The obligation follows from the legally relevant facts despite the ordinary formation chapter. This does not mean that every helpful act creates an enforceable demand for money. The later provisions identify situations, parties and limits. Their importance is that they can recognise a duty without pretending that an absent owner or an unconscious patient negotiated terms. A court still needs proof of the facts bringing the claim within the relevant provision and of the amount recoverable under it.

    **Voluntary management and care**

    Section 649 addresses managing another person's business or property without prior information or authority. A person who takes on that role cannot simply abandon it without the handover or removal described in the section. Section 650 requires good-faith care as for one's own property and addresses loss caused in bad faith or recklessly. These provisions combine responsibility with assistance. They prevent someone from assuming control, leaving the owner dependent on that intervention and then walking away carelessly. The absence of an ordinary contract does not leave the manager free of all legal obligations arising from the management undertaken.

    Section 652 gives a practical example of reimbursement. Someone protecting property during fire, flood or another listed disaster may recover reasonable expenses from the owner under its conditions. Suppose a neighbour pays workers to move stored grain away from rising water while the owner cannot be reached. The relevant claim concerns reasonable protection expenditure. It is not necessarily a claim for any reward the neighbour later chooses. The distinction between reasonable expenses and an invented price matters because the law supplies the obligation; the parties did not agree an unlimited fee for the intervention in advance.

    **Other statutory situations**

    The chapter separately addresses maintenance, funeral and treatment expenses. These provisions identify who should bear the cost and sometimes exclude assistance intended as a gift. Their conditions must be respected. Urgent treatment of an injured stranger differs from a relative providing help gratuitously within the stated exception. Similarly, lawful voluntary performance producing a benefit under section 653 differs from expenditure undertaken for an unlawful purpose. The Nepali wording of that provision concerns an actor not legally compelled to perform. The unofficial English text should not be used to reverse that important qualification when describing the reimbursement rule.

    Sections 659 to 661 address further payment situations. Section 659 concerns a promise to pay an amount legally payable by another, followed by the promisor's failure and payment by the person legally liable. Section 660 concerns goods, services or work, while section 661 addresses supply to persons incapable of contracting or those they must support. These are statutory bases requiring their own analysis. They should not be confused with enforcing an incapable person's own agreement. Nor should India's section 68 property-based necessaries rule be substituted for Nepal's differently worded provision simply because both concern a protected recipient.

    **The meaning of unjust enrichment**

    Section 664 addresses a benefit received without the lawful basis described in the chapter. Benefit is broader than receiving cash. It may increase property, reduce a liability, provide a service or involve use of another's property. However, a gain is not unjust merely because it is large or unequal. A payment properly owed under a lawful contract has a basis. A freely conferred benefit with the relevant agreement not to reclaim it also needs the statutory qualification considered. The inquiry therefore connects a particular benefit with the absence of justification, not simply with a moral complaint about somebody becoming better off.

    A duplicate payment illustrates the distinction. If a customer pays the same debt twice by mistake and proves the second payment was not owed, section 666 provides a route for its return. The recipient cannot justify retaining it merely by pointing to the existence of the original debt after that debt has already been satisfied. Conversely, a first payment actually due under the agreement is not unjust simply because the customer later needs the money. The facts must show why the particular transfer lacked the relevant basis. Proof of the payment and the underlying liability is therefore central to the analysis.

    **Specific consequences and their limits**

    Section 665 concerns mistaken delivery, and section 667 adds consequences for receipt in bad faith, including the stated interest or returns and compensation where the amount or goods are lost or damaged. Other sections address a third person's payment of debt or tax. Found goods receive their own treatment in section 670, including police notification and safeguards. These provisions show why restitution is not one undifferentiated remedy. The appropriate result depends on how the benefit arrived, the recipient's legal position and the facts specified by the particular section. An honest mistake and deliberate wrongful retention need not carry identical consequences.

    Both chapters include limitation provisions, sections 663 and 671, using the checked two-year period from the cause of action. Claims must also establish the relevant expenses, price or benefit rather than treating an assertion of fairness as proof of amount. In conclusion, indirect contract and unjust enrichment fill important spaces outside ordinary agreement. They recognise duties arising from lawful intervention and unjustified receipt while preserving specific conditions and exceptions. The best approach identifies the statutory situation, the person liable, the recoverable amount and any qualification, keeping these duties distinct from contractual damages and from a volunteer's unrestricted demand for payment.

??? example "Q2. Explain quantum meruit and distinguish it from damages and restitution."
    !!! warning "Unverified"
        This answer uses the checked Nepal text through Jestha 2076. Complete later-amendment currency remains unverified.

    **Meaning and legal setting**

    Quantum meruit is commonly explained as payment for what is deserved. In contract study it directs attention to work performed or a benefit supplied where a claim for remuneration arises. The phrase does not itself decide whether a claim exists or how much must be paid. Those questions depend on the governing law and facts. Nepal's Civil Code contains several provisions relevant to completed work and benefits, especially sections 538, 539 and 660. They should be identified separately. A Latin expression cannot replace the statutory conditions or justify billing a stranger for every unsolicited service that someone chooses to perform.

    The distinction matters when a project ends before completion. Suppose a builder has lawfully completed an identifiable part of a repair before the other party's breach brings the agreement to an end. The builder's possible claim for completed work is different from a claim for all the expected profit on the unfinished project. It is also different from recovery of an advance which the builder never earned. The same dispute may contain all these questions. Careful analysis identifies what each payment would compensate or restore, rather than putting every amount under the convenient label quantum meruit and adding them together.

    **Proportionate contractual payment under section 539**

    Section 539 addresses a contract ending through breach or another reason. It allows the injured party to claim the contractual amount in proportion to work or performance completed. The agreed contract remains relevant to the calculation. If a contract identifies stages and their prices, those terms help explain the completed portion, subject to the facts and law. The section is therefore not simply a permission to ignore the price and demand any market figure. The claimant must establish the work performed, the termination setting and the connection between that work and the proportionate amount claimed from the other party.

    Previous payments and defective work also need attention when identifying the actual claim. A person cannot reasonably treat the same completed stage as both fully paid and wholly unpaid. Evidence might include the agreement, delivery records, progress certificates and payments. These are examples of proof rather than a claim that one particular document is always decisive. The important point is that a proportionate claim has to be explained and supported. An assertion that a project is half finished does not automatically prove entitlement to half of every possible sum associated with the overall commercial arrangement or its expected profits.

    **Reasonable value in other statutory settings**

    Section 538 addresses return and accounting when a contract is cancelled, invalidated or otherwise falls within its stated situations after benefits or partial performance. Subsection (2) requires a reasonable amount for services or benefits that cannot simply be returned as cash or goods. This is related to valuation but arises through a particular statutory setting. A service already consumed cannot literally be handed back in the same way as an unused item. The law therefore needs a monetary response. The claimant must still identify the service, the recipient, the relevant ending or invalidity and the reasonable amount supported by the evidence.

    Section 660 supplies another payment provision concerning goods, services or work. It sits within the indirect-contract chapter, whose wider framework and individual rules matter. These provisions should not be merged into a universal rule that anyone who confers a benefit may recover a full commercial charge. Some adjacent sections expressly exclude gratuitous help, and unjust enrichment has its own lawful-basis qualifications. The legal source of entitlement must come before valuation. Otherwise, an apparently fair discussion of the price may conceal the fact that no applicable right to charge has yet been established on the actual circumstances presented.

    **Distinction from breach damages**

    Damages under section 537 respond to loss caused by breach. They require the relevant contractual duty, breach and legally recoverable loss. Quantum-meruit language instead concerns payment measured by work or benefit in the applicable setting. A builder may have performed work worth a proportion of the agreed price and also suffered a separately provable loss because of wrongful termination. Recognising those different questions does not authorise counting the same financial disadvantage twice. Each head of claim needs its own purpose and evidence. The damages provision's limits cannot be avoided merely by renaming a speculative loss remuneration for services.

    For example, a contractor who completed a paid stage cannot claim that same stage again as damages simply because the relationship ended unhappily. Equally, a proportionate-payment claim does not automatically include every expected future sale or reputational opportunity. Section 537 distinguishes the applicable actual and anticipated loss categories and excludes the stated indirect or imaginary losses. The remedy must follow the proven legal injury. Separating payment from damages therefore improves both fairness and accuracy. It allows the value of actual performance to be addressed without turning it into a vehicle for unlimited recovery of every disappointing commercial consequence.

    **Distinction from restitution and comparison**

    Restitution concerns returning or accounting for a benefit which the law requires the recipient to restore. A duplicate payment of a debt may be returned under section 666 even though the payer performed no service for which remuneration is due. Conversely, a contractual proportionate-payment claim under section 539 remains connected with an agreement and completed performance. The categories may meet in service valuation, but their legal bases are not identical. One should ask whether the claim enforces agreed payment, compensates breach loss or reverses a legally unjustified benefit. Those questions help locate the right Code provision and prevent a misleading single label.

    English law's Benedetti v Sawiris illustrates restitutionary valuation of services in a setting where the price was not expressly quantified by contract. It is a useful comparison, not the source of Nepal's section 539. In conclusion, quantum meruit is best understood through a demonstrated right to recover and a justified measure of work or benefit. The Nepal analysis identifies the applicable statutory route, proves performance, accounts for payments and distinguishes damages from restoration. This method gives the Latin phrase practical meaning while preventing it from becoming an unsupported promise that every helpful act, failed bargain or unfinished project guarantees a full financial return.

## Test yourself

??? question "Must a quasi-contract claimant invent an accepted offer?"
    No. Establish the statutory facts creating the obligation.

??? question "What can the flood rescuer claim under 652?"
    Reasonable protection expenses under its conditions, not an arbitrary reward.

??? question "Is every beneficial act chargeable?"
    No. Identify the legal basis and any gratuitous-assistance qualification.

??? question "What is central to 539?"
    Proportionate contractual payment for completed work or performance in its ending setting.

??? question "Is a payment actually owed necessarily unjust enrichment?"
    No. A lawful basis for retaining it changes the analysis.

    No. Apply Nepal 661 and its own wording.

## One-minute revision

- Sections 648–663 govern indirect or quasi-contract obligations.
- Particular reimbursement rules have particular conditions.
- Quantum meruit does not create an unlimited volunteer's bill.
- Section 539 concerns proportionate contractual payment.
- Section 664 asks about unjustified benefit, not mere profit.
- Mistaken payments and bad-faith receipts have distinct provisions.
