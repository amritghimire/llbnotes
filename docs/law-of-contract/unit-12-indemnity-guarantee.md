# Unit 12: Indemnity and guarantee

!!! abstract "In one line"
    Indemnity allocates a covered loss; guarantee backs another person's debt or performance if that person defaults. The wording, trigger and statutory protections matter.

!!! warning "Unverified"
    Nepal provisions were checked against the Nepali government text through Jestha 2076 and Law Commission repository text. Complete later-amendment currency remains unverified. A special bank instrument or on-demand undertaking needs its own terms and legal analysis; textbook labels alone do not decide its effect.

## Why it matters for the exam

- Revocation of continuing guarantee appears in 2078 Q7(c).
- Learn Nepal's writing requirement, debtor-notice requirement and three-month advance notice for future revocation. Do not substitute India's rules.

## 12.1 Indemnity: meaning, features, rights and duties

Section 571 concerns an undertaking to bear specified loss while a person acts under another party's direction, with the conduct and losses described there. Its defining setting must be explained rather than replaced with an unlimited promise against every possible loss.

Subject to the contract, 571(2) permits the holder to recover the agreed indemnity amount, the amount borne for third-party loss, relevant litigation costs and the stated enforcement expenses. The indemnifier must meet the undertaking within its scope. The holder must establish coverage and the sums claimed. Under 571(3), unless the contract provides otherwise, the person carrying out directed work bears responsibility for loss caused by bad-faith intentional harm or by negligent conduct. Negligence is a separate alternative; it does not also require proof of an intention to cause loss. Any claimed contractual allocation must still satisfy the general lawfulness rules.

Example: a person carries out directed work under a lawful indemnity undertaking and faces a covered third-party claim. Identify the direction, covered conduct, loss and costs. The mere existence of a third-party demand does not prove every term of the indemnity claim.

Sections 572–573 separately address subrogation in their covered-loss setting, payment and transferred recovery rights. They are related provisions, not the definition of indemnity itself. Do not confuse them with the surety's creditor substitution under 567.

## 12.2 Guarantee

### 12.2.1 Meaning

Under 563, a third person undertakes to meet a debt or duty if the person primarily liable fails. Identify creditor, principal debtor and surety. The creditor can include a person entitled to performance or benefit, not merely a moneylender. Section 563(4) requires the guarantee contract to be written.

### 12.2.2 Types

A specific guarantee concerns a particular transaction. A continuing guarantee extends to a series under 570. Guarantees may be limited by amount, period or covered obligations. Co-surety arrangements involve several guarantors under 569. These labels describe the terms; they do not prove an unlimited liability.

### 12.2.3 Features

There is a principal duty, default as the ordinary statutory trigger, a backing undertaking and agreed scope. General validity rules remain applicable. The guarantee must be read, not assumed from a person merely introducing the debtor. Nepal's statutory writing requirement differs from India's permission for an oral guarantee.

### 12.2.4 Surety's rights, duties and liability

Section 564 makes the surety responsible on the stated default, generally corresponding to the principal duty unless otherwise agreed. A discharge of the debtor merely by operation of law does not alone end the guarantee. Subsection (2) excludes surety liability to the extent covered by security where both security and guarantee were provided. Do not ignore that qualification or infer an unverified universal execution sequence from it.

Under 564(4), before demanding the unperformed obligation from the surety, the creditor must notify the debtor to perform according to the contract. This is not an asserted requirement to complete every possible lawsuit against the debtor first.

Section 566 regulates the surety–debtor relationship, including performance, limits on dealing with supplied security without consent and changes to the purpose or terms of the backed transaction. Under 567, a paying surety steps into the creditor's position and can recover the stated debt, interest or charges and enforcement expenses. Section 569 generally divides co-surety responsibility equally unless otherwise agreed; where divided into parts, each bears the guaranteed part.

### 12.2.5 Discharge of surety

Section 565, subject to its qualifications, addresses material variation without consent for later transactions; release of the principal duty by agreement; the creditor's release or remission; agreed lesser settlement, extension or promise not to sue; impairment of the surety's remedy; lost, damaged or returned security to its value; and performance to the extent completed.

Mere failure to take timely action or try collection does not by itself discharge under 565(2), subject to its wording. Do not confuse this with unlimited time to sue: limitation still needs checking. Release of one co-surety does not automatically release the others. Where the shares cannot be separated, subsection (4) says that even the purportedly released surety is not freed from liability. A dispute over the principal transaction alone does not discharge under (5), subject to contrary terms.

### 12.2.6 Continuing guarantee and revocation

Section 570 covers a series of transactions, with responsibility for unpaid amounts within the guaranteed limit during the operative period. Under (3), the surety can revoke for **future transactions by at least three months' advance notice to the creditor**. This does not erase existing covered liability. Death ends the guarantee for later transactions unless otherwise agreed, under (4).

Example: a guarantee covers successive lawful credit supplies up to a stated limit. Separate supplies already covered from future supplies after effective revocation. A notice today is not automatically effective today under Nepal's three-month provision.

### 12.2.7 Grounds of invalidity

Section 568 permits the surety to have the guarantee avoided for the stated misleading information by the creditor or with consent, concealment or omission of material guarantee matters, or failure of an intended additional guarantor to consent. State the avoidance route rather than calling every such guarantee automatically nonexistent. General capacity, consent, legality and form requirements also remain relevant.

### 12.2.8 Indemnity versus guarantee

| Point | Indemnity | Guarantee |
|---|---|---|
| Basic concern | Covered loss in 571's setting | Another's default under 563 |
| Main relationships | Holder and indemnifier; third-party loss may be involved | Creditor, debtor and surety |
| Scope | Covered conduct, loss and agreed undertaking | Backed duty, default, terms and statutory protections |
| Recovery after payment | Examine 572–573 where applicable | Creditor substitution under 567 |
| Nepal writing rule | No special guarantee rule imported into 571 | Express writing requirement in 563(4) |

## Nepal's law

Part 5 chapter 7, sections 563–574. Section 574 supplies the chapter's checked two-year claim period. General contract rules supplement the specific provisions. An indemnity and a guarantee can both involve third-party conduct; number of people alone is not a reliable classification test.

## Full 20-mark answers

??? example "Q1. Explain indemnity and distinguish it from guarantee under Nepal's Civil Code."
    !!! warning "Unverified"
        This answer uses the checked Nepal text through Jestha 2076. Complete later-amendment currency remains unverified.

    **Purpose of the two undertakings**

    Indemnity and guarantee both allocate financial responsibility, but they do so through different legal structures. An indemnity concerns a covered loss within the undertaking and applicable statute. A guarantee answers for another person's debt or duty if that person defaults. Nepal's Civil Code places these provisions in Part 5 chapter 7, defining guarantee in section 563 and addressing indemnity in section 571. The distinction cannot be settled merely by counting the people involved or reading the document's title. The actual promise, its trigger and the statutory conditions determine whether and to what extent a person must bear the claimed obligation.

    Suppose someone agrees to protect a person carrying out directed work against the covered loss described by their undertaking. That raises the indemnity framework. Now suppose a shop obtains supplies on credit and another person undertakes to pay if the shop defaults. That raises guarantee. Both situations may involve a third person, such as an injured claimant or supplier, but the role of that person differs. The first analysis concerns coverage of loss; the second concerns backing a principal obligation upon default. These ordinary examples show why a legal explanation must identify the relationship and promise instead of relying on a superficial numerical distinction.

    **Indemnity under section 571**

    Section 571 describes the directed-work and conduct setting in which the undertaking is treated as indemnity. This wording should not be replaced with an unlimited promise against every possible loss. The claimant needs to identify the direction, relevant conduct, loss and agreed protection. For example, an indemnity holder facing a third-party claim must establish that the claim falls within the undertaking. The fact that a demand was made does not itself prove liability for every amount demanded. The contract's scope and the statutory conditions remain essential to deciding what the indemnifier has actually promised and what the law permits recovery of.

    Subject to the contract, subsection (2) addresses the agreed indemnity amount, sums borne for third-party loss, relevant litigation costs and the specified expenses of enforcing payment. These heads explain why indemnity may involve more than the immediate physical damage alone. They nevertheless require the connection contemplated by the undertaking. A claimant should identify each sum and its basis rather than present an undifferentiated total. Equally, the indemnifier cannot disregard an established covered obligation merely because the loss involved another person's conduct. The point of the promise is to allocate the risk described, within the terms and law governing the transaction.

    **Limits and related recovery rights**

    Section 571(3) addresses personal responsibility for loss caused while carrying out directed work, unless the contract provides otherwise. Its Nepali wording distinguishes bad-faith intentional harm from negligent conduct. Negligence does not require the additional proof of intent which the first alternative involves. This matters to both coverage and the person ultimately responsible. The holder cannot assume that following instructions automatically establishes protection for every loss. The contractual qualification, manner of conduct and general lawfulness requirements must be examined together. A lawful allocation of risk is a separate question from whether the actor caused the particular loss through the conduct described by the section.

    Sections 572 and 573 separately address subrogation in their covered-loss setting. After the relevant payment, recovery rights can pass as the statute provides, allowing the payer to claim against the person responsible for loss. This must not be confused with the definition of indemnity itself. Nor is it identical to section 567's substitution of a paying surety into the creditor's position. Both mechanisms concern rights after payment, but the statutory relationships and conditions differ. Careful section identification prevents the answer from using the word subrogation as if it created an identical universal right in every transaction involving compensation or guarantee.

    **Guarantee and its trigger**

    Under section 563, guarantee involves an undertaking to fulfil a debt or duty if the person primarily responsible fails. The usual roles are creditor, principal debtor and surety. The creditor can be a person entitled to performance or another benefit, not only a lender of money. The terms determine the backed obligation, period and limit. Section 563(4) requires the guarantee contract to be written. A person should not be treated as an unlimited surety merely because they introduced the debtor or expressed confidence in the debtor's reliability. The legally sufficient undertaking and its scope must be established on the actual facts.

    Section 564 addresses liability when the principal person fails, generally corresponding to the principal obligation unless otherwise agreed. The provision includes important qualifications concerning security and notice to the debtor before demand on the surety. These details distinguish a proper Nepal account from a copied foreign slogan about immediate liability. They should not, however, be expanded into an unverified requirement to complete every possible lawsuit against the debtor first. The answer needs to state the actual notice rule and security qualification while recognising that the parties' terms and other chapter provisions affect the particular guarantee obligation asserted by the creditor.

    **Comparison and conclusion**

    The guarantee chapter gives the surety discharge protections, recovery after payment, co-surety rules and the continuing-guarantee framework. Indemnity instead centres on covered loss and the particular rights and limits in section 571, with related provisions where applicable. India's Contract Act sections 124 to 126 use their own definitions and allow oral or written guarantee, unlike Nepal's express writing rule. English enforcement of the covered guarantee promise generally requires the written evidence contemplated by the Statute of Frauds, subject to its legal qualifications. These comparisons demonstrate the need to identify jurisdiction before treating a familiar textbook rule as binding local law.

    In conclusion, indemnity and guarantee should be distinguished through the promise made, the event activating responsibility and the recovery framework. Indemnity is not merely a guarantee with fewer named people, and guarantee is not an unlimited promise covering every loss associated with the debtor. The Nepal answer identifies sections 563 and 571, explains scope and proof, states writing and notice requirements where applicable and preserves the separate post-payment rights. This method gives both undertakings practical meaning while preventing their labels from concealing unsupported liability, deliberate harmful conduct or an assumption that every foreign definition and remedy automatically applies in Nepal.

??? example "Q2. Explain a surety's liability, rights, discharge and revocation of continuing guarantee."

    **Nature and scope of liability**

    A surety undertakes to answer for another person's debt or performance on the relevant default. Nepal's Civil Code section 563 supplies the guarantee framework and requires writing. The undertaking must identify the backed obligation and its agreed limits. A guarantee can support cooperation by giving a creditor additional security, but it also exposes the surety to responsibility for another person's failure. The Code therefore combines liability with safeguards, discharge rules and recovery rights. A complete account must explain both sides, rather than present the surety as either automatically responsible for everything or free to withdraw from every existing obligation whenever difficulties arise.

    Section 564 generally connects liability with failure of the principal duty and, subject to the contract, corresponding responsibility. A debtor's release merely through operation of law does not alone end the guarantee. The section also contains a security qualification: where security and guarantee both exist, surety liability is excluded to the extent covered by the security. Before demand against the surety, subsection (4) requires notice to the debtor to perform according to the contract. These rules must be stated accurately. They do not establish an unrestricted creditor demand divorced from notice, nor a universal requirement to exhaust every conceivable lawsuit first.

    **Relations with the debtor and recovery**

    Section 566 regulates the surety's relationship with the debtor, including getting the principal duty performed and restrictions on dealing with property or security supplied by the debtor without consent. Changes to the purpose, nature or terms of the backed transaction require the stated attention to the surety's consent. These protections matter because the surety's risk was undertaken on a particular basis. It would be misleading to assume that a guarantee of one transaction automatically covers every later change the debtor and creditor find convenient. The actual undertaking, any consent and the governing statutory qualification must be examined before extending its scope.

    When the surety pays or performs, section 567 substitutes the surety into the creditor's position in the stated circumstances. The surety can seek the relevant debt or performance from the debtor and recover the stated interest, charges and necessary enforcement expenses. This explains why a guarantee is not necessarily intended as a final gift to the principal debtor. Nevertheless, the claim must fit the provision and the amount actually supported. Co-sureties have further rules under section 569, generally equal responsibility unless otherwise agreed, with liability limited to the guaranteed part where the arrangement is divided into parts. Their internal relationship should not be assumed from the mere number of signatures.

    **Discharge under section 565**

    Section 565 addresses several routes of discharge, subject to its qualifications. Material variation without consent affects the later transactions described. Release of the principal obligation by agreement, the creditor's release or remission and specified settlements, extensions or agreements not to sue can also matter. The creditor's impairment of the surety's remedy is a further ground, while lost, damaged or returned security affects liability to its value. Actual payment or performance reduces the backed obligation to the extent fulfilled. These are distinct legal events. An answer should identify which occurred and the extent of release rather than assume that every alteration erases every earlier liability.

    Mere failure to take timely legal action or attempt collection does not alone discharge the surety under subsection (2), subject to its wording. This differs from a binding agreement changing the creditor's enforcement position. It also does not create unlimited time to bring proceedings; the applicable limitation period still needs attention. Release of one co-surety does not automatically release the others, and subsection (4) keeps even the purportedly released surety liable where the shares cannot be separated. A dispute about the principal transaction alone likewise does not discharge the guarantee in the terms of subsection (5). These qualifications prevent broad textbook slogans from becoming inaccurate statements of the Nepal position.

    The extent of discharge is as important as the ground. If material variation affects later transactions under the stated rule, it should not automatically be described as erasing every previously covered debt. If security is lost, the provision connects release with its value rather than necessarily the whole guarantee. A payment by the debtor likewise reduces the obligation to the extent actually satisfied. These distinctions require the amount, timing and relevant consent to be established. They protect the surety from an enlarged or impaired risk while preventing an unsupported claim that any change, payment or lost item always removes all responsibility under an otherwise operative undertaking.

    **Continuing guarantee and future revocation**

    Section 570 defines continuing guarantee by its extension to a series of transactions. A supply-credit arrangement covering successive deliveries up to a stated amount provides an ordinary example. Subsection (2) connects responsibility with unpaid amounts within the guaranteed limit during the operative period. The number of transactions does not itself make liability unlimited. The terms, amount and period need to be read. This differs from a specific guarantee for one delivery. Identifying the type matters because a later order may be covered by a continuing undertaking but fall outside a guarantee confined to an already completed single transaction.

    Subsection (3) permits revocation for future transactions by at least three months' advance notice to the creditor. A notice today is therefore not automatically an immediate Nepal release. Existing covered liability must be separated from later transactions after effective revocation. India's section 130 has its own notice rule and does not supply Nepal's minimum period. Under section 570(4), death ends the guarantee for later transactions unless otherwise agreed. These rules require a timeline. The dates of supplies, notice and effective ending should be identified before calculating whether a disputed unpaid amount remains within the guarantee or falls outside its future coverage.

    **Avoidance and conclusion**

    Section 568 provides the surety's avoidance route for the stated misleading information, concealment or omission concerning the guarantee, and absence of an intended additional guarantor's consent. This differs from prospective revocation of an otherwise operative continuing guarantee. The distinction matters to both ground and consequence. General validity rules also remain relevant, and section 574 provides the chapter's checked two-year claim period. In conclusion, a surety's responsibility follows the written undertaking and the Code's default, notice and security rules. Discharge, recovery and future revocation each have their own conditions. Applying those conditions with the actual transaction timeline protects creditors without converting guarantee into unsupported or unlimited liability.

## One-minute revision

- Identify the promise, trigger and amount limit.
- Guarantee must be written in Nepal.
- Apply debtor notice and security qualifications under 564.
- Section 565 governs discharge; 567 post-payment recovery.
- Continuing guarantee covers a series, not unlimited liability.

- Indemnity at 571 differs from the related subrogation provisions.
