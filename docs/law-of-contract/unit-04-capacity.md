# Unit 4: Capacity to contract

!!! abstract "In one line"
    Capacity asks whether a person can legally make the commitment. It is different from whether the person appeared to agree or signed a document.

!!! warning "Unverified"

## Why it matters for the exam

- No standalone capacity question appears in the two available papers. It remains a syllabus unit and part of every valid-contract answer.
- Explain section 506 with the general capacity provisions. Avoid replacing Nepal's scheme with the effect of a minor's agreement under an Indian case.

## 4.1 Meaning of capacity

Capacity is legal ability, not practical cleverness, literacy, wealth or social status. A child may understand a purchase but still lack ordinary independent contractual capacity. An adult unable to read is not for that reason incapable. A company acts through authorised people. Identify who the party is and whether any representative has authority.

Section 505(1) makes capacity an enforceability condition. Section 506 supplies the contract-specific rules. General sections 32–35 explain adulthood, incapacity, partial capacity and court resolution of disputes about capacity.

## 4.2 Persons who cannot contract

### 4.2.1 Minor

Under section 32, eighteen is the threshold of adulthood and legal capacity. Section 33 treats a person below ten as legally incapable. Section 34 treats a person aged ten to below eighteen as partially capable for general civil purposes, with the specified role of a guardian or curator. **Partial civil capacity does not override section 506's exclusion of minors from ordinary independent contracting.**

Section 506(3) permits a guardian or curator to contract on behalf of an incapable or partially capable person. This is a representative transaction, not automatic validation of the child's own signature. Check the representative's legal powers and the transaction's requirements. Section 517(2)(ञ) treats a contract made by a person not qualified to contract as void.

Example: a seventeen-year-old signs a major loan alone. The lender cannot establish ordinary capacity merely by pointing out that the borrower is over ten. Whether a legally authorised representative can make a different transaction requires separate examination.

### 4.2.2 Person of unsound mind

The general explanation in section 33 concerns inability, because of physical or mental ill health, to understand an act and its consequences in the ordinary sense. Section 506 addresses the timing of soundness. A usually unsound person can contract during a sound interval. A usually sound person cannot contract while unsound. Do not infer incapacity from a diagnosis, disability or unusual decision alone.

Example: an adult signs while temporarily unable to understand the act and its consequences because of the relevant condition. Ask about that time and that transaction, using evidence. A different contract made after recovery needs a fresh capacity assessment.

### 4.2.3 Others disqualified by law

Section 506(2) recognises disqualification from a particular contract by law. Subsection (5) preserves a special law permitting a person otherwise excluded under this chapter to make a particular contract. Name and check that law before asserting an exception.

Section 506(4) requires a legal person to act through an authorised director, management decision or representative. Authority is not identical to capacity. A company's existence does not establish that any employee may sell its assets. Section 519(2)(ग) addresses contracts beyond or without authority.

## Full 20-mark answers

??? example "Q1. Explain the contractual capacity of minors and persons of unsound mind in Nepal."
    !!! warning "Unverified"
        This answer uses the checked Nepal text through Jestha 2076. Later amendments remain incompletely verified.

    A person's apparent willingness to agree does not alone establish an enforceable contract. The law also asks whether the person has the legal capacity to undertake the commitment. Section 505 of Nepal's Civil Code makes capacity a condition of enforceability, while section 506 provides the contract-specific framework. General sections 32 to 35 explain adulthood, incapacity, partial capacity and disputes about legal status. These provisions should be read together. The purpose is to protect people whose legal ability to undertake obligations is limited, without treating disability, poverty or an unconventional choice as automatic proof of incapacity.

    **Meaning of capacity and minority**

    Capacity is legal ability, rather than intelligence, commercial success or ability to sign. A child may be skilled at using a phone and still lack ordinary independent power to enter a major contract. An adult may be unable to read and still have capacity if the other legal requirements are satisfied. This distinction matters because a signature proves an act of signing, not every condition of validity. The proper question is whether the person could legally enter the particular agreement at the relevant time and, if represented, whether the representative had lawful authority to act.

    Section 32 treats a person who has completed eighteen years as an adult with legal capacity, subject to law. Section 33 addresses persons below ten and persons unable to protect their interests because of the specified unsound condition. Section 34 describes those aged ten to below eighteen as partially capable for general civil purposes and provides a role for a guardian or curator. These age categories explain the general civil scheme. They do not mean that every person over ten can independently make every contract. The specific rule in section 506 must also be applied.

    **The contract-specific rule for minors**

    Section 506 excludes minors from ordinary contractual capacity. A seventeen-year-old does not overcome that exclusion merely by being partially capable under section 34. For example, a lender cannot establish an enforceable major loan simply by showing that the child understood how to spend the money or signed the lender's form. The child's apparent agreement and the legal capacity condition are separate matters. Section 517(2)(ञ) treats a contract made by a person not qualified to contract as void. The answer must identify that consequence rather than describe minority as merely a reason to renegotiate an otherwise ordinary obligation.

    Section 506(3) allows a guardian or curator to contract on behalf of an incapable or partially capable person. This is an important qualification, but it should not be overstated. The representative transaction requires the relevant legal powers and compliance with the applicable rules. It is not a general permission for an adult to approve any bargain and thereby make the child's personal undertaking valid. The identity of the party and the capacity in which the adult acts must be clear. A transaction for a protected person's affairs differs from making that person independently liable for every promise an adult chooses to endorse.

    **Protection and the consequences of invalidity**

    The protective approach would be weakened if a trader could bypass it by changing the label of the claim. Calling a promised contractual payment compensation does not itself create a lawful basis for recovery. At the same time, invalidity of the contract does not decide every issue involving property already transferred or benefits received. Other statutory obligations may require separate examination. A good answer acknowledges that distinction without inventing a complete restitution rule. The student should explain the established capacity consequence and identify any further issue as requiring its own legal source and factual analysis.

    This is also where comparative law needs care. India's section 68 provides a particular reimbursement rule involving necessaries supplied to a person incapable of contracting and persons whom that person is legally bound to support. It operates against property in the terms stated. That Indian provision cannot simply be cited as Nepal's own law. Likewise, a famous Indian minors case cannot replace section 506 or determine every power of a Nepal guardian. Comparative examples should illuminate the issue, while the actual conclusion remains grounded in the governing Nepal provisions and the legally relevant transaction.

    **Persons of unsound mind**

    The Code's general explanation concerns inability, arising from the relevant physical or mental condition, to understand the act and its consequences in the ordinary sense. The analysis is functional and tied to the person's condition. A diagnosis alone does not prove that all decisions by the person are invalid. Equally, outward confidence does not establish understanding. The inquiry needs evidence about the person at the time of the agreement. This avoids both exploitation of a vulnerable person and unnecessary exclusion of someone who can understand and undertake the particular commitment in question.

    Section 506 expressly addresses intervals of soundness and unsoundness. A person usually not of sound mind may contract while sound. A person usually sound may not contract while unsound. Suppose an adult experiences a temporary condition preventing ordinary understanding when signing a transaction, but later recovers. The relevant contract must be assessed at its own time. A different agreement after recovery requires a fresh assessment. Neither a permanent label nor an assumption based on the person's usual state answers both situations. This timing rule is a major point to include in an examination answer.

    **Special law, representation and proof**

    Section 506 also recognises particular legal disqualifications and special statutory permission for particular contracts. These clauses require the student to identify the law involved. A copied list declaring all prisoners, foreigners or insolvent people incapable is insufficient without a Nepal source and the relevant scope. Legal persons raise another question. They act through authorised management or representatives under subsection (4). A company's capacity does not mean that every employee can bind it. Absence or excess of representative authority raises the separate issue addressed by section 519, rather than necessarily showing that the company itself lacks capacity.

    Section 35 provides for judicial determination where capacity status is disputed. In a factual problem, the answer should therefore identify the contested facts, the applicable capacity category and the evidence needed, rather than assume the result from a social description. In conclusion, Nepal's approach combines age protection, assessment of soundness at contracting, lawful representation and special statutory rules. The general civil provisions and section 506 must be read together. A complete answer protects vulnerable persons while preserving the legal significance of competent choice, and keeps contractual invalidity separate from any additional noncontractual claim requiring independent support.

??? example "Q2. Distinguish contractual capacity, consent and representative authority."
    !!! warning "Unverified"
        This answer uses the checked Nepal text through Jestha 2076. Later amendments remain incompletely verified.

    Capacity, consent and authority are closely connected, but each answers a different legal question. Capacity asks whether a person can legally make the commitment. Consent asks whether that person actually agreed in the legally relevant way and whether that agreement was defective. Authority asks whether someone acting for another can bind that other person. Nepal's Civil Code gives these issues separate treatment. Section 505 includes capacity and consent among enforceability conditions, section 506 develops capacity, section 518 addresses specified defects of consent and section 519 addresses certain authority defects. Distinguishing them is essential to an accurate legal conclusion.

    **Capacity as legal ability**

    Capacity does not mean education, wealth or bargaining strength. It concerns the legal ability to enter the particular agreement. The general rules in sections 32 to 35 recognise adulthood at eighteen, incapacity and partial capacity. Section 506 then supplies the specific contractual exclusions and qualifications. A minor may be commercially experienced but still lack ordinary independent contractual capacity. Conversely, an adult with little schooling is not incapable simply because the contract is complicated. The law's categories and the facts relevant to those categories must control the assessment, rather than assumptions about social position or appearance.

    Soundness at the time of contracting illustrates the functional aspect of capacity. Section 506 allows a usually unsound person to contract while sound and prevents a usually sound person from contracting while unsound. The question is not settled by the person's usual reputation. It requires attention to the relevant interval and transaction. A person may also be disqualified by a particular law from a particular contract. That is not necessarily a general inability to act in every civil matter. A careful answer therefore identifies the scope of the rule before applying a broad description such as incapable.

    **Consent as actual and legally sufficient assent**

    A competent adult may still not have agreed to the proposed terms. Offer and acceptance under section 504 require assent in the same meaning. If a seller proposes one identified item and the buyer purportedly accepts a different item at another price, the issue may be formation rather than capacity. Likewise, section 505 requires consent expressing a binding commitment. Casual discussion or an invitation to negotiate does not necessarily supply it. Capacity makes agreement legally possible, but it does not prove that a particular agreement was actually reached between the people concerned.

    Even where assent appears, its quality may be challenged. Section 518 permits the injured party to ask a court to set aside contracts resulting from coercion, undue influence, fraud or misrepresentation. A fully capable adult can be threatened or deceived. There is no need to pretend that the person became a minor or permanently incapable before recognising that problem. An unlawful threat to secure a signature illustrates coercion, while manipulation through domination may raise undue influence. The correct legal route follows the nature of the defect and carries the consequences of a voidable contract under the checked text.

    **Authority as power to act for another**

    Representation introduces a further distinction. A company may possess legal personality and the ability to contract, yet must act through humans. Section 506(4) identifies authorised directors, management decisions and representatives. An employee's personal adulthood and soundness do not automatically authorise that employee to sell the company's property. Personal capacity and representative power concern different persons and different issues. The first asks whether the employee can make their own legal commitments. The second asks whether the employee's act is attributable to the company in the particular transaction before the court.

    Section 519(2)(ग) addresses contracts made on another's behalf without authority or beyond the authority given. An answer should not call every such case the principal's incapacity. The principal may be perfectly capable and may have chosen a limited representative role. The problem is that the act did not fit that role. To analyse it, identify the principal, the person acting, the supposed source of power and its scope. Evidence might concern an appointment or management decision. Detailed agency consequences belong to the agency rules, but the foundational distinction must be maintained even at this introductory stage.

    **Guardians and protected persons**

    A guardian's transaction brings capacity and authority together without making them identical. Section 506(3) permits a guardian or curator to contract on behalf of an incapable or partially capable person. The protected person's own legal position explains the need for representation. The adult's lawful power explains whether the representative transaction can proceed. It would be wrong to say that any adult signature automatically cures the child's independent incapacity. The adult must hold the relevant legal role, act within its powers and comply with the rules governing the particular transaction and protected interests.

    Consider a seventeen-year-old who signs a major supply agreement alone. The ordinary independent-capacity issue arises under section 506. Now consider an agreement made by a properly empowered guardian for the child's lawful affairs. The legal route differs, even if the economic subject is similar. A third possibility is an unrelated adult who claims to act as guardian without holding that role. That raises authority as well. The same printed document can therefore conceal different legal questions. The student's task is to identify which commitment is being asserted against whom and on what lawful basis.

    **Consequences and examination method**

    Different routes can lead to different consequences. Section 517(2)(ञ) addresses a contract by an unqualified person as void. Section 518 addresses specified consent defects as grounds of avoidance at the injured party's initiative. Section 519 identifies unenforceable transactions involving required form or authority defects. These categories should not be flattened into a single statement that the agreement is bad. Their timing, remedies and relationship with completed acts or third-party interests differ. A reasoned answer names the category and explains why its conditions fit the facts, while leaving separately sourced recovery questions for further analysis.

    In conclusion, an orderly analysis identifies the actual party, tests that party's capacity, examines formation and free consent, and checks representative authority where someone acted for another. It then considers mandatory form and the resulting legal consequence. This sequence avoids confusing vulnerability with incapacity or employment with unlimited power. It also prevents a signature from becoming an automatic answer to every problem. Nepal's Code treats the ability to agree, the quality of agreement and the power to represent another as related safeguards, each of which must be satisfied on its own legally relevant terms.

## Test yourself

??? question "Does being over ten establish ordinary independent capacity to contract?"
    No. Read general partial capacity with the specific exclusion of minors in section 506.

??? question "Can a usually unsound person contract during a sound interval?"
    Section 506 expressly allows this, subject to the other requirements.

??? question "Is inability to read the same as legal incapacity?"
    No. The statutory test must be applied.

??? question "Can any company employee bind the company?"
    No. Authority must be established for the transaction.

??? question "Does minority automatically decide every recovery claim?"
    No. Distinguish contractual liability from separately established noncontractual obligations.

??? question "Does an adult's signature automatically validate a child's own agreement?"
    No. A lawful representative transaction requires the relevant role and powers.

## One-minute revision

- Eighteen is the general adulthood threshold.
- General partial capacity does not override contract-specific section 506.
- Assess soundness at the time of contracting.
- Guardians and curators act through a representative legal route.
- Capacity, consent and authority are different questions.
- Do not import foreign lists or recovery rules without Nepal sources.
