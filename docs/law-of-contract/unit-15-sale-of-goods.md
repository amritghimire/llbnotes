# Unit 15: Sale of goods

!!! abstract "In one line"
    A sale transfers ownership for a price; the law separately asks what goods were promised, when ownership and risk pass, and what follows defective or missing performance.

!!! warning "Unverified"

## 15.1 Meaning

Section 545 treats a contract to transfer goods immediately or later for a price as a sale contract, conditional or unconditional. Its goods are movable property excluding currency, securities and actionable claims. A sale of land belongs under the relevant property rules, not this definition merely because a price is paid.

## 15.2 Features

Identify seller, buyer, goods, price and ownership transfer. Price may follow agreement, established dealings or the statutory reasonable-price fallback (s 547). Section 548 gives the payment/transfer default and recognises the stated banking payment methods. Do not confuse ownership with physical delivery or an unpaid price with absence of every contract.

## 15.3 Sale and agreement to sell

| Point | Completed sale | Agreement to sell |
|---|---|---|
| Ownership | Has passed under the governing rule | Will pass later/on the condition |
| Possession | May remain with seller temporarily | May already be with buyer for a limited purpose |
| Risk | Apply s 555 and contract, not possession alone | Apply s 555 and fault/delivery qualifications |
| Default | Identify price, delivery and compensation claims | Identify unperformed transfer obligation and remedy |

This is a useful textbook distinction. Nepal s 545 includes both immediate and future transfers within the chapter; do not say the future bargain falls outside sale law.

## 15.4 Goods and their types

Existing goods exist at formation; future goods will be produced or acquired. Specific goods are individually identified; unascertained goods are not yet identified to the bargain; a future selection can identify them later. Deliverable condition concerns whether the promised goods are ready for contractual handover. These categories help analyse intention and transfer under s 554; a textbook classification does not itself override the agreed terms.

## 15.5 Conditions and warranties

A textbook condition is central to the bargain; a warranty is a less central assurance whose breach ordinarily supports damages rather than rejection under the relevant foreign rule. Nepal's provisions should be stated directly: description/sample conformity (s 549), seller's title/right to sell and freedom from the stated competing possession (s 550), satisfactory quality and purpose-related fitness with qualifications (s 551), different quality and replacement/improvement (s 552), and sample sale (s 553). Do not assume India's ss 12–17 consequences apply word for word.

Section 553 requires bulk/sample quality correspondence and reasonable comparison opportunity, subject to its wording and contract. The available translations do not justify confidently substituting a verbatim Indian latent-defect formula.

## 15.6 Caveat emptor and exceptions

The buyer should specify needs and inspect where appropriate. But s 551's quality and fitness rules, s 549's description, s 553's sample safeguards, title rules and general fraud/free-consent rules qualify any buyer-beware slogan. A seller cannot answer every mismatch with “you should have checked.” Conversely, an expressly identified defect and the contract's lawful specifications can affect the result. Section 551(5) prevents inventing an additional particular quality guarantee without its contractual basis.

## 15.7 Transfer of ownership

Section 554 first looks to agreement and intention shown by terms, conduct and circumstances. It also has defaults for specific deliverable goods and goods requiring measurement or other work for pricing. Apply the actual subsection; do not silently replace the disputed wording identified above. Under s 555 risk ordinarily follows the stated ownership rule unless otherwise agreed, with fault-caused delay and the seller's undertaken destination delivery requiring separate treatment.

## 15.8 Title and exceptions

The *nemo dat* starting point protects the true owner. Section 550 states the seller's undertaking concerning entitlement; s 559 addresses essential ownership/use documents and the consequence of not providing them, subject to its terms. Apparent authority or authorised sale must be established under the applicable Nepal provision, not assumed from possession. India's Sale of Goods Act ss 27–30 supplies textbook exceptions including mercantile-agent, joint-owner, voidable-title and seller/buyer-in-possession situations. They are **Indian exceptions**, not a verified exhaustive Nepal list.

## 15.9 Performance

Sections 556–558 concern examination, acceptance and delivery time. Allow the reasonable inspection required before treating acceptance as established. Section 557 covers receipt and the specified conduct evidencing acceptance, including retention without timely rejection and ownership-like dealing. Section 560 addresses short quantity, excess quantity, mixed description and instalments. With short delivery the buyer can reject or accept and pay the agreed unit rate. Excess/mixed deliveries have the stated choices; no instalment assumption overrides the bargain. Rejection requires prompt notice with reasons; the buyer is not automatically bound to carry rejected goods back.

## 15.10 Unpaid seller

The textbook has rights against goods and personal rights against the buyer. India's ss 45–54 expressly regulate lien, stoppage in transit and resale; ss 55–56 concern price and damages. Explain their conditions **as Indian comparison**: possession supports lien in the stated cases; stoppage concerns a qualifying insolvent buyer while goods remain in transit; resale is a separate conditional power. Non-payment alone does not establish all three.

In the Indian comparison, **s 47 lien** requires the seller's possession and the applicable case: sale without a credit stipulation, expired credit, or buyer insolvency. **Sections 50–52 stoppage** require an insolvent buyer and continuing transit, exercised by taking possession or effective notice under the Act; actual buyer/authorised-agent receipt normally ends transit. **Section 54 resale** distinguishes perishable goods, notice of intended resale followed by non-payment within reasonable time, and an expressly reserved resale right on default. Required notice affects the seller's damages claim and the buyer's right to resale profit. Retention/stoppage alone does not rescind the original sale. These are conditional Indian powers, not automatic Nepal entitlements.

For Nepal, read the agreed concurrent performance, general performance and breach chapters with s 561's compensation for non-acceptance/refusal to pay. Identify any lawful right to withhold or terminate from those rules and the facts. Do not assert a Nepal statutory stoppage/resale scheme under ss 545–562 that the chapter does not expressly set out. See [Unit 9](unit-09-performance.md) and [Unit 11](unit-11-breach-remedies.md).

## 15.11 Buyer's remedies

Use s 552 for different-quality acceptance/rejection and notified replacement/improvement. Use s 560 for quantity/description choices and rejection notice. Section 561 addresses non-delivery compensation using the relevant contract/market difference and loss under the general compensation chapter. Specific performance or cancellation requires the governing general provision and its conditions; it is not an automatic remedy for every ordinary market purchase. Avoid double compensation.

## 15.12 Void sale

Section 546 makes the stated sale of specific goods already destroyed/damaged before formation without the seller's knowledge void. This differs from later accidental loss, which requires the ownership/risk rules. General incapacity, unlawful object and consent defects must be classified under their own rules; do not call every voidable transaction void. Section 562 gives two years from accrual for chapter claims.

## Full 20-mark answers

These are full practice answers. Adapt the emphasis to the question; the paragraph headings help you organise a 35-minute answer.

??? example "Q1. Explain the rights of an unpaid seller, distinguishing Nepal and Indian rules. (20 marks)"
    !!! warning "Unverified"
        Nepal Civil Code provisions were checked against the Nepali government text through Jestha 2076. Complete later-amendment currency remains unverified. Foreign textbook rules below are comparisons, not automatically Nepal law.

    **Introduction**

    An unpaid seller is a seller whose price remains wholly or partly unpaid. The central problem is that the seller may have performed, or be ready to perform, while the buyer has not paid. The law must determine whether the seller can retain goods, stop delivery, sell again or claim money from the buyer. These are different remedies with different conditions. In Nepal, the starting points are the contract, the Civil Code's general performance and breach rules and the sale chapter, especially section 561. The Indian Sale of Goods Act provides a useful but separate textbook scheme.

    **First identify the transaction's stage**

    Before listing rights, establish whether ownership has passed, who possesses the goods, whether delivery has started, when payment is due and what the agreement permits. A seller still holding identified goods before a concurrent payment deadline occupies a different position from a seller who has delivered everything on agreed credit. Non-payment is important, but it does not automatically answer each property and remedy question. The student's task is to connect the proposed remedy to the transaction's actual stage and legal basis. Otherwise the same list may produce contradictory conclusions.

    Nepal section 545 includes immediate and future ownership transfer arrangements. Section 547 concerns the price and section 548 gives the applicable payment and transfer default. Sections 554 and 555 separately address ownership and risk. Possession is therefore not a complete substitute for ownership, and an outstanding price does not necessarily mean ownership has not passed. The terms and statutory rules must be read together. For instance, a lawful credit sale can postpone payment without necessarily postponing every consequence of the sale. The answer should state what the parties actually agreed.

    **Nepal's performance and compensation approach**

    Nepal's sale chapter does not reproduce the Indian Act's complete named list of unpaid-seller rights against goods. A Nepal answer should therefore examine the lawful agreement and general rules concerning reciprocal performance, non-performance, termination and damages. Where payment and delivery are concurrent, the conditions for demanding or withholding performance matter. A contract may also contain a lawful cancellation or security arrangement. None of this permits the student to invent an unrestricted power to seize goods already delivered or to sell them without the necessary legal basis.

    Section 561 addresses compensation where the buyer refuses acceptance or payment and where the seller fails to deliver. Its contract and market-price comparison must be read with the general compensation provisions. The claim should identify the breach, the relevant price difference and recoverable loss. A loss calculation cannot simply assume that the full agreed price, the goods and a second sale's entire proceeds all belong to the seller. Amounts recovered and the effect of any substitute transaction must be accounted for so that the claimant is compensated rather than paid twice.

    **Indian right of lien as comparison**

    The Indian Sale of Goods Act sections 45 to 54 expressly address rights against goods. Under section 47, lien concerns retaining possession where there is no credit stipulation, credit has expired or the buyer is insolvent. Its practical feature is that the seller still has the goods. It is not the same as taking goods back from every person who later holds them. A right dependent on possession can be lost in the circumstances set out by the Indian Act. The student should name the jurisdiction and avoid presenting this entire scheme as Nepal sections 545 to 562.

    **Stoppage in transit as comparison**

    Stoppage in transit is a distinct Indian statutory protection concerning an insolvent buyer while the goods remain in transit. The seller seeks to stop their onward delivery through the carrier or other relevant custodian. The questions include whether the buyer is insolvent, whether transit has begun and whether it has ended under the applicable rule. Mere non-payment and the existence of a transport vehicle do not establish every condition. Once the buyer has lawfully received the goods, the same transit-based explanation cannot simply be repeated without further analysis.

    This comparison illustrates why stage matters. A seller who personally retains goods considers possession-based protection. A seller whose goods are moving through a carrier considers the applicable transit protection. A seller who has already delivered to the buyer normally needs to examine personal claims and any separately established security or recovery right. These are analytical categories, not an assertion that Nepal has adopted every Indian provision. In a Nepal problem, identify an actual Nepal statutory or contractual basis before claiming a special right to order a carrier to stop delivery.

    **Resale and personal claims**

    Section 54 resale distinguishes perishable goods, notice of intended resale followed by non-payment within reasonable time, and an expressly reserved resale right exercised on default. Required notice affects damages and the buyer’s right to resale profit. A right to retain or stop goods does not automatically mean a right to sell them in any manner, at any time and keep every rupee. The law's conditions concerning the goods, notice and the particular default must be examined. The distinction is important because resale affects ownership and can generate new rights in a later buyer. It is unsafe to treat it as a purely private accounting decision between the first seller and buyer.

    Personal claims against the buyer include the Indian Act's price and damages provisions in sections 55 and 56. In Nepal the contractual obligation and section 561 must be considered with the general remedies. A claim for an unpaid price and a claim for damages from refusal to accept are not always identical. The answer should explain what performance was due and what remedy fits it. Section 562 supplies a two-year cause-of-action period for the Nepal chapter; limitation should be tied to accrual rather than assumed to run from an arbitrary date.

    **Conclusion**

    The unpaid seller problem is best solved by separating the goods, the price and the breach. Identify possession, ownership, transit, credit terms and default before selecting a remedy. India's lien, stoppage and resale scheme is important for textbook comparison, but Nepal's answer must remain based on the Civil Code and lawful terms. A strong conclusion states the particular right available on the proved facts, explains any conditions or accounting and avoids automatic seizure, unrestricted resale and duplicate recovery. This approach answers the examination question fully while preserving the distinction between comparative learning and Nepal law.

??? example "Q2. Discuss implied safeguards, ownership, risk and remedies in sale of goods. (20 marks)"
    !!! warning "Unverified"
        Nepal Civil Code provisions were checked against the Nepali government text through Jestha 2076. Complete later-amendment currency remains unverified. Foreign textbook rules below are comparisons, not automatically Nepal law.

    **Introduction**

    A sale promises more than physical delivery of an object. The buyer expects goods matching the bargain, a seller entitled to transfer them and the quality or suitability required by law and agreement. The seller expects the price and proper acceptance. Nepal's Civil Code sale chapter, sections 545 to 562, deals with these connected questions. A complete answer should first identify the promised goods, then consider conformity and title, then ownership and risk, and finally the remedy for defective or missing performance. These questions should not be reduced to the single slogan that the buyer must beware.

    **Description and the seller's entitlement**

    Section 549 addresses correspondence with description and sample. If both form the bargain, meeting one does not excuse failure to meet the other. A buyer ordering a described grade of goods by reference to a sample should not be forced to accept a different described product merely because its appearance resembles the sample. The answer must identify what was actually promised. This is different from a disappointed personal preference that never formed part of the bargain. The terms, descriptions and relevant communications supply the factual starting point.

    Section 550 concerns the seller's right to sell and the stated freedom from competing possession or claims, subject to the arrangement. A seller cannot ordinarily supply a better title merely by possessing the object. The textbook expression nemo dat quod non habet explains this starting concern. However, any exception must have an identified legal basis. India's sections 27 to 30 contain particular exceptions concerning matters such as authorised commercial dealings and certain possession situations. Their existence does not establish that Nepal has adopted an identical exhaustive list. Authority and entitlement must be checked under Nepal law.

    **Quality, fitness and disclosed defects**

    Section 551 deals with satisfactory quality and purpose-related fitness within its wording. If the buyer communicates a relevant purpose and the statutory conditions are met, the seller's performance cannot be assessed only by whether some goods were delivered. Equally, the contract's quality specifications and identified defects matter. The section contains qualifications and does not justify inventing an additional particular quality assurance without its basis. The answer should state the required quality, the relevant purpose, any disclosure and the actual deficiency before concluding that the seller has breached an obligation.

    The buyer-beware idea is therefore qualified. It encourages attention to needs and inspection, but does not abolish the description, title, quality, sample or free-consent rules. A seller who knowingly deceives the buyer cannot answer the problem merely by saying that inspection was possible. On the other hand, a buyer cannot ignore an expressly identified defect and assume every expectation is guaranteed. The legal result depends on the governing rule and facts. The student should explain these limits rather than present either total seller responsibility or total buyer responsibility as a universal principle.

    **Sample and different-quality delivery**

    Section 553 addresses sale by sample, including quality correspondence and reasonable opportunity to compare the bulk. Its precise defect and inspection wording should be handled cautiously because the available English translation does not reliably support a verbatim Indian formula. The checked broad point is that sample sale creates defined safeguards, not merely a chance to look at a display item. The answer should avoid supplying an invented correction to disputed statutory wording. Where exact interpretation is essential, the gap should be identified rather than concealed behind a familiar textbook sentence.

    Section 552 gives choices where delivered quality differs from the contract. The buyer may accept the whole, reject the whole or accept part and reject the rest under the section. The provision also addresses notification for replacement or improvement and the seller's corresponding performance. This is a Nepal statutory remedy and should be explained directly. A textbook distinction between condition and warranty may aid comparison, but India's classification and consequences in sections 12 to 17 should not displace Nepal's actual remedies for the facts presented.

    **Ownership and risk**

    Section 554 first looks to the parties' agreement and intention shown by the terms, conduct and circumstances. It also contains default rules concerning particular deliverable goods and further work needed for price determination. Ownership is not always identical to physical delivery or completion of payment. Because the actor and notification wording in subsection (3) is problematic in the available material, a precise disputed application requires a reliable interpretation rather than silent substitution of the Indian rule. An exam answer can still state the governing intention principle and identify the factual issue clearly.

    Section 555 addresses risk, including contractual variation, fault-caused delay and the seller's undertaken destination delivery. Suppose goods are damaged while delivery is delayed. The question is not answered merely by pointing to the truck or warehouse where they happened to be located. Establish ownership under the governing rule, then examine any agreed risk allocation and the statutory qualification concerning the person responsible for delay. This sequence prevents the common error of treating possession as a complete answer to every accidental-loss problem.

    **Inspection, acceptance and remedies**

    Sections 556 and 557 concern reasonable inspection and acceptance through the stated receipt or conduct. Section 558 addresses delivery time. Section 560 gives specific choices for short quantity, excess quantity and mixed description, and does not assume instalments where none were agreed. A buyer who rejects must give prompt notice with reasons, but is not automatically required to transport rejected goods back. It is important to distinguish rejection from acceptance followed by a compensation claim. The facts concerning notice, retention and ownership-like conduct help decide which analysis applies.

    Section 561 addresses compensation for non-delivery and the buyer's refusal to accept or pay, read with the general compensation chapter. Section 546 separately concerns specific goods already lost or damaged before formation without the seller's knowledge; later loss is a different risk question. Section 562 supplies the chapter's two-year cause-of-action period. In conclusion, sale law coordinates conformity, title, ownership, risk and remedies. A strong answer applies the Nepal safeguards directly, labels foreign comparisons and avoids using either caveat emptor or a condition-and-warranty slogan as a substitute for the actual statutory analysis.

## Test yourself

??? question "Does future transfer fall outside s 545?"
    No. The chapter includes immediate and future, conditional and unconditional sale arrangements.

??? question "Is possession always ownership?"
    No. Use s 554's agreement and intention rules; delivery can occur at another time.

??? question "Does s 552 concern quantity?"
    Its Nepali heading and substance concern different quality; quantity choices appear in s 560.

??? question "Does buyer beware eliminate the seller's quality duties?"
    No. Apply ss 549–553 and relevant consent rules.

??? question "Can every unpaid Nepal seller invoke India's s 50?"
    No. India's stoppage provision is comparative; establish a Nepal legal basis.

??? question "Which loss rule concerns goods already destroyed before formation?"
    Section 546 in its stated specific-goods and knowledge setting; later loss needs risk analysis.

## One-minute revision

- Identify goods, price and promised transfer.
- Description, title, quality and sample are distinct safeguards.
- Ownership, possession, payment and risk need not coincide.
- Use s 552 for quality and s 560 for quantity choices.
- Label Indian unpaid-seller and title exceptions clearly.
- Never silently repair a doubtful translation.
