# Unit 10: Termination of contract

!!! abstract "In one line"
    Termination ends the duty to continue performance on a recognised ground. It does not necessarily erase payment, compensation or accounting for what happened before the ending.

!!! warning "Unverified"
    Nepal provisions were checked against the Nepali government Code through Jestha 2076. Complete later-amendment currency remains unverified. Textbook modes must be connected to the actual Nepal rule, not treated as automatic universal releases.

## Why it matters for the exam

- 2080 Q2 asks termination or discharge and its modes.
- The prescribed Bhairab Neupane case appeared in 2078 Q1 and concerns expiry, nonrenewal and disputed rent. Its complete brief belongs to the later case phase.
- Learn a reason for ending, its conditions, notice if required, and its financial consequences.

## 10.1 Meaning of termination

Discharge concerns obligations which no longer need performance. Termination often refers to ending continuing duties. Avoidance, expiry, fulfilment and cancellation for breach are different routes with potentially different consequences. A fully paid completed sale and an unfinished repair wrongfully cancelled are not identical legal situations.

Section 527 lists circumstances where performance is not required. Section 533 governs agreed alteration and substitution; 531 governs specified impossibility; 535 governs breach and cancellation. Use the correct route rather than merely writing “the contract is over.”

## 10.2 Modes of termination

| Mode | Nepal source and conditions | What to distinguish |
|---|---|---|
| Performance | 521 and the actual agreed duties | Completion of one side may leave payment outstanding |
| Waiver or release | 527(क) | Identify who released which duty; do not assume every related right was abandoned |
| Agreed alteration, suspension or substitution | 533 | Suspension is a pause; a replacement agreement can discharge the old obligation in the terms stated |
| Avoidance of a voidable contract | 518, 527(ख) | Apply the judicial avoidance framework; do not equate it with every unilateral regret |
| Delay after additional-time notice | 525 | Later performance possible, reasonable notice and subsequent noncompliance |
| Failure of essential time | 526 | The statutory purpose and intention test permits immediate cancellation |
| Material breach | 535 | Cancellation by notice in the stated circumstances, not automatic extinction for every minor defect |
| Fundamental change making performance impossible | 531 | Apply its examples, exclusions and accounting rules |
| Expiry or agreed ending event | Actual terms, interpreted under 515; 513 where contingent | Whether work, rent or payment continued needs evidence |

### Agreement and substitution

Under 533 the parties can alter work, extend time, suspend duties, substitute an act or replace the contract. The resulting agreement operates according to the section. The old obligation need not be borne except where the new agreement preserves it. A debtor cannot create novation merely by telling the creditor to seek payment from a friend.

### Breach and impossibility

Breach may justify remedies and, where the conditions apply, cancellation. Later impossibility under 531 is different: actual prevention by a qualifying fundamental change matters, while difficulty, reduced profit and its other listed exclusions do not automatically discharge performance. See [Unit 6](unit-06-legality.md).

### Death, legal change and limitation

Death does not automatically end every contract. Section 528 provides succession subject to inherited property and a personal-skill exception. A personal-service obligation may also raise 531(2)(घ). Identify the actual obligation. Later illegality is addressed by 531(2)(क).

The passage of a claim-filing period is not automatically a universal rule that every debt or obligation ceases to exist. Sections 534 and 544 provide chapter-specific limitation rules. No general Nepal merger or insolvency-discharge rule is asserted here without the relevant special law.

!!! warning "Unverified"
    Detailed textbook “operation of law” lists, including merger and insolvency, require separate Nepal statutory checks. They must not be recited as automatic Code rules for every contract.

### Effects after termination

Section 535(3) releases future performance following the stated cancellation, but other remedies may remain. Section 538 addresses benefits received and accounting, 539 proportionate completed-work payment, and 537 damages. Section 531 has its own accounting consequences. Choose the applicable route and avoid claiming the same sum twice.

## Nepal's law

Use 515, 518, 521, 525–528, 531, 533 and 535–539. DN 3775, [Bhairab Prasad Neupane v Chitwan Irrigation Project](https://nkp.gov.np/full_detail/2825), discusses expiry without renewal and proof of further rent or damage under historical law. Do not rewrite it as a decision applying the 2074 Code.

## Full 20-mark answers

??? example "Q1. Explain the modes and consequences of termination of contract in Nepal."
    !!! warning "Unverified"
        This answer uses the checked Nepal text through Jestha 2076. Complete later-amendment currency remains unverified.

    **Meaning and approach**

    Termination concerns the ending of continuing contractual duties on a recognised ground. Discharge is often used more widely for release from obligations, including their fulfilment. The expressions should not obscure the need to identify why performance no longer has to continue and what rights remain. Nepal's Civil Code supplies several routes through the performance and breach chapters. A completed transaction, an agreed replacement, a cancelled contract and a contract made impossible by a qualifying event do not necessarily have identical consequences. The correct answer connects each mode with its conditions and considers the work, money and rights already arising before the ending occurred.

    Section 527 identifies circumstances in which contractual performance is unnecessary, but it should be read with the provisions governing the particular ground. Section 533 deals with agreed changes and substitution, section 531 with fundamental change and section 535 with breach. Timing failures have further treatment in sections 525 and 526. This organisation matters because a party cannot choose whichever label produces the most convenient result. A demand for release must rest on the legal route supported by the agreement and facts. Identifying that route also helps determine whether notice, consent or another condition is required before continuing obligations cease.

    **Performance and agreed release**

    The ordinary way of bringing a duty to an end is to fulfil it. Section 521 requires contractual obligations to be performed. If the carpenter supplies the agreed desks and the buyer pays the agreed price, the completed obligations no longer await performance. Completion on one side alone may leave the other side's payment duty outstanding. That is why a statement that the contract has been performed needs care about which duty is meant. Defects, retained obligations and particular terms may raise further questions. The legal effect follows the actual commitments rather than a general assumption that physical delivery always settles everything.

    Section 527(क) recognises release from performance by the person to whom it is owed. The scope of the release matters. Waiving one instalment or accepting a specified adjustment is not necessarily abandonment of every associated right. The evidence should show who released whom and from what. A debtor's own assertion that the creditor should no longer insist on payment is not equivalent to the creditor's waiver. This mode reflects voluntary control of the relevant entitlement within the law. It must remain distinct from a unilateral decision not to perform an obligation which the other party has not agreed to release.

    **Alteration, suspension and substitution**

    Section 533 permits parties to alter work, extend time, suspend performance, substitute an act or replace the contract. These are different practical choices. A pause does not necessarily abolish the agreement permanently. A replacement contract can bring the original obligation to an end except where the new arrangement preserves it. The terms must therefore be read carefully. Suppose a creditor agrees with the necessary parties to replace one debtor with another. That agreed substitution differs from the original debtor simply directing the creditor to seek payment from a friend who never became an accepted replacement under the transaction.

    Variation also needs a distinction between changing a duty and excusing failure after it has already occurred. An agreement extending a deadline may affect future performance, but what happens to an accrued claim depends on the new terms and applicable law. The answer should not assume that every alteration erases all earlier consequences. Section 533 provides the statutory framework, while interpretation identifies the scope of the parties' actual decision. This preserves both freedom to reorganise a transaction and security against a party unilaterally rewriting the agreed obligations whenever a more convenient performance arrangement later becomes available to them.

    **Delay, essential time and breach**

    Section 525 addresses delay where performance remains possible after the original time. It provides a route involving reasonable additional-time notice and cancellation if the stated performance does not follow. Section 526 deals with a different situation in which timing is essential to the contract's purpose under its nature or the parties' intention. Failure then permits immediate cancellation under the provision. These distinctions prevent every missed date from being treated alike. Delivery required for a single event may differ from a delay which leaves the purpose achievable. The particular timing condition and statutory route must be established before asserting cancellation.

    Section 535 addresses failure to fulfil contractual obligations, notice of refusal and conduct demonstrating inability to perform. Its material-failure provisions allow cancellation by notice in the stated circumstances, after which the cancelling party need not continue performance under subsection (3). A minor defect should not simply be assumed to extinguish every duty automatically. Nor should cancellation be confused with an award of damages. The legal ground, required communication and surviving remedies need separate treatment. Where several persons form one side, section 536's rule about the cancellation authority also matters rather than assuming any individual can always cancel only their convenient share.

    **Impossibility and consequences**

    Section 531 addresses fundamental changes making performance impossible. Its qualifying circumstances and exclusions must both be stated. Mere difficulty, reduced profitability and the other listed excluded situations do not automatically discharge performance. Death likewise does not end every contract: section 528 recognises succession with an inherited-property limit and personal-skill exception. A particular personal service may raise a different result under the relevant provision. Expiry of the agreed period can also matter, but continued transactions and accrued payment require evidence. A copied list of operation-of-law headings cannot replace the actual Nepal statutory basis for each claimed release.

    Ending future performance does not necessarily erase past benefits or loss. Section 538 provides accounting and return rules in its circumstances, section 539 concerns proportionate completed-work payment and section 537 governs damages. Section 531 has its own financial consequences. In conclusion, termination must be analysed through ground, conditions and effects. The answer should identify the obligation, apply the correct provision, explain any notice or agreement and account for what has already happened. This avoids both forcing performance after a lawful release and allowing termination language to become an unsupported escape from payment, restitution or compensation which the applicable law still requires.

??? example "Q2. Distinguish termination by agreement, breach and impossibility, and explain surviving rights."

    **Different reasons for the same practical ending**

    An unfinished contract may stop because the parties agree to stop, because one party commits a breach justifying cancellation or because a fundamental change makes performance impossible. The practical result can look similar, but the legal grounds differ. Nepal's Civil Code addresses agreed changes in section 533, breach in section 535 and later impossibility in section 531. The distinction affects consent, notice, responsibility and financial consequences. A correct analysis should therefore begin with the reason performance stopped, rather than assume that every unfinished transaction is a wrongful breach or that every ending automatically wipes out whatever payment or loss arose beforehand.

    Consider a contract to repair a shop. The parties may jointly decide that a different building will be repaired instead. Alternatively, the contractor may refuse the promised work despite remaining able to perform. A third possibility is destruction of an essential identified subject in circumstances satisfying the impossibility provision. Each needs its own legal route. A party should not be allowed to conceal a voluntary refusal by loosely describing it as changed circumstances. Equally, genuine agreed substitution should not be treated as if one side necessarily committed a wrong simply because the original project was not completed in its original form.

    **Agreement as a source of change**

    Section 533 allows alteration, extension, suspension, substituted performance and a new contract replacing the original. These choices depend on agreement, not the wish of one person alone. The resulting arrangement governs according to the provision, with the original obligation no longer borne except as preserved in the new contract. The scope matters. A temporary suspension may postpone work while retaining the relationship. A replacement may define a substantially new duty. Clear terms should establish what changed, what remained and how earlier payments are treated, instead of leaving those questions to assumptions after one party later becomes dissatisfied with the new arrangement.

    Section 527 also recognises waiver of performance. That is distinct from a debtor's unilateral withdrawal and from a third person's promise made without the required consent to substitution. For example, a contractor cannot simply announce that another firm is now solely liable and insist that the customer release the original commitment. Section 529 requires the other party's consent for transfer of obligations. A genuine agreed replacement under section 533 provides a different mechanism. This distinction protects the creditor's or customer's choice about whom to trust while allowing the parties to make a lawful new arrangement if they actually agree to do so.

    **Breach and the cancellation route**

    Section 535 recognises breach through failure, communicated refusal or conduct demonstrating inability. Its material-failure rule allows the other party to cancel by notice in the stated circumstances. A breach can therefore provide a legal basis for ending future performance without a new mutual agreement to stop. However, the conditions must be established. A party should identify the obligation, the failure and the applicable cancellation ground, rather than treat every imperfection as automatic discharge. The distinction is important where the other party disputes both the alleged defect and the suggestion that it affected the essential performance promised by the agreement.

    Timing failures also require attention to sections 525 and 526. The additional-time notice route differs from immediate cancellation where time is essential in the statutory sense. These provisions help assess whether an alleged delay justified the ending chosen. The answer should connect the notice or timing requirement with the facts. Cancellation under section 535 releases the cancelling party from continuing performance under subsection (3), but that does not itself quantify damages. Responsibility for loss and the value of completed work are further questions. Ending the duty and calculating the monetary result are connected, but legally distinct, parts of the analysis.

    **Fundamental change and impossibility**

    Section 531 concerns a fundamental change from the circumstances of formation which makes performance impossible. Later illegality, specified uncontrollable events, loss of an essential subject and the stated personal-service incapacity situations illustrate the rule. It requires actual prevention connected with the obligation. The existence of an earthquake somewhere does not establish that this particular repair became impossible. The parties' agreement and the actual event must be examined. This makes the inquiry different from merely showing that performance would now cost more or that one party no longer sees the bargain as financially worthwhile in the changed commercial conditions.

    Subsection (3) expressly excludes mere difficulty, reduced profit or loss and other stated circumstances from the relevant fundamental-change category. In its setting, subsection (4) permits negotiation over terms, which is different from automatic discharge. The law therefore preserves contractual responsibility for ordinary risks while recognising defined serious changes. Death likewise requires the personal-performance and succession provisions rather than a blanket release rule. These distinctions are practical safeguards against relabelling an ordinary default as impossibility. A person relying on the section must establish the required legal facts, not simply invoke force majeure or hardship as if those words resolved the entire dispute.

    **Payments, work and surviving claims**

    Section 538 addresses return and accounting for benefits in its cancellation or invalidity settings, while section 539 allows proportionate contractual payment for completed work in its circumstances. Section 537 separately addresses loss caused by breach. Under section 531, payments and work done before qualifying change have their own stated treatment. The answer must identify which route applies. A paid advance, an unpaid completed stage and an alleged lost future profit are not the same item. Each should be linked with its legal basis and evidence, without assuming either that termination makes all money recoverable or that it makes no money recoverable at all.

    In conclusion, agreement changes obligations through consent, breach can justify cancellation through the statutory failure and notice rules, and impossibility operates through its defined fundamental-change requirements. Surviving rights follow the applicable provision and the facts already established. An orderly answer separates the ending ground from financial consequences, respects accrued duties and avoids duplicate recovery for the same disadvantage. This gives termination its proper role in contract law: it regulates when continuing performance may stop, while leaving the law to determine payment, restoration and compensation through the distinct conditions governing those claims rather than through an unsupported universal rule.

## Test yourself

??? question "Does completing delivery necessarily end the buyer's unpaid price obligation?"
    No. Identify the separate obligations and whether each was fulfilled.

??? question "Can a debtor create novation alone?"
    No. The required agreement to substitution must be established.

??? question "Does suspension always mean permanent ending?"
    No. It may pause duties under the agreed terms.

??? question "Does death end every contract?"
    No. Apply 528 and the relevant personal-service rules.

??? question "Does cancellation automatically eliminate all accounting for benefits?"
    No. Consider 538, 539 and the relevant ending provision.

    That conclusion has not been established. Apply the relevant limitation provision to the claim.

## One-minute revision

- State the ground, conditions and effect.
- Completion and release are different routes.
- Section 533 governs agreed changes and substitution.
- Sections 525–526 distinguish timing failures.
- Sections 531 and 535 address different ending grounds.
- Termination can leave payment, accounting and damages issues alive.
