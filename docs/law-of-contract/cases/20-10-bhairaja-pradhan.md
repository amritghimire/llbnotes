## Facts

The prosecution alleged that the defendants played the card game called marriage with money stakes at Bhairaja Pradhan's premises. The defendants disputed gambling and the alleged seizure circumstances. The government appealed after the Appellate Court acquitted them using reasoning that excluded marriage from gambling.

The Supreme Court separated the legal question about the game's possible character from the evidential question whether these defendants had been proved to stake property. Witnesses did not substantiate the alleged seizure and the prosecution failed to establish whose money was staked and the relevant circumstances (paras 45–47).

## Issues

1. Can marriage played with property stakes and the stated chance elements fall within Gambling Act s 2?
2. Was the charged conduct proved against these defendants?

## Arguments

**Government:** the game could satisfy the statutory definition and the acquittal reasoning was wrong.

**Defendants:** they disputed gambling and the prosecution's account of money/cards being seized from an actual game. The court assessed proof rather than treating possession of money or the game's name as conclusive.

## Decision

The Supreme Court rejected the categorical view that staked marriage could not be gambling, so altered the Appellate Court reasoning to that extent. **The defendants nevertheless remained acquitted** because the charged money-stakes conduct was not proved (paras 46–47).

## Ratio decidendi

Our English paraphrase of the official headnote and reasons, not an official translation:

1. Historical s 2 required property win/loss, chance and staking. Marriage played with the relevant money/property stakes could satisfy that definition (para 45).
2. Legal possibility does not prove a particular criminal charge. The seizure and witness evidence did not establish these defendants' alleged staking conduct (para 46).
3. The proper acquittal rested on evidential failure, not blanket legal immunity for the named card game (para 47). This was a criminal prosecution, not enforcement of a civil wagering promise.

## Why it matters

Remember the two conclusions together: a staked game can fall within the definition, yet the accused must be acquitted when the particular charge is not proved. This avoids both “marriage is always lawful” and “everyone playing marriage is automatically guilty.”
