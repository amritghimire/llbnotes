## Facts

Bhairab rented his Bharatpur house to the irrigation project under a two-year agreement dated 2036/12/4 at Rs 2,100 monthly. The court found an extension to 2040/3/30 and no later extension. Rent had been paid through 2040/8/12. Bhairab nevertheless claimed later rent, furniture/electrical damage and utility charges.

The project said it had left and did not owe the claimed later amounts. The final reasons relied on the notice sent on 2040/7/13, service at the landlord's address, payment vouchers and evidence of departure. The Supreme Court found no proof of the alleged later occupation or the claimed damage (para 10).

## Issues

1. Was rent due for the claimed later period without established renewal or continued occupation?
2. Were the claimed furniture, electrical and utility amounts proved?

## Arguments

**Landlord:** occupation and liability continued; he sought the pleaded later rent and associated loss/charges. The permission-to-appeal discussion noted utility evidence that required examination.

**Project:** rent was settled through departure, notice had been given and the later claims were not owed. The final judgment accepted the relevant notice, payment and evidential position; permission to appeal itself was not a final finding for the landlord.

## Decision

The Supreme Court upheld dismissal of the landlord's rent and compensation claims. The appeal failed. The final result rested on expiry/nonrenewal, notice and departure, paid rent and absence of proof of continuing occupation or the claimed loss (para 10).

## Ratio decidendi

Our English paraphrase of the official headnote and reasons, not an official translation:

1. The agreement had expired and no further renewal or new agreement was established (para 10).
2. On the evidence, the project gave advance notice, vacated and paid rent through the relevant date. The landlord did not prove the asserted later occupation (para 10).
3. The alleged furniture entrustment/damage and electrical loss lacked proof; the later utility position did not establish the asserted liability (para 10). The case is not permission to occupy rent-free simply because a paper term has expired.

## Why it matters

This priority case teaches **expiry plus evidence**, not expiry alone. Learn the final finding date and distinguish it from dates recited in the pleadings and lower-court account. The judgment's decision year is 2045; its NKP reporting year is 2046.

### Then and now

| Then, as discussed in the judgment | Present comparison and limit |
|---|---|
| Historical expiry, extension, notice, departure and payment | Compare Code ss 610–623 for lease structure, but s 622 expressly applies Part 4 chapter 9 to house rent. Do not treat a general lease rule alone as the complete modern house-rent law. |
| Failure to prove later occupation and specific loss | A present claimant must establish the appropriate breach, recoverable loss and applicable legal basis. Expiry alone does not authorise free continued occupation. |

    **Introduction and citation**

    Bhairab Prasad Neupane v Chitwan Irrigation Project officials and others, D.N. 3775, NKP 2046 issue 3, concerns a landlord's claim for rent and related losses after a government project left a rented house. The Supreme Court decided it on 2045/12/22 through Justices Mahesh Ram Bhakta Mathema and Prachanda Raj Anil, with Justice Mathema writing. The difference between decision year 2045 and publication year 2046 is normal reporting chronology. The case is important for expiry, renewal, notice and proof. It should not be reduced to the claim that every obligation disappears immediately when a written period ends.

    **Material facts**

    Bhairab provided his house in Bharatpur to the irrigation project under an agreement dated 2036/12/4. The original term was two years and the monthly rent was two thousand one hundred rupees. The final reasons found an extension up to 2040/3/30, but no established extension after that date. Rent had nevertheless been paid through 2040/8/12. Bhairab claimed rent for a later period together with furniture, electrical and utility amounts. Thus the litigation required more than proof that the project had once been a tenant. It required proof of the later liability actually claimed.

    The project denied using the house after the relevant departure and denied owing the additional sums. The courts below rejected the landlord's claim. During the permission-to-appeal stage, utility information was treated as a reason for examining the evidence further. That procedural step should not be confused with the Supreme Court's final conclusion. Permission to appeal allows a question to be considered; it is not itself a judgment that the claimant has proved every alleged fact. The final determination appears in paragraph 10 and must control the account of the result.

    **Issues and competing positions**

    The main issues were whether the agreement or subsequent conduct supported rent for the later period, whether departure and payment had been established, and whether the claimed damage and charges were proved. Bhairab maintained that occupation and responsibility continued. The project relied on the end of the arrangement, notice, actual vacation and payment. An examination answer should present those as competing cases before stating the court's findings. Otherwise a pleading that the project remained in occupation could accidentally be repeated as though the court had accepted it.

    **Expiry and the absence of renewal**

    The court examined the original undertaking and its extension. It found no further renewal or new arrangement after the extension identified in the final reasons. This undermined the attempt to treat the old undertaking as proof of an indefinitely continuing rent obligation. However, the court did not stop with the paper term. It also asked whether the project remained in occupation and whether the sums claimed were otherwise supported. This combination matters because the commercial and legal problem is different if a tenant actually remains and uses property after a fixed term has ended.

    A sound explanation therefore links expiry to the actual later circumstances. The landlord could not recover the asserted additional rent merely by showing the old agreement. Equally, a tenant cannot use this case as an automatic defence while retaining the premises indefinitely. The court's reasoning included notice and departure, not a general entitlement to free occupation. This limited statement is both more accurate and more useful than a broad slogan. It explains why the result followed on these facts without pretending that the case resolves every possible holding-over arrangement.

    **Notice, departure and payment evidence**

    Paragraph 10 relies on a notice sent on 2040/7/13 and the record showing service at the landlord's address. The court treated the project as having given advance notice before leaving. It also considered the correspondence and vouchers concerning payment through 2040/8/12 and the later utility position. The final reasons found no sufficient proof from the landlord that the project continued to occupy for the period claimed. Dates in earlier allegations and the lower-court summary should not be silently substituted for this final account of what was established.

    This part of the reasoning shows why a contract answer may need evidence as well as legal classification. A rent claim must connect the asserted amount to the relevant period and basis of occupation. Payment vouchers can answer an allegation of non-payment, while a notice and departure evidence can answer a claim of continued use. The legal conclusion depends on which facts are proved. The student should identify the documents and the purpose for which the court used them, rather than merely list dates without explaining their relationship to the liability in dispute.

    **Furniture, electrical items and utilities**

    The court separately considered the associated loss claims. Bhairab had not established which furniture was entrusted in the claimed manner or what electrical parts the project damaged. A demand for compensation does not prove damage, responsibility or value. The utility correspondence likewise did not establish the asserted later liability on the court's findings. These were distinct items requiring their own support. The refusal of those amounts should not be described as a general rule that tenants can never owe damage or utilities. The problem was the proof and basis of these particular claims.

    **Decision and present comparison**

    The Supreme Court upheld the lower result rejecting the rent and compensation claims, and Bhairab's appeal failed. The ratio is that an expired, unrenewed undertaking did not establish the claimed continuing liability where departure, notice and payment were supported and continued occupation or specific loss was not proved. For current study, the Civil Code's lease chapter helps organise possession, rent and return questions, but section 622 expressly refers house-rent matters to Part 4 chapter 9. A modern house-rent dispute therefore cannot be decided solely by attaching this old case to a general lease section.

    The significance is practical: identify the agreed term, any extension, notice, actual use, payment and evidence for each separate loss. The case also teaches the difference between allegations, permission-to-appeal observations and final findings. A strong examination answer gives the historical citation, material facts, issues and actual dismissal before commenting on the limited principle. It preserves accrued obligations that are legally established while rejecting the unsupported claim that an old tenancy paper proves all future rent and damage. That is the proper connection between termination doctrine and the court's evidential reasoning.
