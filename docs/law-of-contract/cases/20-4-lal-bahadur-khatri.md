## Facts

Jaya Lal Subedi paid Lal Bahadur Khatri Rs 100,000 under an advance writing dated 2054/5/23 concerning land held by Lal Bahadur and his daughter Nirmala Mahat. The balance was to be paid when the transfer was completed. Nirmala's presence, consent or approval for transferring her land was not established.

When the transfer did not occur, the payer sought the principal and interest. Both parties challenged the Appellate Court's result: Lal Bahadur objected to repayment, and Jaya Lal sought interest. The Supreme Court examined the writing's enforceability, its classification and the actual interest wording (paras 15–16).

## Issues

1. Could the writing bind the absent landholder without her consent?
2. Was it the claimed enforceable contract or the asserted loan instrument?
3. Were principal and interest both recoverable under the historical provisions?

## Arguments

**Lal Bahadur:** the advance writing should be treated as contractual and the repayment result should not stand on the payer's transaction-law route.

**Jaya Lal:** the money was received and the writing's enhanced-payment term supported his claimed interest. The court distinguished this argument from an express interest provision.

## Decision

The Appellate Court's order returning **Rs 100,000 principal only** was upheld. Both appeals failed. Interest was refused under the historical rule because the writing did not state interest; the court did not order transfer of the daughter's property (paras 15–16).

## Ratio decidendi

Our English paraphrase of the official headnote and reasons, not an official translation:

1. An enforceable contract must be legally capable of implementation. A promise concerning another person's land requires the relevant person's consent or authorised instrument; that was absent here (para 15).
2. The court distinguished a cited advance-contract precedent because its facts did not cure Nirmala's absent consent. It did not announce that all advance writings have identical effect (para 15).
3. The admitted advance was recoverable within the historical No. 40 period. The writing was not the asserted No. 2 loan instrument, and No. 6 did not supply interest where none was written (paras 15–16).

## Why it matters

A parent is not automatically authorised to transfer a child's property merely by calling it a family transaction. The judgment does not establish Nirmala's age, so do not invent a minor-capacity holding. Its particular lesson is missing consent/entitlement and careful classification of the money claim.

### Then and now

| Then, as discussed in the judgment | Present comparison and limit |
|---|---|
| Missing consent to transfer another person's land | Compare Code s 505 consent and legally enforceable obligation, with the relevant ownership/representation rules. Capacity, consent and ownership are separate questions. |
| Historical principal-only recovery and No. 6 interest wording | Current interest and restitution must have their own statutory/contractual basis. Do not import this result as a universal modern no-interest rule. |
