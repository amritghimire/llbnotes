## Facts

Five relatives held interests in a shared house/property. Under a writing dated 2044/4/21, Dhanbhai Maharjan was to acquire the others' interests. Of the Rs 80,000 payable for those interests, Rs 40,000 was paid and the remaining Rs 40,000 was to accompany the registered transfer. The arrangement fixed duties, and the court found voluntary signatures and agreement.

Dhanbhai and one of the other contracting parties, Sahila, died. Dhanbhai's heirs sought performance and deposited the unpaid balance. Nani Chhori, Sahila's wife, resisted, disputing contractual character, succession and limitation. The claimants relied on refusal on 2055/8/1 and sued on 2055/8/23 (paras 20–25).

## Issues

1. Did the document create contractual obligations despite its label?
2. Did the nonpersonal rights and duties pass to heirs?
3. Did the agreement itself need registration, and what event started limitation?

## Arguments

**Nani Chhori:** the paper was not a legally effective contract; the original persons had died; the claim was late, including on the proposed six-month registration calculation.

**Claimants:** the signed arrangement defined binding obligations, its performance did not depend on personal skill, and the suit followed the identified refusal within the applicable period. They tendered the remaining payment through deposit.

## Decision

The Supreme Court upheld the order requiring transfer registration under the agreement. The heirs' rights/duties survived and the suit was within Contract Act 2023 s 18(2)(ग)'s historical three-month period from the relevant refusal (paras 21–25).

## Ratio decidendi

Our English paraphrase of the official headnote and reasons, not an official translation:

1. Substance and agreed duties determine contractual character; absence of the word “contract” does not decide it (paras 20–21).
2. Under historical s 10(2), rights and duties passed to heirs except those dependent on personal qualification. The transfer duty here was nonpersonal (para 22).
3. This agreement contemplated a later separately registered transfer; it did not itself transfer title or require registration of the agreement in the manner argued (para 23).
4. Refusal to perform the requested transfer supplied the cause for suit in this arrangement. The claim was timely and performance remained due (paras 23–25).

## Why it matters

This is a priority case for **survival of nonpersonal contractual duties**. It also teaches the distinction between signing a promise to make a transfer and completing the later legal instrument that transfers title. Read the full set of holdings, not only the sentence about heirs.

### Then and now

| Then, as discussed in the judgment | Present comparison and limit |
|---|---|
| Contract Act 2023 s 10(2), nonpersonal succession | Code s 528 addresses succession with its own inherited-property and personal-performance qualifications. Do not infer unlimited personal liability of every relative. |
| Agreement versus later transfer registration | Code s 505 preserves mandatory formalities. Do not read this case as removing every present registration requirement. |
| Historical s 18(2)(ग), three months from the identified cause | Present limitation depends on the applicable provision and accrual. The old three-month figure is not a current universal rule. |

    **Introduction and citation**

    Nani Chhori Maharjan v Rahindra Maharjan and others, D.N. 7610, NKP 2062 issue 10, concerns the continuing effect of an agreement after original parties died. The Supreme Court decided it on 2062/10/4 through Justices Anup Raj Sharma and Rajendra Prasad Koirala, with Justice Sharma writing. The prescribed syllabus uses Rabindra, while the public text reads Rahindra. The decision number is the safest identifying locator. Its importance is not a general claim that relatives always answer another person's promises. It concerns an established contract whose performance did not depend on the deceased persons' individual skill.

    **Material facts**

    The dispute arose from a shared house and property in which five relatives had interests. They made a written arrangement on 2044/4/21 under which Dhanbhai Maharjan would acquire the other interests. The amount payable for those interests was eighty thousand rupees. Forty thousand was paid, and the remaining forty thousand would be paid with the registered transfer. The court examined the writing and found that the parties had voluntarily agreed to specified obligations. Thus the case involved an identified arrangement about existing rights, rather than a vague family expectation that someone might give property later.

    Dhanbhai and Sahila, another party, subsequently died. Dhanbhai's heirs sought the promised transfer and deposited the unpaid balance. Sahila's wife Nani Chhori resisted enforcement. The issues therefore extended beyond the original payment: the court had to determine whether a contract existed, whether its rights and obligations survived, whether registration arguments defeated it and whether the action was timely. These questions should be stated separately. An answer dealing only with death would omit the formation and limitation reasoning on which the actual order also depended.

    **Arguments and issues**

    Nani Chhori argued that the document was not the asserted enforceable contract and that work not completed during the original parties' lives should not be imposed on wives or children. She also challenged limitation, including the proposition that time should follow the suggested six-month registration calculation. The claimants relied on the agreement's terms, the nonpersonal nature of performance and their deposit of the balance. They identified refusal on 2055/8/1 and filed on 2055/8/23. Their position was that the agreed transfer remained due and the suit followed the relevant cause within the governing period.

    **Contractual character**

    In paragraphs 20 and 21 the court considered the substance of the writing. Whether a paper is a contract does not depend solely on whether its heading uses that word. The relevant parties had rights in the property, voluntarily signed and defined what each side would do. Contract Act 2023 section 2(क) supplied the historical framework. The court found contractual obligations, and the claimants had not failed to meet the payment structure: part was paid and the remaining amount was deposited. The defendants could not avoid the promised performance merely through the label attached to the document.

    This reasoning should be distinguished from a rule that every signed family paper is enforceable. The judgment examined actual rights, assent and defined obligations. It did not eliminate legal form or recognise every uncertain promise. The facts are also important when comparing other prescribed advance cases, where the court classified the instrument differently or found missing consent. A good answer therefore explains why this document operated as an agreement instead of assuming that a similar topic, such as land or family property, necessarily produces the same result in every case.

    **Succession to nonpersonal duties**

    Paragraph 22 applies Contract Act 2023 section 10(2). The historical provision transferred rights and duties to heirs on death or the stated incapacity, except rights acquired or obligations to be performed through personal qualification. The duty here was to complete the agreed property transfer and was not dependent on unique personal skill. The heirs therefore succeeded to the relevant contractual position. Nani Chhori's argument that the wife or children could never be required to complete the arrangement failed. The exception for personal qualification remained part of the rule and should not be omitted.

    **Registration and cause of action**

    Paragraph 23 distinguishes the agreement from the later instrument transferring title. This writing required a separate transfer to be registered when requested; it did not itself accomplish the title transfer. The court rejected the suggested reasoning that the agreement necessarily had to be registered within six months and that limitation automatically began at that point. The actual obligation and agreed sequence mattered. This is a distinction between the promise to execute a legal transfer and the legal act that completes it, not a general rejection of property-registration requirements.

    The court then considered refusal and timing. The claimants identified 2055/8/1 as the refusal to complete the transfer without an additional demand, and filed on 2055/8/23. Historical section 18(2)(ग) provided the relevant three-month period from the cause of suit. The defendants did not establish an earlier cause defeating that timing. Paragraphs 24 and 25 therefore treated the action as timely. Limitation analysis requires both the governing period and its triggering event. Merely pointing to the much older agreement date would not answer the court's actual reasoning.

    **Decision, present comparison and significance**

    The Supreme Court upheld the order for the defendants to execute the registered transfer according to the agreement. The appeal failed. The ratio combines substance-based contractual classification, succession to nonpersonal rights and duties, separation of agreement from transfer formalities and accrual on the relevant refusal. All four parts help explain why performance was ordered. It would be incomplete to say only that the heirs lost or that the old paper was valid without explaining the legal questions connecting the facts to the result.

    For present study, Civil Code section 528 addresses succession with its own inherited-property and personal-performance qualifications, while section 505 preserves mandatory formalities. Those are comparisons, not sections applied in this older case. The historical three-month period must not be supplied as a current universal limitation rule. The case remains useful because it teaches careful reading of the obligation: what was promised, who succeeded to it, what form was still needed and when refusal created a claim. That is the sound basis for an examination comment on its significance.
