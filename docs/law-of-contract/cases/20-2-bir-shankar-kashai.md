## Facts

Anita Lama paid Bir Shankar Kasai Rs 60,000 as an advance toward a proposed registered land transfer under a writing dated 2057/6/13. The transfer was not completed. Anita sought the claimed Rs 120,000 relief under the writing and Contract Act 2056; Bir Shankar disputed liability and relied on the legal character of the land transaction.

The District Court allowed return of Rs 60,000, without the additional compensation sought. Both sides appealed and the Appellate Court upheld that result. Bir Shankar then challenged the repayment obligation before the Supreme Court. His receipt of the advance was admitted (paras 2–10, 14).

## Issues

1. Could the claimed land-transfer obligation be enforced under the historical sale-of-goods provisions relied on?
2. Could the recipient nevertheless keep the admitted advance?

## Arguments

**Bir Shankar:** land was outside the Act's movable-goods definition, so the contract claim should fail; he objected to the repayment order as well.

**Anita:** the advance was received and the proposed transfer was not completed; return of the money should stand. Her earlier appeal sought the additional compensation, which the lower courts had refused.

## Decision

The Supreme Court upheld repayment of **Rs 60,000** and dismissed the defendant's appeal (para 14). It did not award the additional claimed amount or order the land transferred under the cited sale-of-goods framework.

## Ratio decidendi

Our English paraphrase of the official headnote and reasons, not an official translation:

1. Contract Act 2056 s 40's goods definition concerned movable property; land was outside that definition. The particular land-transfer claim could not be enforced by treating it as that goods transaction (para 14).
2. That conclusion did not entitle the defendant to retain money he admitted receiving as the advance. Repayment of the advance stood (para 14).
3. Distinguish recovery of the admitted payment from enforcement of the proposed transfer and from additional compensation. The three are not the same remedy.

## Why it matters

The case teaches **classification and remedies**. Failure of a particular contract-enforcement argument does not automatically justify keeping the other person's money. It is unsafe to cite this case as an unrestricted current rule against all land contracts.

### Then and now

| Then, as discussed in the judgment | Present comparison and limit |
|---|---|
| Historical Contract Act s 40 movable-goods category | Code s 545 also defines goods for its sale chapter as movable property with stated exclusions; land requires the relevant property rules. |
| The court's result on this advance and pleaded statutory route | Do not conclude that every modern agreement concerning land is void, or that every advance always earns double compensation. Check Code ss 504–505, formalities and the particular restitution/remedy basis. |
