# Unit 5: Free consent

!!! abstract "In one line"
    A signature is not the end of the inquiry. The Code protects a person whose agreement was obtained by unlawful pressure, abuse of influence or misleading conduct.

## 5.1 Consent and free consent

Consent means agreement to the same proposed commitment. Free consent asks whether that agreement was improperly obtained. Section 504's matching assent and section 505's binding consent must be read with sections 517–518. Two people signing the same page may still face a question about threats, domination or deception.

An adult freely accepting a commercially poor price is not automatically a victim of a consent defect. Identify the actual conduct, its connection with agreement and the statutory category. Avoid assuming that regret, poverty or a family relationship alone proves invalidity.

## 5.2 Factors affecting free consent

### 5.2.1 Coercion: meaning, consequences and exceptions

Section 518(2)(क) addresses coercion. Its explanation includes holding or threatening to hold another's property, threats against body, life or reputation, and other unlawful acts or threats intended to force an unwanted contract.

Example: a person threatens unlawfully to retain a trader's essential stock unless the trader signs a disadvantageous supply agreement. Examine the threat, unlawfulness and purpose. Ordinary negotiation or a genuine exercise of a lawful right is not automatically coercion. This is a distinction in applying the definition, not a licence to threaten prohibited conduct.

The injured party may seek judicial avoidance under section 518. Do not describe the agreement as automatically void from the beginning. No separately enumerated Nepal “exceptions to coercion” list was verified. The statutory elements, not a copied textbook exception, control.

### 5.2.2 Undue influence: meaning, presumption, consequences and comparison

Section 518(2)(ख) concerns influence used over someone whose will can be dominated to gain an unfair advantage. Its explanation identifies protected relationships and conditions, including guardianship or custody, inability to protect one's interests through age or illness, and economic or positional pressure.

Section 518(3)(ख) places the burden on the allegedly dominant party to establish absence of undue influence where the stated relationship and domination circumstances apply. First establish those triggering facts. Do not say that all agreements with relatives are presumed invalid.

Example: a dependent sick adult is induced by a person controlling their affairs to transfer a valuable right for that person's benefit. Ask about domination and unfair advantage. The remedy is the voidable-contract route. Coercion focuses on the threatening conduct; undue influence focuses on abuse of influence and dependent choice.

### 5.2.3 Misrepresentation: meaning, presumption and consequences

Section 518(2)(घ) includes a false factual account without reasonable basis, misleading a party to their detriment, inducing a mistake about the subject, and leading someone to believe they contract on one subject while obtaining agreement on another.

Example: a supplier gives an unsupported false specification which causes the buyer to agree. Establish what was said and how it affected the transaction. Section 518 does not state a general presumption that every inaccurate statement automatically proves a voidable contract. The express shifted burden concerns undue influence, not every misrepresentation allegation. The injured party may seek the relief permitted by 518.

### 5.2.4 Fraud: meaning, elements, consequences and comparison

Section 518(2)(ग) concerns intentional deception by a party or representative. It includes knowingly presenting falsehood as truth, conduct designed to create false belief, knowing concealment of a fact, and other conduct legally treated as fraudulent.

Ask who acted, what fact or conduct misled, whether the person knew and intended deception, and how it procured the agreement. A deliberate falsified inspection report differs from an honestly mistaken description. **Nepal's misrepresentation definition is broader than merely an innocent statement**, so do not reduce the whole comparison to “fraud dishonest, misrepresentation always innocent.”

Both are grounds of judicial avoidance, but their elements differ. Section 518(3)(क) lets the affected party ask to be put in their pre-contract position without seeking avoidance. Subsection (4) keeps the contract operative until avoided. Under (5), court avoidance does not affect acts already done under the contract. Subsection (6) protects the lawful interests of innocent third parties. Do not promise automatic punitive damages or a guaranteed criminal conviction. Those need separate legal bases.

### 5.2.5 Mistake: meaning, types and consequences

A mistake is an erroneous belief. Textbooks classify mistakes as factual or legal and unilateral or bilateral. For Nepal, section 517(2)(ठ) expressly addresses both parties' mistake about an essential fact at the time of contract. That arrangement is void. If one party deliberately induces another's mistake, section 518 may instead supply the relevant deception ground.

Example: both parties bargain over an identified thing that, unknown to both, has already ceased to exist. Check the essential fact, mutual error and initial-impossibility provisions. A mistake about future profit is not automatically a mutual essential-fact mistake. No general Nepal rule invalidating every unilateral error was verified.

## Full 20-mark answers

??? example "Q1. Explain free consent and the factors affecting it under Nepal's Civil Code."
    !!! warning "Unverified"
        This answer uses the checked Nepal text through Jestha 2076. Later amendments remain incompletely verified.

    Contract law respects agreement because it ordinarily expresses a person's chosen commitment. That justification weakens when the apparent agreement results from unlawful pressure, abused influence or deception. Free consent therefore concerns the quality of the decision, not simply the presence of a signature. Nepal's Civil Code connects this inquiry with sections 504 and 505 on agreement and binding consent, section 518 on voidable contracts and section 517 on certain grounds of invalidity. An accurate answer identifies each defect and its consequence. It does not treat every unpleasant bargain or later regret as proof that no valid choice was possible.

    **Consent and the structure of protection**

    Section 504 concerns assent to a proposal in the meaning in which it was made. Section 505 requires consent expressing a willingness to be bound. These provisions establish the agreement to which the validity rules apply. A person may appear to accept the same terms as the other party, yet have accepted because of an improper threat. That creates a different question from a case in which the parties never agreed on the same subject. The first may concern defective consent, while the second may concern whether agreement was formed at all. The distinction should remain visible throughout the analysis.

    Section 518 identifies coercion, undue influence, fraud and misrepresentation as grounds on which the injured party may obtain judicial avoidance. The arrangement is therefore voidable under the checked text. It remains operative until avoided, rather than being automatically invalid from its beginning. Section 517 separately addresses mutual mistake about an essential fact at formation. The different categories show that the Code does not attach one universal consequence to everything commonly called a consent problem. The student must use the category fitting the facts and then explain its particular legal effect.

    **Coercion**

    Coercion under section 518(2)(क) involves the statutory kinds of pressure intended to secure agreement against a person's will. The explanation includes detention or threatened detention of property, threats to body, life or reputation and other unlawful acts or threats. Suppose a trader is told that essential stock will unlawfully be withheld unless a disadvantageous agreement is signed. The threat and its connection with obtaining the agreement are central. The law is concerned with the improper process by which apparent assent was obtained, not merely with whether the resulting price was commercially attractive or unattractive.

    Ordinary bargaining pressure must be distinguished from coercion. A supplier's refusal to offer a discount is not automatically an unlawful threat. Nor does every warning about a genuine legal remedy establish the statutory ground. The relevant conduct, its legality and its purpose must be examined. At the same time, a powerful person cannot turn prohibited pressure into lawful bargaining merely by using polite words. The injured party may seek avoidance under section 518 when its conditions are established. A list of unsupported textbook exceptions is less useful than careful application of the actual statutory definition to the evidence.

    **Undue influence**

    Undue influence under section 518(2)(ख) addresses unfair advantage obtained through a position enabling one person to dominate another's choice. Its explanation identifies circumstances including guardianship or custody, vulnerability through age or illness and economic or positional pressure. A person controlling a dependent patient's affairs may induce a transfer for personal benefit without making an explicit violent threat. That possible abuse differs from ordinary persuasion between independent adults. The inquiry concerns the relationship, the power exercised and the unfair advantage sought. Merely showing that the parties are related does not complete the legal analysis.

    Section 518(3)(ख) places an evidential burden on the allegedly dominant party in the stated circumstances to show that the contract was not produced by undue influence. The triggering relationship and domination facts must first be established. This protects a vulnerable party where the other side is better placed to explain the transaction. It does not mean that every agreement with an older or poorer person is presumed invalid. The purpose is to address abuse of influence rather than outlaw all assistance, dependence or advice. The resulting contract is subject to the statutory avoidance route when the ground is made out.

    **Fraud and misrepresentation**

    Fraud under section 518(2)(ग) involves intentional deception by a party or representative. The explanation includes knowingly presenting falsehood as truth, conduct creating a false belief, knowing concealment and other conduct treated as fraudulent by law. A supplier who deliberately fabricates a safety report to secure an order provides an illustration. The answer should identify the actor, the misleading fact or conduct, knowledge and deceptive purpose, and the connection with the agreement. A general allegation that the other party behaved unfairly is not a substitute for establishing those features on the particular facts.

    Misrepresentation under section 518(2)(घ) covers the described misleading acts, including unsupported false factual accounts, misleading a party to their detriment, causing error about the subject and obtaining one agreement while creating belief in another. It should not be reduced to the proposition that it is always innocent. Nepal's description is wider. Both fraud and misrepresentation can support avoidance, but proof of intentional deceit is especially important to the fraud category. The student's task is to explain the difference without assuming that every inaccurate statement is fraudulent or that every misleading transaction necessarily involves the same mental element.

    **Mistake and legal consequences**

    Textbooks discuss mistakes of fact or law, made by one or both parties. Nepal's section 517(2)(ठ) specifically concerns both parties' mistake about an essential fact when contracting. That arrangement is void. An example is a bargain concerning an identified thing whose relevant existence has already ended, unknown to both parties. The essentiality and mutuality must be examined. An incorrect estimate of future profit is not automatically the same problem. Where one party induces the other's mistake by deception, section 518 may provide the appropriate route instead of a general claim that all mistakes invalidate contracts.

    Section 518's further provisions address restoration-related relief, the contract's operation before avoidance, acts already done and innocent third-party interests. Section 520 supplies different limitation rules for different validity categories, including the checked one-year period for a voidable-contract claim from the relevant cause arising. In conclusion, free consent protects the integrity of voluntary agreement through specific legal grounds. A strong answer connects each ground with an example, proof and consequence. It preserves the distinction between an agreement void from the start and one the injured party may have set aside, while recognising that the governing version of the law must be checked.

??? example "Q2. Distinguish coercion from undue influence and explain their consequences."
    !!! warning "Unverified"
        This answer uses the checked Nepal text through Jestha 2076. Later amendments remain incompletely verified.

    Coercion and undue influence both concern apparent agreement obtained through an improper process. They are not interchangeable expressions for a weak bargain. Nepal's Civil Code section 518 treats each as a ground for judicial avoidance, but describes different kinds of conduct and proof. Coercion centres on the specified threatening or unlawful pressure intended to force agreement. Undue influence centres on abuse of a position allowing one person to dominate another's choice for unfair advantage. The comparison is important because a correct conclusion requires facts fitting the particular ground, rather than a general feeling that one party had more power than the other.

    **Coercion and the threatening act**

    Section 518(2)(क) explains coercion through conduct such as detaining or threatening to detain property, threats to body, life or reputation, and other unlawful acts or threats intended to make a person contract against their will. The definition asks what was done or threatened and why. Suppose a person unlawfully holds a shopkeeper's essential equipment until the shopkeeper agrees to a disadvantageous supply arrangement. The pressure targets the shopkeeper's ability to refuse. The fact that the shopkeeper eventually signs does not remove the need to examine the way the apparent agreement was secured.

    The focus is not confined to physical force. Property pressure and the other statutory threats may matter. At the same time, every difficult commercial situation is not coercion. A trader may urgently need goods without the supplier having committed or threatened an unlawful act. A genuine demand for lawful performance must also be distinguished from prohibited pressure. The exact conduct and its connection with the decision are therefore essential. An examination answer should neither narrow coercion to violence alone nor expand it into a rule setting aside every contract entered under economic urgency or disappointment.

    **Undue influence and the relationship**

    Section 518(2)(ख) deals with influence over a person whose will can be dominated, used to obtain an unfair advantage. The explanation identifies circumstances such as guardianship or custody, vulnerability through age or illness and economic or positional pressure. The law recognises that a person may lose a meaningful independent choice without an explicit threat. For example, someone controlling a sick dependent adult's financial affairs may induce a transfer for the controller's benefit. The relationship and the use made of it require examination. Mere affection, advice or assistance is not automatically abuse.

    This ground therefore draws attention to a different set of facts. Who relied on whom? Who could control the decision or essential resources? What advantage was obtained, and why was it unfair in that setting? An independent adviser explaining a transaction is not in the same position as someone exploiting dependence for personal gain. The Code's examples help locate the risk but do not eliminate the need for analysis. A family relationship alone does not prove domination, and an unequal price alone does not prove that the relationship was actually used to procure the agreement in the legally objectionable way.

    **Difference in method of pressure**

    Coercion commonly operates through a stated or demonstrated threat: agree or face the prohibited consequence. Undue influence can work through trust, dependence and control, making the weaker person's decision follow the stronger person's will. These are explanatory contrasts, not rigid boxes into which all facts must neatly fit. A single transaction may contain both threats and domination. The answer should explain any overlap and identify the statutory elements supported by the evidence. It should not choose a label merely because one party is an employer, relative, guardian or lender.

    Consider two examples. In the first, a stranger threatens unlawful harm unless a business owner signs a contract. A long-standing relationship of confidence need not be shown to explain the coercive act. In the second, a person managing an elderly adult's affairs arranges an unfair personal benefit through that dependent relationship, without an express threat. Undue influence becomes the more immediate inquiry. The examples demonstrate why the grounds are distinct. They also show why neither ground is established simply by proving that the victim would have preferred better financial terms or later regretted the decision.

    **Difference in proof**

    For coercion, the answer needs evidence of the relevant threat or unlawful act, its purpose and its connection with the agreement. The ordinary fact-finding task cannot be replaced with an assumption that all severe bargaining pressure is unlawful. Messages, conduct and surrounding circumstances may help establish what happened. The unit does not claim that a particular type of document is invariably sufficient. Rather, the evidence must support the statutory elements. This keeps the focus on a legally recognisable defect in the process of consent rather than a general complaint about hardship.

    Undue influence has an express burden provision in section 518(3)(ख). Where the stated relationship and domination circumstances apply, the party denying improper influence must establish that the contract was not caused by it. The student should explain the triggering facts before discussing the shifted burden. Otherwise, the rule can be misstated as a presumption against every transaction involving unequal resources or a personal relationship. The provision responds to the difficulty of proving what occurred within a relationship of control. It does not abolish factual inquiry or make all dependent persons incapable of receiving advice and entering lawful transactions.

    **Shared consequence and its limits**

    Both grounds fall within the voidable-contract framework. Section 518 allows the injured party to seek judicial avoidance. Under the checked subsection (4), the agreement remains enforceable until avoided. That differs from a void arrangement under section 517, invalid from its beginning. The distinction matters to the sequence of events and the way relief is described. A person challenging the contract should not assume that a private declaration of invalidity answers every legal question. The statutory process and consequences must be identified, including the relevant limitation category under section 520.

    Section 518 also addresses restoration-related relief, completed acts and innocent third-party interests. Avoidance therefore does not mean that every later legal interest disappears automatically or that every prior act can simply be ignored. Additional claims require their own basis. In conclusion, coercion and undue influence share a concern for genuine choice but attack different defects. The best comparison explains threat versus abused dominance, the evidence required, the special undue-influence burden and the shared voidable result. It then applies those distinctions to the facts, rather than relying on an oversimplified contrast between physical pressure and mental pressure alone.

??? example "Q3. Explain fraud, misrepresentation and mistake, including their legal consequences."
    !!! warning "Unverified"

    Fraud, misrepresentation and mistake all involve a problem in the understanding on which an agreement is made, but their legal structures differ. Fraud concerns intentional deception. Misrepresentation concerns misleading conduct within the applicable statutory description. Mistake concerns an erroneous belief, which may be shared or confined to one party. Nepal's Civil Code places fraud and misrepresentation within section 518's voidable-contract framework, while section 517 expressly addresses both parties' mistake about an essential fact at formation. The answer must preserve those differences. A single statement that an agreement based on error is always void does not accurately express the Code.

    **Fraud and deliberate deception**

    Section 518(2)(ग) addresses deceptive conduct by a party or representative. Its explanation includes knowingly presenting falsehood as truth, conduct likely to create the relevant false belief, knowing concealment of a fact and other conduct legally regarded as fraudulent. The mental element distinguishes fraud from an ordinary error. Suppose a supplier knows that a machine has failed a material inspection but fabricates a report showing that it passed. If that deception procures the buyer's agreement, the facts raise the statutory fraud issue. The legal analysis should identify both the false impression and the supplier's deliberate role in creating it.

    A useful method is to ask who acted, what fact or conduct was misleading, what the actor knew and intended, and how the agreement was obtained through that deception. These questions organise the evidence without inventing a quotation or a separate test from an unchecked case. An accusation that the other side is dishonest is insufficient on its own. The stated conduct must be connected with the contracting process. Likewise, failure to perform later does not automatically prove that every earlier statement was fraudulent. A breach and fraud can raise different factual and legal issues even within the same commercial dispute.

    **Misrepresentation and its breadth in Nepal**

    Section 518(2)(घ) describes several misleading situations. These include presenting a false factual account without reasonable basis, misleading a party to their detriment, causing error about the subject and creating belief in one agreement while obtaining another. The description should be explained in its own terms. A supplier who makes an unsupported false claim about the capacity of a pump may raise a misrepresentation issue even where deliberate fabrication has not been established. The account, its basis, its effect and its connection with assent matter. A genuine statement of opinion also needs careful factual examination before being treated as a false factual representation.

    It is tempting to define misrepresentation only as innocent falsehood and fraud only as dishonest falsehood. That contrast can introduce the topic, but it is too narrow as a complete account of Nepal's wording. The statutory misrepresentation category includes a range of misleading conduct. There is no general rule in section 518 presuming that every inaccurate statement automatically satisfies it. Nor should the shifted burden expressly attached to undue influence be extended without authority to every allegation of misrepresentation. The legal ground must be established through the particular facts and the applicable statutory provision.

    **Comparison with the Indian textbook treatment**

    India's Contract Act sections 17 to 19 distinguish fraud, misrepresentation and their consequences in their own terms. Section 17 contains a particular explanation about silence, while section 19 contains an ordinary-diligence qualification in the circumstances it states. Those details must not be imported as though they were automatically part of Nepal's section 518. For Nepal, knowing concealment is expressly relevant to the fraud description. Whether a particular omission falls within the provision depends on its facts. The foreign source explains the comparison, while the Code supplies the Nepal conclusion.

    This comparative discipline is especially important when discussing consequences. Fraud may support several kinds of claim in different legal systems, but a contract answer should not automatically promise punitive damages, criminal conviction or unlimited compensation. Section 518 establishes the checked route of avoidance and its accompanying rules. A different remedy or offence needs its own source and elements. This is not a denial that other consequences may exist. It is a refusal to treat one established contract ground as proof of every possible civil and criminal result the injured person might seek after discovering the deception.

    **Mistake and essential mutual error**

    Textbooks classify mistakes as factual or legal and unilateral or bilateral. These categories help organise the problem, but the statutory consequence must still be identified. Section 517(2)(ठ) concerns both parties being mistaken about an essential fact when the contract is made. The shared error must concern something essential, rather than a minor detail or a later disappointed forecast. Suppose both parties bargain about an identified item whose relevant existence has already ended, unknown to either. The example raises essential mutual mistake and possibly initial impossibility, requiring the relevant Code provisions to be considered together rather than one label applied mechanically.

    A wrong estimate of future profit is different. Contracting commonly involves uncertainty about future demand and cost. Calling an unsuccessful prediction a mistake does not automatically bring it within section 517's essential-fact rule. A unilateral error also requires its own analysis. These notes do not claim a verified Nepal rule invalidating every one-sided mistake. If one person deliberately caused the other's error, fraud or misrepresentation under section 518 may be the more appropriate route. The distinction follows the mechanism by which the false understanding arose and the statutory ground supported by the evidence.

    **Legal effects and conclusion**

    Under section 517, the specified void arrangement is invalid from the beginning. Under section 518, fraud and misrepresentation make the contract subject to judicial avoidance at the injured party's initiative. The checked text preserves its operation until avoidance and addresses restoration-related relief, prior acts and innocent third-party interests. Section 520 distinguishes limitation categories, including the one-year period for voidable-contract proceedings from the relevant cause for avoidance. These features prevent a simple equation between all factual errors and one legal result. The timing and character of the defect change the analysis and the available route of challenge.

    In conclusion, fraud requires attention to intentional deceit, misrepresentation to the statutory kinds of misleading conduct, and mistake to the nature and distribution of the erroneous belief. The Nepal consequence must then be connected with section 517 or 518 as appropriate. A good answer uses ordinary examples, identifies proof and avoids foreign-rule shortcuts. This protects contractual choice without turning every broken promise, bad estimate or inaccurate remark into an automatically void agreement. It also recognises the limits of the checked source version and the need for separate authority before asserting additional remedies or criminal consequences.

## One-minute revision

- Identify agreement first, then its consent defect.
- Coercion concerns statutory threats or unlawful pressure.
- Undue influence concerns abused dominance and has a specific burden provision.
- Fraud concerns intentional deception; Nepal misrepresentation is not limited to innocent error.
- Four section 518 grounds are voidable, not automatically void.
- Essential mutual factual mistake belongs under 517.
- Explain relief and limitation without guaranteeing every remedy.
