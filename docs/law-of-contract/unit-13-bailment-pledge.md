# Unit 13: Bailment and pledge

!!! abstract "In one line"
    Bailment gives another person custody for a purpose; pledge makes property security. Neither ordinarily gives the recipient unrestricted ownership.

!!! warning "Unverified"

## 13.1 Bailment

### 13.1.1 Meaning

Under s 575, a person delivers goods to another to return them, sell them or deal with them as directed after the agreed purpose. A bicycle left for repair is the simplest example. The repairer receives possession; the customer retains ownership. The section's goods include movable property and relevant rights, excluding currency. It requires **writing when the goods' value exceeds Rs 25,000** in the checked version.

### 13.1.2 Features

There must be identifiable goods, delivery or legally sufficient change of possession, a purpose, and an obligation governing their return or disposal. Under s 576 receipt completes bailment; goods already held by the recipient can become subject to bailment without pointless redelivery. Merely allowing a visitor to stand beside a bicycle does not transfer custody. A sale transfers ownership; a money loan generally requires repayment of equivalent money, rather than return of the same entrusted object.

### 13.1.3 Types

Textbooks classify bailment as gratuitous or for reward, and as benefiting the bailor, bailee or both. Storage without charge, lending a tool without charge, and paid repair illustrate these categories. Nepal's actual duties come from ss 575–583 and the lawful contract. Do not infer a different negligence standard merely from a foreign category.

### 13.1.4 Rights and duties of bailor and bailee

The bailor must disclose known defects and special precautions (s 577). The section also places liability for damage caused by the goods' defect in its stated circumstances even where the bailor lacked knowledge; do not replace it with India's gratuitous-bailment formula. The bailor generally bears relevant expenses unless otherwise agreed (s 582), and bears the bailee's exposure where the bailor lacked entitlement (s 580).

The bailee must use agreed care, or reasonable care as for the bailee's own goods, and follow the authorised purpose (s 578). Subject to contrary terms, loss despite proper care is not automatically a duty to replace. Negligence, bad faith or breach changes the result. Unauthorised use creates liability. Mixing without consent is restricted: separable mixtures require the bailee to bear separation expense and loss; the rights in an inseparable unauthorised mixture belong to the bailor under the stated rule. With consent, proportional rights and profits apply.

Return is due when the time expires or purpose ends; profits or additions ordinarily accompany it (s 579). Examine responsibility for loss after return becomes due, including a bailor's refusal to receive. A repair bailee can retain the repaired goods for unpaid repair charges and, after the statutory reasonable period of non-payment, sell to recover under s 581. This is **not a universal sale power for every bailee**.

### 13.1.5 Termination

Expiry, completion and lawful termination bring the custody purpose to an end. Section 579 allows recall where the bailee wrongfully keeps goods. Return does not erase an accrued damages claim. Unlawful-purpose bailment is void under s 583; chapter claims have a two-year cause-of-action period under s 584. Always distinguish ending future custody from deciding past liability.

### 13.1.6 Finder of lost goods

A finder did not negotiate with the owner. The obligation is statutory: s 670 allows the finder to keep the goods safely after informing the police with their actual description, until the owner is found. These are conditions for retaining found goods; the section does not compel every passer-by to take possession. The owner must bear the relevant expense; the finder may retain until it is paid. The checked text provides ownership after three years without anyone claiming. Do not infer immediate ownership, an automatic reward, or India's finder-sale powers under Contract Act ss 168–169. See [Unit 7](unit-07-indirect-contract.md) for obligations without an ordinary bargain.

## 13.2 Pledge

### 13.2.1 Meaning

Section 585 concerns property given as security for a loan and property deposited to secure performance. Its definition includes property rights and title documents; it is broader than the English textbook's possessory pledge of movable goods. Use the Code's category when answering Nepal questions.

### 13.2.2 Features

Identify the secured debt or task, the security property, the provider's entitlement, and the agreed/default enforcement terms. The creditor's custody or security interest does not mean unrestricted ownership. A repair bailment's main purpose is repair; a pledge's purpose is security.

### 13.2.3 Rights and duties of pledgor and pledgee

After payment or completion, return is due under s 586; divisible security may support the statutory partial release. Section 587 provides lawful recovery through market-value sale or auction on default; the failed-sale ownership route remains subject to law. Account for proceeds, refund surplus and recover an established shortfall by lawful means. Redemption remains available until sale or the relevant ownership transfer on payment of the required debt, interest, other dues and delay-related amounts. A performance deposit can be applied to completion and the resulting costs as provided.

### 13.2.4 Pledge by a non-owner

Section 588 does not simply make an innocent creditor owner. An unaware recipient may demand equivalent substitute security owned by the provider and avoid the transaction if it cannot be supplied. The section denies the stated collateral rights where the earlier acquisition contract was already void or the recipient knew the provider lacked ownership; personal recovery against other assets remains distinct. A holder of limited rights can provide only that limited interest. Under s 589 multiple creditors ordinarily share proportionately unless the governing arrangement provides otherwise, with the stated restriction on a later contrary priority. Section 590 gives two years from accrual for chapter claims.

### 13.2.5 Bailment and pledge compared

Ordinary bailment serves custody, repair or authorised use; pledge serves security. Return follows completion of the custody purpose in bailment and discharge of the secured obligation in pledge. Enforcement powers therefore differ. The [comparison table](#compare) below sets out each distinction.

??? example "Q1. Explain bailment and the rights and duties of its parties. (20 marks)"
    !!! warning "Unverified"

    **Introduction**

    Bailment separates ownership from possession. A person may own a bicycle but give it to a mechanic for repair. During repair the mechanic has custody and must deal with the bicycle for the agreed purpose. The customer does not sell the bicycle merely by delivering it. This distinction explains why the law protects both the owner's interest in getting the goods back and the custodian's interest in receiving proper instructions, expenses and lawful charges. Nepal regulates this relationship in sections 575 to 584 of the Muluki Civil Code 2074.

    **Formation and identifying the parties**

    The bailor is the person entrusting the goods; the bailee is the person receiving them. Section 575 covers delivery for return, sale or disposal according to the owner's direction after the purpose is fulfilled. Identifying the particular goods and the purpose helps distinguish bailment from a sale or ordinary debt. A promise to repay equivalent currency does not, merely for that reason, constitute custody of the same goods. The statutory definition and exclusions must be applied before using the textbook label. General requirements of lawful contract also remain relevant.

    Section 576 makes receipt important. Delivery can involve an actual handover, but a person already holding the goods may agree to hold them under the bailment arrangement. The law does not require goods to travel out of a warehouse and back merely to demonstrate a new legal purpose. However, access alone is insufficient: a visitor allowed to inspect a vehicle does not necessarily receive custody. Section 575 also requires writing where the goods' value exceeds twenty-five thousand rupees in the checked text. That requirement should appear in a Nepal answer.

    **The bailor's disclosure duty**

    The person providing the goods is often best placed to know their dangers. Section 577 requires disclosure of known defects and precautions needed for safe handling. A bailor who knows a machine's switch sometimes fails must not conceal that information from the person asked to use or repair it. The section also addresses loss caused by defects in the goods even where the bailor did not know of the defect in the circumstances stated there. It is unsafe to substitute the Indian distinction concerning gratuitous bailment for Nepal's actual wording.

    **Care and authorised use**

    Section 578 places the main custody obligations on the bailee. The agreed care standard applies; otherwise the bailee must take reasonable care as for the bailee's own goods. This does not mean that a person habitually careless with personal property may treat entrusted property carelessly. The reference is to reasonable conduct. Subject to contrary terms, loss despite the required care does not automatically make the bailee an insurer. Negligence, bad faith or departure from the contract must be analysed separately rather than inferred merely because an accident occurred.

    Use must remain within the authorised purpose. Permission to store a motorcycle is not permission to ride it for personal travel. Unauthorised use can create liability under section 578. The issue is therefore not only whether the motorcycle was physically damaged through poor handling but also whether the bailee departed from the agreed purpose. A careful answer identifies the permitted activity, compares it with the actual conduct and connects the resulting loss to the statutory rule. This approach is clearer than saying that a bailee is liable for every possible loss.

    **Mixing, accounts and additions**

    Mixing entrusted goods with other goods can destroy identification and complicate return. Section 578 distinguishes consent and separability. Where mixing is authorised, the parties have the applicable proportional rights and profits. Where unauthorised mixing can be separated, the bailee bears separation expenses and the relevant loss. Where the unauthorised mixture cannot be separated, the Code assigns the stated rights in the goods to the bailor. An Indian compensation formula should not replace this rule. The facts must show whether there was permission and whether separation remains possible.

    Section 579 governs return when the time expires or the purpose is complete. Unless the contract provides otherwise, additions and profits belong with the returned goods. If an entrusted animal produces an addition during custody, the bailee cannot simply treat that addition as personal property. Responsibility after return becomes due also requires attention to the cause of delay. The bailor's refusal to receive the goods may matter. Thus the answer should distinguish the due date, an actual offer of return, the owner's response and any subsequent loss.

    **Expenses, entitlement and the repairer's protection**

    Bailment creates rights for the custodian as well. Section 582 places the relevant expenses on the bailor unless otherwise agreed. Section 580 addresses the consequences of a bailor's lack of entitlement, protecting the bailee against the stated third-party claims and expenditure. These duties prevent a person from obtaining custody services while leaving the innocent recipient exposed to undisclosed defects in title or uncompensated costs. The bailee should nevertheless identify the legal basis and amount of each claim rather than treating every expense as automatically recoverable.

    Section 581 specifically protects a repair bailee. It requires timely return and the applicable compensation, but permits retention for unpaid repair charges. After the statutory reasonable period of non-payment, the repairer may sell to recover as provided. This is a limited statutory protection connected to repair charges. It does not establish that every warehouse keeper, borrower or unpaid custodian has a universal power to sell anything held. In an examination problem, the service actually undertaken must be identified before a sale power is asserted.

    **Ending the relationship and conclusion**

    Completion, expiry and lawful termination end the custody purpose, while wrongful retention can justify recall under section 579. Section 583 makes unlawful-purpose bailment void, and section 584 provides two years from accrual for claims under the chapter. Ending custody does not erase damages already caused. Bailment therefore rests on a balanced allocation: the bailor supplies proper goods, information and expenses, while the bailee exercises authorised custody, care and return. A strong answer applies these reciprocal duties to facts and uses Nepal's own provisions rather than an undifferentiated list of foreign textbook rules.

??? example "Q2. Distinguish bailment from pledge and explain pledge enforcement. (20 marks)"
    !!! warning "Unverified"
        Nepal Civil Code provisions were checked against the Nepali government text through Jestha 2076. Complete later-amendment currency remains unverified. Foreign textbook rules below are comparisons, not automatically Nepal law.

    **Introduction**

    A person may hand over property for many reasons. Leaving a watch for repair creates a custody relationship for repair. Giving security to support repayment of a loan serves a different purpose. Both arrangements involve another person's property, but the legal questions differ. In the first, the central questions concern care, authorised use, repair charges and return. In the second, the central questions concern the secured obligation, the creditor's rights on default and the provider's right to recover the security. Nepal addresses bailment in sections 575 to 584 and security and deposit in sections 585 to 590.

    **Meaning and scope**

    Section 575 covers entrusting goods for return or dealing with them as directed after a purpose. The parties are bailor and bailee. Section 585 concerns property given as security for a loan or deposited to secure performance. The parties can be described as pledgor and pledgee in the security setting. Nepal's statutory property category includes relevant rights and title instruments. It should not be narrowed automatically to the English textbook idea of a possessory pledge of movable goods. The applicable Nepali section controls the legal classification, whatever shorthand the parties use.

    **Purpose and extent of authority**

    Purpose is the most useful distinction. A bailee ordinarily receives custody to store, repair, use or otherwise deal with the goods in an authorised manner. A pledgee receives security for debt or performance. A repairer cannot use a customer's vehicle for private journeys merely because it is in the workshop. Equally, a secured creditor cannot assume unlimited ownership merely because the debtor has delivered security. Each recipient must remain within the legal and agreed purpose. Possession, ownership and a security interest are related ideas, but they are not interchangeable.

    **Return and disposal**

    In ordinary bailment, the agreed time or completion of the purpose triggers return under section 579. Additions and profits ordinarily accompany the goods, subject to the agreement. In pledge, section 586 connects return to payment of the secured debt or completion of the secured performance. Where the property and obligation permit the statutory partial release, divisibility matters. The fact that some money has been repaid does not always mean the debtor can demand any selected item without examining the remaining security arrangement and the applicable rule.

    The power of disposal is also different. Custody alone gives no general right of sale. Section 581 supplies a particular repairer's protection for unpaid repair charges after the required reasonable period. It cannot be turned into a universal bailee's power. Section 587 specifically addresses enforcement of security after default. A student should state which legal basis authorises the proposed sale. Saying simply that both recipients may sell whenever unpaid overlooks the difference between an ordinary custody purpose and a security purpose and may produce the wrong result.

    **Enforcing security on default**

    Under section 587 the creditor must follow the lawful recovery process, including the relevant market-value sale or auction route. The existence of default does not itself justify immediate confiscation. Where the law permits an ownership-transfer route following an unsuccessful sale, its conditions and the governing law must still be met. A private statement that the creditor owns the property after one missed payment cannot replace those requirements. The examination answer should identify the default, the enforcement route, the value obtained and the resulting accounting.

    The creditor must apply proceeds to the secured obligation and the amounts lawfully recoverable. Any surplus belongs back to the person entitled to it. If a lawful sale produces less than the recoverable amount, section 587 addresses recovery of the shortfall through other property as provided. This prevents two opposite errors: treating the creditor as entitled to keep a windfall and treating the security's insufficiency as necessarily extinguishing every remaining debt. The calculation should show the debt, permissible charges, proceeds, surplus or deficit, and any separate recovery.

    **Redemption and performance deposits**

    Redemption is the provider's opportunity to discharge the obligation and recover the security before the relevant sale or ownership transfer. Section 587 requires the necessary debt, interest, other dues and additional delay-related liability to be met in its stated circumstances. The right therefore needs both a time limit and a payment explanation. It is inaccurate to say that redemption lasts forever after a completed lawful disposition. Conversely, it is inaccurate to say that a single default necessarily destroys it before the statutory enforcement event.

    A deposit securing a task requires attention to performance rather than only repayment. The Code permits application toward completing the task and meeting the relevant costs and shortfall under the statutory arrangement. The recipient should account for what was actually required and spent. A performance deposit is not automatically a penalty belonging absolutely to the recipient. Its purpose and the governing clause must be read with the Code. The distinction becomes important where partial work has already been completed or another person finishes the work at a different cost.

    **Defective title and competing creditors**

    Section 588 deals with security supplied by someone lacking full entitlement. An innocent recipient may have the stated right to demand equivalent substitute security owned by the provider and to avoid the arrangement if that cannot be supplied. It does not follow that innocence converts another person's property into the creditor's property. Where the prior acquisition contract was already void or the recipient knew of the lack of ownership, the section restricts the collateral rights. A limited-interest holder can supply only that limited interest, while personal recovery against other assets is a distinct issue.

    Section 589 also matters where more than one creditor claims against the security. The checked rule provides proportionate sharing unless the governing arrangement provides otherwise, with the stated restriction concerning a later contrary priority. Students should not assume a foreign priority rule without reading this provision. Section 590 gives two years from accrual for chapter claims. In conclusion, pledge is distinguished by its security purpose and conditional enforcement rights. Good analysis protects both lawful recovery and the provider's redemption and accounting rights, while ordinary bailment remains governed primarily by custody, care and return.

??? example "Q3. Discuss the bailee’s rights, termination and the finder of lost goods. (20 marks)"
    !!! warning "Unverified"
        Nepal Civil Code provisions were checked against the Nepali government text through Jestha 2076. Complete later-amendment currency remains unverified. Foreign textbook rules below are comparisons, not automatically Nepal law.

    **Introduction**

    Bailment is often taught as a list of the bailee's duties, but it also gives the bailee legal protection. The custodian may need information about dangerous goods, reimbursement of expenses and protection against the bailor's defective entitlement. The relationship must eventually end through return or another authorised disposal. A finder of lost goods raises a related custody problem without an ordinary negotiated contract. Nepal treats contractual bailment in sections 575 to 584 of the Civil Code and the finder in section 670. These sources should be kept distinct even though both require responsible handling of another person's property.

    **Why the bailee needs rights**

    The bailor normally retains the ownership interest, while the bailee undertakes a defined task. A person who receives machinery for repair may spend money, use specialised labour and face danger from hidden defects. If the law protected only the owner's right to return, custody could expose the bailee to unfair costs and undisclosed risks. Reciprocal duties make the relationship workable. They also discourage a bailor from entrusting property without authority and then leaving the recipient to deal alone with the true owner's claim. Each protection must nevertheless come from the contract or an identified statutory rule.

    **Disclosure and protection concerning entitlement**

    Section 577 requires the bailor to disclose known defects and necessary special precautions. If a chemical container needs special storage, the recipient must receive the information needed for safe custody. The provision also allocates liability for defect-related damage in its stated circumstances, including the rule not dependent on the bailor's knowledge. The question is therefore more precise than whether the bailor behaved kindly or carelessly. The answer must identify the defect, required disclosure, actual knowledge where relevant and the connection between the goods and the damage suffered.

    Section 580 addresses the bailor's lack of entitlement and the resulting claims or expenditure borne by the bailee. Suppose a person entrusts goods that another person lawfully claims. The bailee's custody agreement does not settle the true owner's rights. The section instead addresses the bailor's responsibility for the bailee's resulting exposure. An examination answer should avoid saying that the bailee becomes owner or can ignore every third-party claim. The safer analysis separates title to the property from the contractual responsibility owed by the person who supplied it.

    **Expenses and the repairer's retention**

    Under section 582 the relevant expenses fall on the bailor unless the arrangement provides otherwise. Parties may agree on a price including storage or ordinary expenses, so the contract must be read before adding separate charges. The bailee should establish that the expenditure relates to the custody purpose and is recoverable under the applicable arrangement. This prevents both unfair non-payment and an unsupported demand for every amount the custodian chooses to spend. Clear records of the task, instructions, dates and expenses assist the legal analysis.

    Section 581 gives a specific repair bailee a right to retain repaired goods for unpaid repair charges. It also provides the stated sale route after a reasonable period of continuing non-payment. The retention connects the goods to the work done on them. It is not a general permission to hold unrelated property for any debt owed by the customer. Nor is a right to retain always identical to a right to sell. A good answer identifies the particular statutory power and its conditions before describing any disposal as lawful.

    **Duties continue while goods are held**

    Having a claim for payment does not remove the duty to exercise the required care. Section 578 controls care, use and mixing. The bailee cannot punish non-payment by deliberately damaging the goods or using them beyond the authorised purpose. Subject to contrary contractual terms, reasonable care rather than automatic insurance is the statutory starting point. Where loss follows negligence, bad faith or breach, liability must be considered. The reason for retaining the goods and the quality of their treatment are separate questions that should receive separate answers.

    **Return and termination**

    Section 579 governs return when the agreed period ends or the purpose is fulfilled. Unless otherwise agreed, additions and profits accompany the goods. A bailee who has finished the work should communicate and return as required, rather than assume indefinite custody on unchanged terms. The rule also addresses responsibility after return becomes due. A bailor's refusal to accept return can affect that responsibility. It is therefore important to state who caused the delay instead of treating every event after the due date as necessarily the bailee's fault.

    Wrongful retention may justify recall under section 579. Unlawful-purpose bailment is void under section 583, while section 584 provides a two-year cause-of-action period for the chapter. Termination concerns the future custody relationship; it does not wipe out a claim for earlier damage or unpaid lawful charges. If a vehicle was negligently damaged before its return, physically handing it back does not by itself settle the compensation claim. Similarly, ending the arrangement does not automatically cancel properly accrued repair charges or the accounting between the parties.

    **Finder of lost goods**

    A finder has not obtained the owner's negotiated permission, but taking custody creates statutory responsibilities. Section 670 allows the finder to keep the goods safely after informing the police with their actual description, until the owner is found. These are conditions for retaining found goods; the section does not compel every passer-by to take possession. The finder must not immediately sell or consume the property on the theory that lost means abandoned. The owner's loss of possession does not by itself show an intention to give up ownership. The finder should preserve the object and make the statutory disclosure so that identification and return remain possible.

    The section recognises relevant expenses and retention until the owner pays them. The checked text also provides ownership after three years without anyone claiming. That later possibility must not be confused with immediate ownership or an automatic reward. India's statutory finder powers, including its particular sale rules, should not be inserted into Nepal's section without authority. In conclusion, both the contractual bailee and the finder require protection, but the source and limits differ. Strong answers explain reciprocal bailment rights, lawful termination and the finder's separate statutory duties without merging the two legal relationships.

- Bailment separates custody from ownership.
- Check purpose, delivery and the s 575 writing threshold.
- Care, use, mixing and return are separate duties.

- Pledge secures debt or performance; account for proceeds and redemption.
- A finder follows s 670, not an assumed foreign sale rule.
