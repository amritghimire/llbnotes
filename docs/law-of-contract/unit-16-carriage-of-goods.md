# Unit 16: Carriage of goods

!!! abstract "In one line"
    Carriage law decides who must deliver goods safely, which transport regime applies, and how loss, delay, declarations and documents affect recovery.

## 16.1 Introduction and importance

Trade needs movement between producers, markets and buyers. Carrier custody creates risks of loss, delay and misdelivery. The carriage bargain is distinct from the underlying sale: a seller may promise goods while another person promises transport. Identify who contracted with the carrier and who can claim, rather than assuming that every owner and recipient has identical contractual rights.

## 16.2 Meaning

Section 602 covers a contract to carry goods between places. Unless otherwise agreed, the receipt or bill issued on handover is evidence of the contract. Its carrier definition includes commercial internal navigation, ropeway, animal and other carriage, but **excludes air and sea transport**. It also excludes the owner's own wage worker, agent or supervised person carrying the owner's goods in the stated setting. Thus not every person moving a box is a carrier under this chapter.

## 16.3 Features

Identify the goods, origin and destination, delivery instructions, carrier, charges, timing, declarations and authorised recipient. Section 603 requires proper-condition delivery within the agreed or otherwise reasonable time. A receipt helps prove quantity and handover but does not eliminate the need to prove the condition, loss and applicable liability rule.

## 16.4 Routes of carriage

### 16.4.1 Land carriage

For a Nepal road-carriage problem within s 602, apply ss 603–609 and the contract. Section 604 ordinarily makes the first entrusted carrier answer where multiple carriers or modes are used, unless otherwise agreed. It addresses loss before delivery but has an express disaster/accident exception. Do not substitute an absolute foreign insurer rule.

Section 605 permits sale without owner consent where disaster/accident prevents timely carriage and perishability or serious value/quality decline creates the statutory need. Proceeds must go to the owner. This is an emergency protection, not a general sale power for unpaid freight.

Section 606's checked default compensation cap is **Rs 100,000**, unless a higher value was declared when contracting or otherwise agreed. Its subsection (2) separately excludes liability for the listed undeclared categories, including valuables, documents, perishable, dangerous, fragile and specified electronic goods, unless the required declaration is made at contracting or handover. The precise statutory list matters; do not reduce it to “only jewellery.” Insurance under subsection (3) must not be assumed from the cap alone.

Section 607 supplies the agreed or statutory value/compensation approach for covered goods, read with the general compensation chapter. Section 608 concerns the end of the carrier’s custody liability by authorised delivery, return before dispatch or the specified s 531(2)(b) prevention-and-return situation, subject to contrary terms. Section 609 gives two years from accrual.

### 16.4.2 Carriers: common-carrier rights, duties, liability and termination

The textbook common carrier holds out the relevant service to the public, unlike a private carrier undertaking a specially negotiated task. Historically strict common-law liability must be distinguished from modern legislation. Useful rights include agreed freight and any legally established retention or expense claim; do not infer a power to sell or seize from the word carrier. Duties include proper custody, delivery and compliance with the applicable documentation and safety rules.

For **India**, the Carriage by Road Act 2007 regulates common-carrier registration, forwarding notes/receipts and liability (ss 3, 8–12). It is a modern statutory comparison, not Nepal's Act. For **Nepal**, use the s 602 definition and the particular rights and exceptions above. Termination must leave accrued loss and payment claims to be resolved.

### 16.4.3 Rail carriage

A railway receipt records the consignment and transport arrangement. Loading, description, dangerous goods, freight, delivery and claim requirements depend on the railway law and contract. India's Railways Act 1989 is the labelled textbook example: s 65 concerns the receipt; s 93 sets general responsibility with listed exceptions, qualified by the reasonable-foresight-and-care proviso; s 99 deals with responsibility after transit. It is not correct to say that naming an excepted cause always ends the inquiry. Do not present those Indian sections as Nepal's current railway regime.

### 16.4.4 Sea carriage: affreightment, charterparty, bill of lading and master

Affreightment is the sea-carriage agreement. A charterparty hires a ship or capacity on terms: a voyage charter concerns a voyage; a time charter concerns an agreed period; a demise/bareboat arrangement transfers a different degree of possession and operational control. These textbook categories require the actual contract to determine management, freight/hire and risks.

A bill of lading normally records receipt, evidences carriage terms and, in its appropriate transferable form, operates as a document of title. It does not mean every sea document automatically transfers ownership. Under the **UK Carriage of Goods by Sea Act 1971 schedule**, article III rules 3–4 address the receipt particulars and evidential effect; article III rules 1–2 address seaworthiness due diligence and cargo care. This is a UK Hague–Visby comparison, not a conclusion that it governs every Nepal-linked shipment. Charterparty and bill terms can interact, but are not the same instrument.

The master commands the ship, manages navigation and cargo operations within authority, and signs or authorises relevant documents where empowered. A sale of cargo or exceptional emergency action needs its actual legal basis; command does not create unlimited ownership powers. Avoid attributing an unrestricted general agency to every master.

### 16.4.5 Air carriage: documents and carrier liability

The **Montreal Convention 1999**, as an international comparison, addresses cargo documentation in articles 4–11: air waybill/cargo receipt, alternative records and essential particulars. Article 18 concerns cargo destruction, loss and damage during carriage by air, with stated exceptions; article 19 concerns delay and its defence. Article 22's monetary limits and their revision require a current source and applicable regime. An air waybill is not simply a transferable sea bill of lading with a new name.

## Full 20-mark answers

These are full practice answers. Adapt the emphasis to the question; the paragraph headings help you organise a 35-minute answer.

??? example "Q1. Explain the carrier’s rights, duties and liability under Nepal’s Civil Code. (20 marks)"
    !!! warning "Unverified"

    **Introduction**

    A contract of carriage concerns movement of goods from one place to another. It supports trade by allowing goods to travel through a professional custodian rather than remain with their owner. The legal problem is how to allocate responsibility for proper delivery, delay, loss and exceptional events. Nepal's Civil Code sections 602 to 609 provide a specific framework, but its scope must be read first. The chapter's definition excludes air and sea carriers and the owner's own wage worker, agent or supervised person in the stated setting. It is therefore inaccurate to apply the chapter automatically to every movement of goods.

    **Formation and proof**

    Section 602 recognises the carriage agreement and, unless otherwise arranged, treats the receipt or bill issued on handover as evidence of it. The document can help establish the goods, the parties and the agreed movement. A good answer should identify the origin, destination, delivery time, recipient and relevant declarations. The underlying sale and the carriage contract remain distinct. A buyer and seller may have their own dispute concerning price or risk, while the carrier's obligation arises from the transport arrangement and applicable law. One contract should not simply be substituted for the other.

    The definition includes commercial carriage by internal navigation, ropeway, animal or other relevant means. This breadth is useful in Nepal, where goods may travel through different transport methods. However, breadth does not remove the express exclusions. In particular, a chapter excluding air and sea cannot supply the complete liability regime for those modes merely because a Nepali trader owns the goods. The student should begin by classifying the actual carrier and service. Only then should the substantive delivery and compensation rules be applied to the claim.

    **Proper condition and time**

    Section 603 requires the carrier to take the goods to the specified destination in proper condition. It addresses loss, destruction, breakage, defacement and other damage affecting proper arrival. Delivery must occur within the agreed period or, where none is fixed, a reasonable time. It must be made to the owner, agent or designated person as provided. A carrier who delivers a consignment to an unrelated person cannot necessarily answer that the vehicle reached the correct town. The identity of the authorised recipient is part of proper performance.

    Reasonable time depends on the arrangement and circumstances rather than an invented universal number of days. The answer should identify any express deadline and whether the actual delay falls within an applicable exception. It should also establish the condition at handover and delivery. Evidence of quantity, packing, visible damage and dates assists the legal reasoning. The receipt is important but not magical: it does not by itself prove every later allegation about cause, value or the person entitled to compensation. Each disputed element needs an evidential basis.

    **Multiple carriers and exceptional loss**

    Section 604 addresses a journey involving more than one carrier or transport method. Unless the contract provides otherwise, the carrier first entrusted with the goods bears the stated responsibility. This helps prevent the owner from being left without a clear contractual respondent simply because the journey was subcontracted or divided. The actual agreement must still be checked. The section also addresses theft, loss, damage or destruction before delivery, but contains an express disaster or accident exception. That exception must appear in a Nepal answer and cannot be erased by a foreign common-carrier slogan.

    The existence of the exception does not justify a vague conclusion that any difficulty on the road excuses every breach. The facts must fit the statutory situation, and the other provisions and contractual responsibilities must be considered. Conversely, the carrier should not be described as an absolute insurer where the Code states a qualification. The useful examination method is to identify the ordinary delivery duty, the event causing loss and the exact exception claimed. This allows the conclusion to follow from the law rather than from sympathy for either party.

    **Emergency disposal and charges**

    Section 605 creates a narrow emergency sale power. If disaster or accident prevents timely carriage and the goods are perishable or liable to serious decline in value or quality, sale without the owner's consent may be permitted under the stated conditions. The proceeds must be passed to the owner as required. This protects value when waiting would destroy it. It is not a universal power to sell a customer's goods because freight is unpaid or because storage has become inconvenient. The factual trigger and accounting remain essential.

    The carrier may claim the lawful agreed freight and relevant contractual entitlements. However, a payment claim must be distinguished from a right to retain or sell property. Such a property power requires an actual legal basis. Section 605 should not be used to fill an unrelated gap. A careful answer states the charge, the agreed payment trigger and any established remedy. It should avoid importing every Indian or English common-carrier right merely because the Nepal relationship has the same commercial purpose. Similar problems can be governed by different statutory solutions.

    **Declarations, compensation and conclusion**

    Section 606 limits compensation in the checked default setting to one hundred thousand rupees unless a higher value was declared when contracting or otherwise agreed. Subsection (2) separately addresses listed undeclared categories, including valuables, documents and specified perishable, dangerous, fragile or electronic goods. The declaration issue is therefore not just a cap calculation: it may affect liability itself. Section 607 gives the applicable agreed or statutory value and loss approach, read with the general compensation chapter. A claimant must establish value and recoverable loss, not assume the purchase invoice settles every issue.

    Section 608 concerns the end of the carrier’s custody liability through authorised delivery, return before dispatch or the specified prevention-and-return situation linked to section 531(2)(b), subject to the contract. Section 609 supplies two years from accrual for chapter claims. In conclusion, Nepal's carriage framework balances proper delivery with defined exceptions, declaration requirements and compensation limits. A complete answer checks scope first, then performance, cause of loss, declared goods and value, remedy and limitation. It does not confuse receipt evidence with automatic liability or apply this land-oriented statutory chapter to excluded air and sea carriage.

??? example "Q2. Explain the main carriage documents and compare land, rail, sea and air carriage. (20 marks)"

    **Introduction**

    Carriage documents help identify what goods were received, where they must go and on what terms the carrier acts. Their legal effects differ between transport regimes. A receipt for road carriage, a railway receipt, a sea bill of lading and an air waybill are not interchangeable simply because each concerns cargo. The examination answer should explain their functions and identify the governing legal setting. Nepal's Civil Code supplies one framework in sections 602 to 609, while rail, sea and air textbook material requires separate and clearly labelled statutory or international comparisons.

    **Land carriage and the Nepal receipt**

    Under section 602, unless otherwise agreed, the receipt or bill issued when the goods are handed over is evidence of the carriage contract. It may record description, quantity, destination and other agreed matters. This evidential role helps connect the owner and carrier to the consignment. However, the chapter does not say that every such receipt is automatically a transferable document of title. A student should distinguish evidence that a carrier received goods from a legal instrument capable of transferring constructive possession or other rights under a particular regime. The document's name alone cannot decide that effect.

    The Nepal chapter's scope is equally important. It includes the stated commercial carriage methods but excludes air and sea transport and the owner's own wage worker, agent or supervised person carrying in the specified setting. Sections 603 and 604 then address delivery, timing, multiple carriers and the disaster or accident exception. Sections 605 to 607 address emergency sale, declarations and compensation. These are Nepal statutory rules. India's Carriage by Road Act 2007 supplies a separate comparison concerning common-carrier registration, forwarding documents and liability; it should not be presented as Nepal legislation.

    **Railway carriage**

    Railway carriage ordinarily uses a railway receipt to record the consignment and the undertaking to transport it. The relevant railway law determines its evidential effect, delivery requirements and claims process. In the Indian textbook setting, the Railways Act 1989 addresses the receipt in section 65, general carrier responsibility in section 93 and post-transit responsibility in section 99. Section 93 contains exceptions and a qualification concerning reasonable foresight and care. Naming a listed cause should therefore not automatically end the discussion without examining the statutory qualification and the actual evidence.

    The railway example shows that transport legislation often contains more than a general promise of safe carriage. It can regulate loading, dangerous goods, freight, delivery and the period after arrival. A student should not assume that goods remaining at a station forever attract the same liability as goods moving on the train. Nor should the Indian sections be assigned Nepal legal status. Where a particular Nepal railway claim is involved, its current statute, contract and route need verification. The comparative syllabus answer can explain the structure without inventing that missing applicability analysis.

    **Affreightment and charterparty**

    Affreightment is an agreement for carriage by sea. A charterparty hires a ship or carrying capacity under negotiated terms. A voyage charter concerns a voyage; a time charter concerns an agreed period; a demise or bareboat arrangement involves a different transfer of possession and operational control. These categories help explain why responsibilities may differ between shipowner, charterer and cargo interests. The actual contract remains necessary. The word charter does not alone establish who manages the crew, bears every operating cost or answers for a particular cargo loss.

    A bill of lading is different from the charterparty. It normally records receipt of goods, evidences the carriage terms and, in the appropriate form, performs a document-of-title function. These functions should be stated separately. Receipt concerns what the carrier acknowledges taking; evidence concerns the terms of carriage; title-document effects concern rights associated with the document under the governing law. Not every document labelled bill automatically transfers ownership of the goods. The form, lawful transfer and applicable rules matter. A charterparty may also interact with bill terms, making attention to each instrument necessary.

    **The UK sea-carriage comparison and master**

    The United Kingdom Carriage of Goods by Sea Act 1971 schedule provides the labelled Hague–Visby comparison. Article III rules 3 and 4 concern bill particulars and evidential effect, while rules 1 and 2 address seaworthiness due diligence and cargo care. These references explain the structured sea-carriage approach. They are not proof that the UK rules govern every shipment involving a Nepali trader. The relevant route, contract and legal applicability must be established separately before using a particular defence, deadline or monetary limit in a real claim.

    The ship's master commands the vessel and performs navigation and cargo functions within the authority supplied by the governing law and arrangement. The master may sign or authorise relevant documents where empowered. However, command does not confer unrestricted ownership of cargo or an automatic power to sell it whenever convenient. Any exceptional emergency action requires its own legal basis. This is an application of the broader distinction between operational control and ownership. The examination answer should state that distinction rather than turn the master into a universal agent for every cargo transaction.

    **Air cargo and conclusion**

    For the international comparison, Montreal Convention 1999 articles 4 to 11 address air cargo documentation, including the waybill, cargo receipt and permitted alternative records. Article 18 concerns cargo loss or damage during carriage by air with its stated exceptions; article 19 concerns delay and the defence. The applicable regime and current revised monetary limits require checking. An air waybill should not be treated as an automatically negotiable sea bill of lading. The fact that both identify a consignment does not make their title effects identical.

    In conclusion, transport documents perform proof, instructions and sometimes title-related functions, but the scope of each function comes from its legal regime. A strong answer compares the Nepal receipt, Indian railway receipt, sea bill and air waybill, then separates the charterparty and the master's role. It also states the unresolved Nepal applicability of the rail, sea and air examples instead of using sections 602 to 609 for excluded modes. This method teaches the full syllabus categories while keeping the legal conclusions tied to sources that actually support them.

## Test yourself

??? question "Does s 602 cover a commercial airline?"
    No. The chapter's carrier definition excludes air and sea transport.

??? question "Is its default cap an unconditional insurance promise?"
    No. Read the declaration rule, listed-category exclusion, other exceptions and contract.

??? question "When can s 605 permit sale?"
    In the specified disaster/accident delay with perishability or serious value/quality decline; proceeds must be accounted to the owner.

??? question "Are a charterparty and a bill of lading identical?"
    No. The first hires ship/capacity on terms; the second has cargo receipt, evidence and appropriate title-document functions.

??? question "Does an air waybill automatically carry sea-bill title effects?"
    No. Apply the air regime.

## One-minute revision

- Classify carrier and mode before applying liability.
- Nepal s 602 excludes air and sea.
- Receipt evidence is not automatically a title instrument.
- Read s 604 exceptions and s 606 declarations before calculating loss.
- Sea charterparty, bill and master have different functions.
- Air documentation and limits need the applicable international/domestic regime.
