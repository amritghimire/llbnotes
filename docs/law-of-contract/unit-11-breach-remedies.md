# Unit 11: Breach of contract and remedies

!!! abstract "In one line"
    Breach is a legally relevant failure or refusal to perform. The remedy depends on the loss, the agreement and the Code's conditions, not simply on how angry the injured party feels.

!!! warning "Unverified"

## 11.1 Meaning and types of breach

Section 535(1) recognises failure to fulfil an obligation, notice that a party will not perform, and conduct showing inability to perform. Textbooks call failure when due **actual breach**, and advance refusal or disabling conduct **anticipatory breach**. Identify the duty and timeline rather than using the labels alone.

Material and minor failures also differ. Section 535(2) addresses cancellation by notice in its material-failure circumstances; subsection (3) releases the cancelling party from continued performance. Do not infer automatic cancellation for every small defect. Section 536 deals with cancellation where several persons form a side. Sections 525–526 separately matter for delay and essential time.

Example: an agreed delivery completely refused differs from a small correctable defect, and from nonperformance caused by the customer's denial of essential access. Apply the proper provision before assuming breach or remedy.

## 11.2 Meaning and types of remedies

### Compensation: sections 537, 542–543

Section 537(1) concerns actual loss caused by breach and the loss contemplated and determined at contracting in its terms. Subsection (2) allows a **reasonable amount not exceeding** a sum agreed in advance. The named sum is not automatically payable in full.

Without that provision, 537(3) addresses direct and actual loss or reasonable compensation in its terms, excluding indirect or imaginary loss. The Nepali text's word for imaginary or speculative loss should not be silently replaced with every detail of an English remoteness test. Subsection (4) concerns a request for proportional time extension in the stated delayed-completion compensation setting; (5) preserves other legal remedies despite payment of compensation. Section 542 generally values compensation in money; 543 includes the stated intentional or negligent failure and the innocent party's expected gain in assessment.

Textbook labels include ordinary damages, special loss known in advance, nominal damages, exemplary damages, liquidated damages and penalties. These classify different ideas. The checked Code expressly supports the above compensation framework; it does not establish an automatic award under every textbook label.

| Textbook label | Plain meaning | Nepal caution |
|---|---|---|
| Ordinary damages | Compensation for the ordinary proved loss of the failure | Apply 537, not the label alone |
| Special damages | Loss connected with particular circumstances known at contracting | Establish the statutory knowledge and loss conditions |
| Nominal damages | A small award recognising breach without proved substantial loss | A general Nepal entitlement is not verified here |
| Exemplary or punitive damages | An award intended to punish rather than simply compensate | Do not assume the Code creates this entitlement |
| Liquidated damages | A sum set in advance for breach | Nepal 537(2) requires reasonable compensation within the cap |
| Penalty | A stipulated burden challenged as punitive under the applicable doctrine | England's test and Nepal's statutory assessment must remain separate |

!!! warning "Unverified"
    No general Nepal entitlement to nominal or punitive contract damages, or a separately codified universal mitigation rule, was verified here. Reasonable steps to avoid further loss are a useful practical issue; India's section 73 explanation expressly addresses means of reducing inconvenience. Do not cite that explanation as Nepal law.

### Cancellation and return: sections 535–539

Cancellation ends future performance under the applicable rule. Section 538 requires accounting and return in its stated ending or invalidity circumstances, including reasonable value for services and the stated legal expenses if return is withheld. Section 539 permits proportionate contractual payment for completed work. Returning an advance, paying for work and compensating breach loss are different claims. Avoid double counting.

### Specific performance: section 540

The injured party may claim actual performance instead of compensation where money is not reasonable and sufficient for the loss. It is unavailable where money is sufficient, court supervision is impracticable, the contract concerns personal skill or knowledge services, actual performance is impossible, or the party in breach itself seeks this remedy. The special nature of an item may help explain inadequacy, but does not bypass these exclusions.

### Preventive order: section 541

Where threatened conduct contrary to the contract's nature would prevent performance, the affected party may seek an order stopping it. The court can issue the appropriate immediate order pending resolution under the contract or law. Further loss caused by disobeying the order is addressed in subsection (3). This is not an unrestricted power to compel every personal service which 540 excludes.

## Nepal's law

Use 535–544, together with the performance provisions relevant to the alleged failure. Section 544 gives the checked two-year claim period from the cause of action. Apply the right category rather than assuming all disputes share one deadline. See [Unit 7](unit-07-indirect-contract.md) for quantum meruit and [Unit 10](unit-10-termination.md) for ending grounds.

## Full 20-mark answers

??? example "Q1. Explain breach and the monetary remedies available under Nepal's Civil Code."
    !!! warning "Unverified"
        This answer uses the checked Nepal text through Jestha 2076. Complete later-amendment currency remains unverified.

    **Breach and its identification**

    A contract creates duties, and breach concerns a legally relevant failure to fulfil them. Section 535 of Nepal's Civil Code recognises nonperformance, notice that a party will not perform and conduct showing inability to perform. The first task is to identify the obligation and the facts constituting failure. A disappointed expectation not actually promised is not necessarily breach. Likewise, failure caused by the other side's prevention requires the relevant performance provisions considered. Monetary remedies follow an established legal basis, not simply the assertion that the claimant suffered inconvenience or would have preferred a more profitable transaction than the agreement ultimately produced.

    Textbooks distinguish actual and anticipatory breach. Actual breach concerns failure when performance is due, while anticipatory breach describes advance refusal or disabling conduct in the relevant legal setting. Nepal's section 535 gives the immediate statutory language. Material failure also matters to cancellation under subsection (2), which requires the stated notice. These distinctions prevent the answer from treating every small defect as automatic termination. The monetary analysis should explain the link between the duty, breach and claimed loss, while any cancellation route is identified separately. The same facts can raise several issues, but their legal requirements must not be assumed to be identical.

    **Compensation under section 537**

    Section 537(1) permits the injured party to recover the actual loss caused by breach or the loss known and determined at contracting as contemplated in the provision. The agreement and the parties' knowledge therefore matter. Suppose a supplier fails to deliver the agreed equipment and the buyer incurs a proved direct replacement expense. That expense raises a different claim from a speculative prediction that every future customer would have bought from the buyer if delivery had occurred. The provision requires legally recoverable loss. It does not guarantee reimbursement of every amount mentioned in the claimant's description of the consequences.

    Proof of amount is as important as proof of breach. The claimant should identify the expenditure, lost benefit or other relevant item and connect it with the failure. Documents, transaction records and the agreement can assist, but no single kind of document is invariably decisive for every case. The claim should also distinguish money already paid from additional loss. An advance awaiting return and a replacement expense are different items whose interaction needs accounting. Treating the entire contract price, every expense and all hoped-for future revenue as automatically cumulative damages risks recovering more than the legal basis and proven facts support.

    **A sum fixed in advance**

    Parties may state a sum payable for breach. Section 537(2) allows recovery of a reasonable amount not exceeding that agreed sum, subject to the provision's framework. The sum therefore acts as a ceiling, not an automatic instruction to award it all. For example, a delay clause naming a maximum amount still requires the statutory assessment of reasonable compensation. The answer should resist both extremes: that the figure is meaningless merely because it was agreed beforehand, and that the court must always award the whole figure without considering the section's conditions or the loss question arising from the breach.

    Textbooks distinguish liquidated damages from penalties, but Nepal's statutory wording must remain central. English law's Cavendish decision addresses a different penalty framework, including legitimate interests and disproportionality. That comparison can explain why agreed sums require legal scrutiny, but it cannot replace section 537(2). India's section 74 also has its own reasonable-compensation rule. A Nepal conclusion must identify the Code rather than select a foreign label and assume the consequence follows automatically. The meaning of the clause, the relevant breach and the statutory limit all need to be considered before determining what sum can actually be recovered.

    **Unagreed compensation and assessment**

    Section 537(3) addresses the situation without the advance compensation provision. It concerns direct and actual loss or the reasonable amount described there, excluding indirect or imaginary loss. The Nepali text should control the explanation. A student should not silently replace that wording with every element of an English remoteness doctrine. Nor should imaginary loss be presented as proved merely because a claimant gives it a detailed calculation. The foundation of the amount must be established. The distinction between genuine financial consequence and speculation helps ensure that compensation responds to the contractual injury rather than creating an unsupported commercial windfall.

    Sections 542 and 543 add guidance. Compensation is generally valued in money unless otherwise provided, with the specific-performance qualification in the Code. In assessment, section 543 addresses whether failure was intentional or negligent and what the innocent party would have gained without breach. These matters must be applied within the legal framework rather than treated as automatic punitive multipliers. The checked provisions do not establish an unrestricted entitlement to exemplary contract damages. Practical avoidance of further loss can be relevant to analysing the facts, but India's express mitigation-related explanation should not be cited as though it were a separately verified Nepal section.

    **Related monetary claims and conclusion**

    Section 538 addresses return and accounting in the stated cancellation or invalidity situations. It includes reasonable value for services and the stated expenses where failure to return benefits requires legal action. Section 539 concerns proportionate contractual payment for completed work when the contract ends in its circumstances. These claims differ from compensation for breach. A completed paid stage cannot simply be claimed again under another label. Conversely, ending the contract does not establish that every unpaid benefit has become irrelevant. Each monetary claim must be matched with its legal purpose and adjusted to avoid counting the same financial disadvantage twice.

    Section 537 also contains the specified delay-related extension request and preservation of other legal remedies despite compensation payment, while section 544 provides the chapter's checked limitation period. In conclusion, monetary relief begins with an established duty and breach, then moves to recoverable loss, reasonable valuation and the treatment of agreed sums. It also distinguishes return of benefits and completed-work payment from damages. This method supports compensation grounded in the transaction and evidence, rather than an assumed right to every textbook damages category or every sum the disappointed party associates with the failed agreement and its wider commercial consequences.

??? example "Q2. Explain specific performance, preventive orders and cancellation as remedies."

        This answer uses the checked Nepal text through Jestha 2076. Complete later-amendment currency remains unverified.

    **Why remedies differ**

    Money does not answer every contractual problem. Sometimes the injured party wants the promised performance itself, wants threatened conduct stopped or wishes to end continuing duties after a serious failure. Nepal's Civil Code provides distinct routes for these responses. Section 540 concerns specific performance, section 541 preventive orders and section 535 cancellation following the stated breach circumstances. Their purposes and limits differ. An answer should identify what the claimant seeks and why that response is legally available. A general statement that a court can make any order needed for fairness would ignore the conditions deliberately attached to each statutory remedy.

    Consider a buyer seeking delivery of a particular promised item, a customer seeking to prevent disposal of something needed for performance and a party wishing to stop further work after material refusal. Each claim requires the relevant facts and provision. The same contract might generate more than one possible remedy, but availability is not automatic. The claimant must also distinguish ending future duties from undoing every earlier act or obtaining a monetary award. The statutory framework determines the proper response, rather than the claimant's choice of the most forceful-sounding label or an assumption that all contractual remedies operate in exactly the same way.

    **Specific performance under section 540**

    Section 540(1) allows an injured party to claim actual performance instead of compensation where money would not be reasonable and sufficient for the actual loss. This starts with the inadequacy of monetary substitution. The special character of a promised item may help explain that inadequacy, but the legal conditions still need proof. The provision does not say that every claimant who prefers performance must receive it. The agreement, the obligation and the practical possibility of carrying it out remain relevant. An unsupported assertion that something is unique cannot alone remove the restrictions found in the rest of the section.

    Subsection (2) identifies exclusions. Specific performance cannot be claimed where money is adequate, where the court cannot supervise whether the work is performed, where personal skill or knowledge services are involved, where actual performance is impossible or where the party in breach itself claims the remedy. These are important examination points because they define the boundary of the right. A personal portrait commission illustrates why simply demanding the named artist be compelled to work raises a statutory exclusion. The existence of a clear written agreement does not automatically override that limit or make every contracted act judicially enforceable in its original form.

    **The practical meaning of the exclusions**

    The supervision and impossibility exclusions concern the workable character of the remedy. A legal order should not be assumed to accomplish what cannot actually be performed or supervised in the manner contemplated. The personal-skill exclusion also recognises a distinct category of service. These limits should be stated through the Code rather than through an unchecked foreign list. An answer may compare English or Indian materials cautiously, but the Nepal conclusion depends on section 540. In India, specific relief is governed by separate legislation, so merely citing Contract Act section 73 would not establish its entire specific-performance framework either.

    The exclusion relating to a claimant in breach requires attention to the claimant's own position. A person cannot simply disregard their failure and demand the remedy as though the other side alone were relevant. This is another reason to identify reciprocal duties and actual conduct. The remedy is connected with an injured party's established claim, not an abstract judicial power detached from the transaction. Other legal questions may remain, but the answer should not promise specific performance where the checked statutory condition or exclusion prevents it. The available alternative must be examined on its own legal basis and evidence.

    **Preventive orders under section 541**

    Section 541 addresses threatened conduct contrary to the contract's nature which would make performance impossible. The affected party may ask the court to stop that conduct. The court can issue the appropriate immediate order while the dispute is resolved according to the contract or law. The remedy therefore has a preventive purpose. It responds before the threatened action defeats performance, rather than waiting to calculate loss afterward. The claimant must identify the conduct and explain its effect on the agreed performance. A vague fear of future disagreement is not the same as establishing the situation addressed by the provision.

    Subsection (3) addresses additional loss arising from failure to obey the order. This reinforces the legal significance of compliance, but it does not turn the preventive route into an unrestricted substitute for specific performance. For example, a claimant should not assume that asking for an order to stop conduct automatically bypasses the personal-service exclusion in section 540. The provisions must be applied together to the actual remedy sought. The inquiry concerns the legal effect of the proposed order, not merely the wording used to describe it. Labels cannot establish availability where the governing conditions have not been met.

    **Cancellation and surviving rights**

    Section 535 permits cancellation by notice in its material-failure circumstances, after which the cancelling party is not obliged to continue performance under subsection (3). This differs from compelling the original act or stopping a threatened one. Timing failures may also require sections 525 and 526 considered. Where several people form one side, section 536 addresses the indivisible cancellation authority in its terms. These conditions prevent a party from treating every minor defect or individual preference as an unlimited right to terminate the whole agreement without the required basis or communication. The particular failure and the legal route must be established.

    Cancellation does not automatically erase all monetary consequences. Section 538 governs benefits and accounting in its settings, section 539 completed-work payment and section 537 breach compensation. In conclusion, specific performance supplies the promised act where its conditions permit, preventive orders stop the defined threatening conduct and cancellation releases continuing duties on the relevant ground. A complete answer explains the statutory limits and then addresses surviving rights without duplicate recovery. This preserves a practical range of remedies while preventing judicial relief from being described as automatic, unlimited or independent of the contract, the claimant's position and the facts needed to bring the claim within the Code.

## One-minute revision

- Identify duty, failure and the appropriate remedy.
- Actual and anticipatory breach describe different timing.
- Cancellation has conditions and notice requirements.
- Section 537's agreed sum is a cap on reasonable compensation.
- Section 540 has an inadequacy test and five exclusions.
- Section 541 prevents specified threatening conduct.
- Payment, return and damages must be distinguished.
