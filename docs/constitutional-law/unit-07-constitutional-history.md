# Unit 7: Constitutional history of Nepal

!!! abstract "In one line"
    Nepal's constitutional history traces changing claims to state power, from hereditary authority toward popular sovereignty, rights and representative government.

## Why it matters for the exam

Compare the source of authority, executive, legislature, rights and limits in each instrument. A chronology alone is not an explanation. Keep **2015 B.S. (1959 A.D.)** separate from **2015 A.D. (2072 B.S.)**.

## 7.1 Pre-constitutional norms and laws

Before the first constitutional document in this syllabus, Nepal was not a society without law. The important distinction is between existing legal or customary authority and a modern constitutional framework controlling that authority.

The 1948 preamble expressly traces Rana authority to royal instruments called Panja-patras dating from 1903 B.S. onwards. Its section 3 preserves succession according to law, custom and usage. These are primary textual examples of authority and customary arrangements predating that document. They do not establish that all earlier powers were democratic or effectively limited.

A broader historical answer would discuss royal commands, customary practices, religious ideas of duty and general codes such as the Muluki Ain.

    The detailed chronology, operative rules and original texts of those materials have not been checked for this unit. Do not supply invented clauses, a uniform account of all communities, or the claim that religious norms alone governed every dispute.

## 7.2 Government of Nepal Act 1948 (2004 B.S.)

The checked [English constitutional text](https://constitutionnet.org/sites/default/files/1948_constitution_english.pdf) identifies Padma Shum Shere as promulgating the instrument. Its preamble invokes delegated hereditary authority and gradual association of people with administration.

- **Section 4** lists rights and promises, subject to its stated qualifications.
- **Section 6** places executive authority in the Rana Maharaja, exercised directly or through subordinate officers.
- **Section 7** provides a Council of Ministers to aid and advise him.
- **Section 22** provides a legislature with two chambers, including elected and nominated elements.
- **Sections 48–49** address justice and courts.

Its significance is the attempt to state governmental arrangements and rights in a constitutional document while preserving concentrated hereditary authority. Textual promises must not be mistaken for proof that the entire scheme operated.

!!! warning "Unverified"
    Its precise implementation history is not established by the constitutional copy. The English heading and section 2 also use different calendar references, so no unverified conversion of its commencement date is taught here.

## 7.3 Interim Government Act 1951 (2007 B.S.)

The [Law Commission text reproduced by ICNL](https://www.icnl.org/wp-content/uploads/Nepal_Theinterim.pdf) begins with the royal proclamation's commitment to government under a Constitution framed by an elected Constituent Assembly. It provides interim arrangements while that Constitution is awaited.

The preamble gives the Council of Ministers an advisory role. Sections 3–5 distinguish directive principles and welfare commitments from immediately enforceable rights. Section 3 says that Part II is not enforceable by courts, while treating its principles as fundamental in governance.

This is a transition document, not the present Constitution. The checked reproduction is published pursuant to the **Third Amendment, 2010 B.S.** It must not be presented as the unamended 1951 text.

## 7.4 Constitution of the Kingdom of Nepal 1959 (2015 B.S.)

The checked [1959 English text](https://constitutionnet.org/sites/default/files/1959_constitution_english.pdf) combines representative institutions with substantial royal power.

| Provision | Feature |
|---|---|
| Article 1 | Fundamental-law status, with protection of succession arrangements |
| Articles 3–9 | Rights, qualifications and constitutional remedies |
| Article 10 | Executive power vested in the King, with advisory and discretionary arrangements |
| Article 18 | Parliament comprising the King and two Houses |
| Article 19 | Senate of 36, half elected by the House and half nominated |
| Article 55 | Broad emergency powers expressed in the text |

The constitutional tension is between representative parliamentary features and royal discretionary authority. Written rights also had substantial qualifications, including Article 8. Do not describe the instrument as identical to today's parliamentary republic.

## 7.5 Constitution of Nepal 1962 (2019 B.S.)

The checked [1962 English constitutional text](https://constitutionnet.org/sites/default/files/constitution_1962.pdf) establishes a monarchical Panchayat framework. It includes first-amendment notes and should not be treated as a certified final edition incorporating every later amendment. It differs from the multiparty parliamentary framework of 1990.

Article 20(2) locates sovereignty in the King and describes executive, legislative and judicial powers as deriving from him. [Rajeev Parajuli, NKP decision 7618, paragraph 22](https://nkp.gov.np/full_detail/2166/), later reproduces and contrasts that position with the 1990 Constitution.

| Checked provision | Constitutional feature |
|---|---|
| Articles 1 and 3 | Fundamental-law claim within a monarchical Hindu state |
| Articles 9–17 | Duties, rights, remedies and substantial public-good restrictions |
| Article 11(2a), marked as a first-amendment insertion | Prohibition on parties and organizations motivated by party politics |
| Article 24 | Executive power exercised by the King directly or through ministers or subordinate officers under the text |
| Article 34 | National Panchayat as the apex of the Panchayat system |
| Articles 81–82 | Emergency and amendment arrangements centred on royal authority, with the stated procedures |

!!! warning "Unverified"
    The complete amendment history and a final consolidated edition remain unchecked. The copy has first-amendment markers. Do not present its institutional composition as unchanged throughout the Panchayat period or invent totals from memory.

Its basic teaching point is that a written Constitution can organize concentrated monarchical authority. Existence of a document is therefore different from the degree of effective constitutionalism.

## 7.6 Constitution of the Kingdom of Nepal 1990 (2047 B.S.)

The checked [1990 English text](https://constitutionnet.org/sites/default/files/1990_constitution_english.pdf) describes constitutional monarchy and multiparty parliamentary government in its preamble.

- **Articles 1 and 3** establish fundamental-law status and sovereignty in the people.
- **Article 4** describes the Kingdom in the terms of that historical Constitution, including its Hindu and monarchical character.
- **Article 35** regulates executive power through the King and Council of Ministers within its stated framework.
- **Article 44** establishes Parliament comprising the King and two Houses.
- **Articles 23 and 88** provide constitutional-remedy and Supreme Court jurisdiction provisions.
- **Articles 115–116** concern emergency and amendment.

The shift in sovereignty is central. Constitutional monarchy is not the same as a republican presidency. The prescribed dissolution and royal-commission cases belong to this historical text. Apply their law as it then stood, not current Articles 76 or 133 as if they were the original provisions.

## 7.7 Interim Constitution 2007 (2063 B.S.)

The checked [UNDP bilingual edition](https://peacemaker.un.org/sites/default/files/document/files/2022/07/npinterimconstitutionwith8amendments2007_0.pdf) incorporates the first **eight amendments**, through its July 2010 publication. It is not a final edition of every amendment made before replacement.

Articles 1–2 concern fundamental-law status and popular sovereignty. Article 37 vests executive power in the Council of Ministers. Part 3 protects fundamental rights; Article 32 and Article 107 address remedies and Supreme Court jurisdiction. The Constituent Assembly framework and Article 138's restructuring provisions give the instrument its transitional significance.

The amended edition's Article 4 describes a federal democratic republican state, but its footnotes record changes. **Do not claim that every republican provision in this 2010 edition already formed part of the original January 2007 text.** Constitutional transition occurred through amendments as well as replacement. The 2015 Constitution supplies the later three-level federal structure studied in [Unit 6](unit-06-federal-system.md).

## Full 20-mark answers

??? example "Q1. Trace Nepal's constitutional development from 1948 to the Interim Constitution of 2007. (20 marks)"
    **Introduction**

    Nepal's constitutional development can be understood as a changing legal account of who holds state power and how that power should be controlled. The six instruments prescribed between 1948 and 2007 do not represent six identical attempts at the same system. They organize different relationships among hereditary authority, representative institutions, rights and popular sovereignty. A useful chronology therefore compares constitutional choices rather than merely listing dates. It must also distinguish a document's promises from evidence of actual implementation, and an original text from provisions introduced through amendment. Those distinctions prevent a simple narrative from becoming a misleading account of legal change.

    **The setting before the first instrument**

    The absence of a modern constitutional document did not mean that authority operated without rules or inherited practices. The 1948 preamble itself refers to royal instruments delegating authority, and section 3 preserves succession through law, custom and usage. These references show that the first document claimed continuity with an earlier order rather than creating all authority from nothing. A fuller account of royal commands, religious norms, customary law and general codes needs the original historical sources. Their detailed operation is not established here. The safe constitutional point is the difference between rules sustaining authority and a superior framework effectively limiting its exercise.

    **Government of Nepal Act 1948**

    The 1948 Act identifies Padma Shum Shere as its promulgating authority and presents gradual association of people with administration as a purpose. Section 4 states rights and promises, while section 6 places executive authority in the Rana Maharaja. Section 7 provides advisory ministers, and section 22 provides a legislature with elected and nominated elements in two chambers. Sections 48–49 concern justice and courts. These features give the document constitutional importance, but also reveal the continuing concentration of power. Its existence cannot establish that all its arrangements became effective in practice. The implementation history and exact calendar conversion of commencement require separate verification.

    **Interim Government Act 1951**

    The 1951 interim instrument addresses governance while a promised Constitution is to be framed by an elected Constituent Assembly. Its preamble reflects this transitional purpose and the position of a Council of Ministers advising the King. Its directive principles also distinguish welfare aspirations from directly enforceable claims. Section 3 states the judicial-enforcement limit for Part II while treating the principles as fundamental in governance. This is significant because constitutional law can direct governmental responsibility through different legal methods. The consulted Law Commission reproduction was published pursuant to the Third Amendment of 2010 B.S., so its complete contents cannot be described as the untouched original 1951 text.

    **Constitution of the Kingdom 1959**

    The 1959 Constitution, corresponding to 2015 B.S., combines rights and representative institutions with substantial royal authority. Article 1 establishes fundamental-law status. Articles 3–9 address rights and remedies, while Article 8 contains important qualifications. Article 10 vests executive power in the King within advisory and discretionary arrangements. Article 18 establishes Parliament comprising the King and two Houses, and Article 19 provides the Senate's composition. Article 55 states broad emergency powers. The constitutional problem is therefore not simply whether parliamentary institutions exist, but how they relate to royal discretion. It must not be confused with the republican Constitution adopted in 2015 A.D.

    **Constitution of Nepal 1962**

    The 1962 instrument belongs to the Panchayat constitutional order. Its central sovereignty position can be checked through the Supreme Court's quotation in Rajeev Parajuli, decision 7618. Article 20(2), discussed there, placed sovereignty in the King and described the principal governmental powers as deriving from him. This differs fundamentally from an account in which state authority belongs to the people and is allocated through a superior Constitution. The checked copy also provides duties and rights in Articles 9–17, while Article 17 allows substantial public-good restrictions. Article 34 establishes the National Panchayat. Its first-amendment insertion in Article 11(2a) prohibits parties and organizations motivated by party politics. These details must be attributed to the checked edition rather than treated as proof of an unchanged text throughout the Panchayat period. Its central significance remains the concentration of constitutional authority despite the existence of written rights.

    **Constitution of the Kingdom 1990**

    The 1990 Constitution changes the constitutional account of authority while retaining monarchy. Article 1 establishes fundamental-law status and Article 3 vests sovereignty in the people. Its preamble identifies constitutional monarchy, multiparty democracy and parliamentary government. Article 35 regulates executive power, while Article 44 establishes Parliament comprising the King and two Houses. Articles 23 and 88 provide constitutional remedies and Supreme Court jurisdiction. The instrument therefore cannot be reduced either to unrestricted royal rule or to today's republic. Its particular allocation of powers explains why historical dissolution and royal-commission disputes must be studied through its own provisions.

    **Interim Constitution 2007**

    The Interim Constitution provides the framework for another transition, including rights, constitution making and restructuring. Article 37 vests executive power in the Council of Ministers. Articles 32 and 107 address remedies and Supreme Court jurisdiction. The Constituent Assembly framework and Article 138's restructuring provisions connect interim governance with the making of a new constitutional settlement. The consulted UNDP bilingual edition contains the first eight amendments through 2010. Its amended republican wording must therefore be distinguished from the original January 2007 text. The transition cannot be understood accurately if later amendments are silently moved back to the date of the original instrument.

    **Overall direction and limits**

    Across these instruments, recurring questions concern the source of sovereignty, control of executive authority, representative participation and enforceable rights. The texts reveal both change and continuity. A legislature can exist alongside concentrated executive authority, and a written rights section can contain broad qualifications. An interim framework can introduce important institutions without settling every question permanently. The later 2015 Constitution supplies the present federal republican framework, but should be used as a comparison rather than as the law applied throughout the earlier period. Historical accuracy requires keeping each instrument and edition in its own legal setting.

    **Conclusion**

    Nepal's development from 1948 to 2007 is best explained through constitutional choices about authority and restraint. The sequence moves through Rana constitutional promises, interim governance, royal-parliamentary arrangements, the Panchayat order, constitutional monarchy and a further constitution-making transition. The most important comparison is not the number of Articles but the relationship between sovereignty, institutions and remedies. A sound answer identifies the checked provisions, acknowledges edition limits and distinguishes formal promises from practical results. That method explains why constitutional history is relevant to present law without pretending that every historical institution has an identical modern counterpart.

??? example "Q2. Compare the constitutional location of sovereignty, executive power and rights in Nepal's historical constitutions. (20 marks)"
    **Introduction**

    Constitutional history becomes clearer when different instruments are compared through the same questions. Who is said to possess sovereignty? How is executive power exercised? What protection is available when power affects an individual? These questions reveal differences that a list of dates cannot explain. Nepal's instruments between 1948 and 2007 contain changing arrangements of hereditary authority, representative institutions and rights. The comparison must nevertheless respect the sources. A later amended edition cannot prove the exact contents of the original document, and a constitutional promise cannot by itself prove that the promised institution operated effectively in practice.

    **Authority in the 1948 Act**

    The Government of Nepal Act 1948 grounds its authority in the Rana order described in its preamble. That preamble refers to royal instruments delegating power, while section 3 protects succession arrangements according to law, custom and usage. Section 6 places executive authority in the Rana Maharaja, exercised directly or through subordinate officers. Advisory ministers and a legislature appear in the scheme, but their presence must be assessed alongside this central allocation. The document therefore illustrates a constitutional form that states rights and institutions while preserving hereditary concentration. It does not justify assuming modern popular sovereignty or equal institutional power.

    **The 1951 interim purpose**

    The Interim Government Act 1951 expresses a different transitional purpose. Its preamble refers to a Constitution to be framed by an elected Constituent Assembly and to ministerial advice during the interim period. This promised relationship between governance and representative constitution making is constitutionally significant. However, it should not be turned into a claim that the promised permanent settlement immediately existed. The checked reproduction includes amendment history, being published pursuant to the Third Amendment of 2010 B.S. A comparison should therefore identify the particular source and avoid attributing every later institutional detail to the original moment of adoption.

    **Royal and representative features in 1959**

    The 1959 Constitution provides a clearer example of a written fundamental instrument combining representative features with royal discretion. Article 10 vests executive authority in the King and specifies advisory and discretionary arrangements. Article 18 provides Parliament comprising the King and two Houses, while Article 19 divides Senate selection between election by the House and royal nomination. These provisions should be read together. The existence of a representative chamber is important, but does not erase the legal position of the monarchy. Equally, the presence of a monarch does not mean the Constitution contains no representative or rights-based elements at all.

    **Sovereignty in the 1962 order**

    Article 20(2) of the 1962 Constitution, as quoted and discussed by the Supreme Court in decision 7618, located sovereignty in the King and described governmental powers as deriving from him. This makes the source of authority central to understanding the Panchayat framework. A written constitution can organize a concentrated system rather than a modern distribution based on popular sovereignty. The checked English copy includes first-amendment markers, including the prohibition on party-political organizations under Article 11(2a). Rights and remedies in Articles 9–17 are accompanied by public-good restrictions. These features show why the existence of written rights is not enough to establish effective restraint. A final amendment audit remains outstanding, so later institutional changes should not be attributed to the original document without checking their source.

    **Popular sovereignty under the 1990 Constitution**

    The 1990 Constitution expressly vests sovereignty in the people through Article 3 and makes itself fundamental law through Article 1. It retains constitutional monarchy and regulates executive authority through Article 35. Parliament under Article 44 includes the King and two Houses. These arrangements show why popular sovereignty and republican headship are different questions. A Constitution may recognize the people as the source of sovereignty while retaining a monarch within a limited framework. Historical disputes must be assessed against that arrangement. The later Court's refusal to treat the old 1962 conception as continuing unrestricted authority illustrates the legal significance of constitutional replacement.

    **Rights and remedies across the texts**

    Rights need comparison with their qualifications and remedies. Section 4 of the 1948 Act lists broad promises, but their implementation requires separate historical evidence. The 1959 text includes rights and Article 9 remedies, while Article 8 states substantial qualifications. The 1990 Constitution protects rights and connects Article 23 remedies with the Supreme Court's Article 88 jurisdiction. These differences matter more than saying every document contains a rights chapter. The important questions are who benefits, what restrictions are allowed and whether an institution can give an effective remedy. A right's wording and its practical enforceability are connected but distinct matters.

    **Directive principles and interim rights**

    The 1951 framework's section 3 demonstrates another distinction. Directive principles are described as fundamental in governance but not enforceable through courts in the same manner as an individual legal claim. The 2007 Interim Constitution separately contains rights and remedy provisions, including Articles 32 and 107. A careful comparison therefore avoids placing every constitutional promise in one category. Welfare principles, institutional duties and enforceable individual rights can use different mechanisms. This lesson remains relevant when studying the current Constitution, but current provisions should be compared by function rather than substituted as if they were the historical law.

    **The ministerial and constitution-making transition**

    Article 37 of the Interim Constitution vests executive power in the Council of Ministers, while the Constituent Assembly framework addresses the preparation of a new constitutional settlement. Article 138 concerns restructuring. The checked eight-amendment edition also contains amended republican and federal language. Its footnotes must be observed so that the original 2007 text is not confused with the later amended order. This transition shows that constitutional development takes place through both amendment and replacement. The present three-level federal structure belongs to the later 2015 framework and should not be described as fully identical to every interim restructuring proposal.

    The comparison also changes the way historical judgments should be read. A court addressing royal authority under the 1990 Constitution must begin with that instrument's allocation of power, even where the parties invoke older practices. The claim that a power once existed does not establish that it survived constitutional replacement unchanged. This lesson links history with legal reasoning. It explains why the same institutional title can carry a different legal meaning when the source, conditions and limits of its authority have changed.

    **Critical assessment and conclusion**

    The comparison reveals a changing legal relationship between hereditary power, popular sovereignty, ministerial authority and remedies. It does not prove that every later instrument immediately achieved all its stated purposes. Constitutionalism depends on implementation as well as textual design. Nepal's historical experience is therefore useful for distinguishing constitutional promises from effective limits, and legal continuity from constitutional change. A strong answer explains the verified provisions and clearly marks source limits. Sovereignty, executive responsibility and rights provide the organizing questions, while attention to the applicable instrument prevents present constitutional assumptions from distorting the historical account.
