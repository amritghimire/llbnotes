# Unit 3: Constitutionalism

!!! abstract "In one line"
    Constitutionalism means that government must actually obey meaningful limits on its power.

## Why it matters for the exam

The key distinction is **having a Constitution** versus **governing constitutionally**. Explain what limits power, who enforces the limit and how an ordinary person benefits.

## 3.1 Concept and meaning

A government may possess an impressive written Constitution while disregarding rights, elections or courts. Constitutionalism asks whether the constitutional restraints work. It requires authority to have a legal basis, institutions to remain within their powers and affected people to have meaningful protection.

Dicey's treatment of the rule of law stresses protection against arbitrary power. Montesquieu's *Spirit of Laws*, Book XI, Chapter 6, explains the danger when making, executing and judging law are concentrated. These are contributions to the idea, paraphrased in their historical settings. They are not definitions of every feature of modern Nepal.

### Essential elements

1. **Constitutional authority.** An office holder must identify the source of a power.
2. **Limited power.** Rights and procedures constrain how the power is used.
3. **Divided functions.** Institutions can restrain misuse by other institutions.
4. **Independent justice.** Courts can decide legal disputes without taking instructions from the executive.
5. **Responsible government.** Elected representatives and public institutions must answer for their conduct.
6. **Participation and inclusion.** People need a meaningful place in political life, including those historically excluded.
7. **Effective remedies.** A legal right needs a practical route to protection.

**Example.** A government announces a restriction on peaceful expression. Constitutionalism asks whether the restriction has lawful authority, fits the specific constitutional limitation and can be challenged before an independent court. Official convenience is not the whole answer.

Constitutionalism differs from obedience to every existing rule without inquiry. A rule may itself be unconstitutional. Article 1 allows its validity to be tested against the superior framework.

## 3.2 Forms of constitutionalism

The syllabus gives no closed list of forms. The following are **analytical approaches**, which can overlap. They are not separate legal categories established by an Article.

| Approach | Central concern | Risk if treated alone |
|---|---|---|
| Liberal | Rights and restraints on interference | Formal liberty may overlook unequal access to protection |
| Democratic or political | Elections, legislative scrutiny and public accountability | A majority may dominate minorities or weaken scrutiny |
| Legal or judicial | Enforceable higher law and independent courts | Courts cannot replace every political decision or administrative task |
| Social or welfare | Positive measures and institutions addressing disadvantage | Policy promises may remain unenforced without resources and law |
| Federal | Constitutionally distributed territorial power | Division can produce disputes unless coordination and remedies work |

Nepal combines these concerns. Part 3 protects rights. Article 74 provides pluralist parliamentary governance. Articles 56–60 distribute territorial and financial authority. Articles 49–55 address principles, policies and obligations. Article 55 restricts judicial questioning of whether matters in Part 4 have been implemented, so do not promise the same direct remedy as for every fundamental-rights claim.

### Constitution, constitutionalism and constitutional morality

The Constitution is the instrument. Constitutionalism is limited and accountable government under it. Constitutional morality is an explanatory term for conduct faithful to the constitutional framework and its values. It is not a free-standing permission to replace text with personal preference.

## Compare

| Constitution | Constitutionalism |
|---|---|
| A fundamental framework exists | The framework effectively limits public authority |
| Describes rights and institutions | Asks whether rights and institutional limits are respected |
| Can be read as a legal document | Must also be evaluated through conduct and enforcement |
| Its existence is necessary in a constitutional order | Its practical success cannot be inferred from existence alone |

## Full 20-mark answers

??? example "Q1. What is constitutionalism? Explain its elements and significance in Nepal. (20 marks)"
    **Introduction**

    Constitutionalism is the idea that public power must be effectively limited and exercised through accountable institutions. It is concerned with the quality of government under a constitutional framework, rather than the mere existence of a document bearing that name. A Constitution can promise rights while political actors disregard them. Constitutionalism asks whether those promises restrain conduct and protect people in practice. Its significance in Nepal lies in connecting popular sovereignty with legal limits, responsible government and enforceable rights. Public authority exists to govern within the constitutional order, not to free office holders from it.

    **Intellectual meaning**

    Different historical accounts explain parts of this idea. Dicey's discussion of the rule of law stresses the danger of arbitrary power and the importance of legal protection. Montesquieu explains why liberty is threatened when legislative, executive and judicial functions are concentrated. Their contributions should be paraphrased accurately rather than presented as invented modern definitions. Together they show that constitutionalism requires more than efficient commands. It requires an arrangement through which power can be justified, questioned and restrained. Nepal's particular answer must nevertheless come from its own constitutional provisions and their lawful interpretation.

    **Constitutional authority and supremacy**

    The first element is a legal basis for authority. Article 2 vests sovereignty and state power in the people and requires their exercise under the Constitution. An official therefore cannot rely simply on personal status or political popularity. Article 1 supplies the superior legal standard by declaring the Constitution fundamental law. Inconsistent legislation is void to the extent of inconsistency. This is crucial because constitutional limits would be ineffective if ordinary authorities could remove them whenever challenged. Supremacy requires both recognition of the superior rule and institutions capable of applying it to disputed conduct.

    **Rights as limits on government**

    Fundamental rights protect people against constitutionally impermissible action and support conditions for dignified public life. They are not merely permissions granted at the government's convenience. Their precise scope, beneficiaries and limitations must be read from Part 3. Article 46 provides constitutional remedies, while Articles 133 and 144 contain important judicial powers. A right becomes practically meaningful when a person can seek protection without the wrongdoer controlling the decision. At the same time, constitutionalism requires accurate analysis of permitted restrictions. Describing every right as absolute would misstate the framework and obscure the safeguards attached to lawful limitations.

    **Division of functions and judicial independence**

    Another element is institutional division. Lawmaking, administration and adjudication have different constitutional roles. Nepal's parliamentary government includes executive-legislative connections, but this does not abolish all functional limits. Courts exercise judicial authority under Article 126, and the Supreme Court has interpretive authority under Article 128. Judicial independence matters because the legality of public conduct cannot depend entirely on the approval of the official whose conduct is challenged. Independence also involves responsibility. A court must decide legal questions through reasons and within jurisdiction, rather than replacing all elected policy choices with its own preferences.

    **Democratic accountability**

    Constitutionalism requires public power to answer to people and representative institutions. Article 74 adopts pluralist parliamentary government. Articles 76 and 100 connect executive authority with constitutional formation and confidence procedures. Legislative examination, public debate and elections can expose misuse that litigation alone may not prevent. Yet majority support is not unlimited authority. An elected government remains bound by rights and legal procedures. The democratic and legal elements therefore support one another. Public choice gives authority to govern, while constitutional limits protect the conditions under which public choice remains free and meaningful.

    **Inclusion and social conditions**

    Formal legal freedom may have limited value to someone unable to access institutions because of poverty, exclusion or discrimination. Nepal's rights and policies therefore need to be understood alongside commitments to inclusion and social justice. This does not mean every policy aspiration creates the same immediately enforceable claim. Articles 49–55 establish the framework for directive principles, policies and obligations, including the limitation in Article 55. Constitutionalism requires attention to that distinction. It also asks whether government is taking lawful and accountable measures to make the constitutional order useful to people rather than leaving its benefits accessible only to the powerful.

    **Federalism and controlled constitutional change**

    Nepal also limits and organizes power territorially. Articles 56–60 distribute institutional and financial authority among three levels. Territorial division can bring decision making closer to communities, but its success requires cooperation and clear responsibility. Disputes about competence must be resolved through constitutional institutions rather than informal domination. Article 274 likewise channels constitutional change through a special procedure. A living constitutional order needs the ability to respond to change without allowing temporary convenience to erase its fundamental limits. Stability and lawful adaptation are therefore complementary concerns rather than reasons to reject every reform.

    **Practical significance and critical view**

    Consider a restriction on peaceful political expression. A constitutionalist analysis asks for its legal source, its connection to the relevant constitutional limitation and the availability of an independent remedy. Merely asserting public interest does not answer those questions. The same approach applies to appointments, expenditure and institutional disputes. However, written safeguards do not guarantee compliance. Delay, unequal access, weak scrutiny and unwillingness to obey decisions can undermine the design. These are reasons to strengthen implementation and accountability, not reasons to abandon legal limits. Effective constitutionalism requires both sound institutions and constitutional conduct by those who operate them.

    A useful way to test constitutionalism is to follow a disputed decision from its legal source to its consequences. Identify who made the decision, the power invoked, the procedure used and the protection available to those affected. Then ask whether a successful challenge would actually change the position. This does not reduce constitutionalism to litigation alone. It shows how legal authority, political responsibility and compliance connect. An institution that can state a constitutional principle but cannot apply it provides only part of the required protection.

    **Conclusion**

    Constitutionalism means limited, responsible and rights-respecting government in practice. In Nepal it rests on constitutional supremacy, popular sovereignty, fundamental rights, responsible parliamentary institutions, independent courts and distributed power. Its legal foundations must be explained through specific provisions rather than general praise of democracy. The decisive question is whether a person affected by public power can demand a lawful justification and obtain protection where the limit has been crossed. A Constitution provides the framework. Constitutionalism requires that the framework continue to govern the officials, institutions and majorities entrusted with power.

??? example "Q2. Distinguish a Constitution from constitutionalism and assess the different forms of constitutionalism. (20 marks)"
    **Introduction**

    A Constitution is a fundamental arrangement of institutions, powers and rights. Constitutionalism is the effective limitation and accountable exercise of power under that arrangement. The difference explains why the presence of a detailed written document does not prove that a government observes constitutional restraints. To assess constitutionalism, one must examine rights, institutions, participation and remedies together. Its different forms are best understood as overlapping approaches to these concerns. They are not separate constitutional boxes into which every country must fit exclusively. Nepal illustrates how legal, democratic, social and federal commitments can operate within one constitutional framework.

    **The document and the practice**

    The Constitution states the rules by which authority is established and exercised. Constitutionalism asks whether those rules meaningfully control the holders of authority. A clause recognizing judicial independence may exist on paper, but its practical value depends on courts being able to decide without improper interference. A right to seek a remedy likewise depends on accessible procedures and obedience to lawful orders. Thus the distinction does not reduce the importance of the written instrument. Instead, it identifies what the instrument is intended to achieve. Legal recognition and effective protection are connected but analytically different questions.

    **Liberal constitutionalism**

    The liberal approach emphasizes the protection of individual freedom against arbitrary interference. Rights, legality, institutional restraints and independent adjudication are central. Dicey's account of the rule of law and Montesquieu's warning against concentrated powers help explain this concern. Their historical arguments do not exhaust modern constitutionalism, but they show why unchecked authority threatens liberty. A Nepal application would examine whether official action respects Part 3 and whether a remedy exists under the relevant constitutional provisions. The limitation of a purely formal approach is that equal words may offer unequal practical protection where people face very different social conditions.

    **Democratic and political constitutionalism**

    A democratic approach emphasizes elections, public reasoning, legislative scrutiny and political responsibility. Government should not remain accountable only to officials or legal experts. People need opportunities to choose representatives and challenge public decisions through political institutions. Article 74's pluralist parliamentary framework and Article 100's confidence procedures support this aspect in Nepal. However, political accountability cannot mean that a parliamentary majority may do anything it wishes. If a majority destroys opposition rights or disables effective scrutiny, it undermines the conditions of democratic choice itself. Constitutionalism therefore needs participation together with limits on how representative authority is exercised.

    **Legal and judicial constitutionalism**

    The legal approach gives particular weight to higher law and enforceable remedies. Independent courts can examine whether public institutions have acted within constitutional limits. In Nepal, Articles 1, 128 and 133 explain important parts of this arrangement. Judicial protection is especially significant where political channels fail an unpopular individual or minority. Yet a court is not an alternative administration for every policy problem. Legal authority, evidence and jurisdiction remain necessary. The strength of judicial constitutionalism lies in reasoned enforcement of constitutional limits, while its weakness appears if legal proceedings become inaccessible or are expected to resolve every disagreement without political responsibility.

    **Social and welfare constitutionalism**

    A social approach recognizes that freedom also depends on material and institutional conditions. People who lack education, health, security or equal participation may be unable to use formal rights effectively. Nepal's constitutional commitments therefore extend beyond a narrow prohibition on state interference. Part 3 and Part 4 must nevertheless be read carefully because their provisions do not all have identical enforcement structures. Article 55 restricts judicial questioning about implementation of matters in Part 4. The distinction does not make those policies meaningless, but directs attention toward legislation, budgets, reporting and political accountability as well as the particular legal remedies available.

    **Federal constitutionalism**

    Federal constitutionalism controls power through a territorial distribution of authority. Different levels govern within the constitutional allocation rather than depending entirely on the centre's ordinary permission. Nepal's Articles 56–60 and Schedules 5–9 establish important parts of this design. The approach can support local participation and allow diverse communities a closer role in government. Its practical difficulty is that public problems often cross boundaries or involve shared subjects. Constitutionalism therefore requires coordination and dispute resolution, not merely additional offices. Territorial division fails its purpose if responsibility becomes so unclear that every level can blame another for inaction.

    **How the approaches fit together**

    These approaches need not compete as exclusive alternatives. Rights can protect meaningful political participation. Democratic institutions can supply the legislation and resources needed to implement social commitments. Judicial review can protect the constitutional allocation of federal powers. Federal institutions can increase opportunities for public accountability. The system becomes weaker when one concern is used to cancel all the others. For example, an appeal to electoral victory cannot justify ignoring rights, and an appeal to rights cannot justify courts taking over every elected decision. Constitutionalism requires institutions to perform their own roles while respecting the legal position of others.

    **Nepal's position and critical assessment**

    Nepal's Constitution combines popular sovereignty, constitutional supremacy, rights, parliamentary responsibility and territorial division. These commitments justify describing its constitutionalism as multidimensional rather than purely judicial or purely political. Their practical success remains a matter of implementation. A court order without compliance, an election without free debate or a policy promise without an accountable programme provides incomplete protection. The appropriate critical question is therefore specific. Which safeguard is failing, who has a constitutional duty to act, and what legal or political mechanism can address the failure? This approach is more useful than declaring the entire Constitution successful or unsuccessful from one institutional feature.

    A simple institutional example shows this overlap. A representative chamber may enact a programme responding to disadvantage, administrators may implement it under law and courts may examine a properly raised rights challenge. The democratic, social and legal approaches then address different aspects of the same programme. No single institution must absorb every function for constitutionalism to operate. The important requirement is that each decision maker can identify its authority, respect the relevant limits and answer through the appropriate constitutional mechanism.

    **Conclusion**

    A Constitution provides the fundamental framework, while constitutionalism concerns government that remains effectively limited by that framework. Liberal, democratic, legal, social and federal approaches each identify an important part of this requirement. Nepal's provisions combine these concerns, although their remedies and institutional methods differ. A complete assessment must therefore connect the constitutional text with responsible conduct and practical protection. The existence of a document is the beginning of the inquiry. The continuing ability of people and institutions to demand lawful, accountable government is the stronger test of constitutionalism.

## Test yourself

??? question "Can a state have a written Constitution without effective constitutionalism?"
    Yes. Institutions may disregard its limits or make remedies ineffective.

??? question "Does limited government mean no public welfare activity?"
    No. Government can provide services through lawful powers and accountable institutions.

??? question "Why is majority support insufficient to justify every act?"
    The majority remains subject to constitutional rights, powers and procedures.

??? question "Are all directive policies enforceable like fundamental rights?"
    No. Read Articles 49–55, including Article 55's limitation, separately from Part 3 remedies.

??? question "Are the forms listed here mutually exclusive?"
    No. They are overlapping analytical approaches, not an exhaustive statutory classification.
