# Unit 9: Fundamental rights

!!! abstract "In one line"
    Fundamental rights protect people through the Constitution, while constitutional remedies give courts authority to respond to violations.

## Why it matters for the exam

Connect the history and purpose of rights with the actual protections in Part 3. Explain who holds a right, its conditions and its remedy. A list without those distinctions is incomplete.

## 9.1 Origin and development

Rights developed through challenges to unchecked authority, constitutional guarantees and international recognition. This is a history of different legal protections, not a claim that every earlier instrument protected everyone equally.

| Stage | Contribution | Qualification |
|---|---|---|
| Earlier limits on rulers | Claims that power must observe law and accepted restraints | Often protected particular groups rather than universal equality |
| US Bill of Rights, ratified 1791 | Constitutional guarantees concerning freedoms and legal process | Read its own historical and constitutional context |
| Universal Declaration of Human Rights, 1948 | A universal standard of human dignity and rights | A declaration is not identical to a domestic constitutional remedy |
| International Covenants adopted in 1966 | Treaty frameworks for civil and political rights and economic, social and cultural rights | Treaty obligations and domestic enforcement require their own legal analysis |
| Nepal's constitutional development | Earlier rights provisions followed by the broader 2015 framework | Keep each historical text and amendment edition distinct |

The US National Archives records ratification of the first ten amendments on 15 December 1791. UN records identify adoption of the UDHR on 10 December 1948 and both Covenants on 16 December 1966. Nepal's earlier provisions are discussed in [Unit 7](unit-07-constitutional-history.md). Do not attribute modern universal protection to every earlier rights instrument.

## 9.2 Importance of rights and duties

Rights protect dignity, restrain arbitrary power and support participation. Political freedom helps people question government. Equality challenges unjustified exclusion. Education, health and other social rights address conditions necessary to use freedom meaningfully.

Duties recognize responsibility toward the constitutional community. Article 48 lists citizens' duties. A person does not lose every right merely because an official accuses them of failing a duty. Rights and duties must both be applied through the relevant law, not through unofficial punishment.

Article 47 required necessary legal provisions for implementing these rights within three years of commencement. That deadline does not establish that every implementing law was enacted or that every right automatically expired when the period ended.

### Citizens' duties under Article 48

Citizens must safeguard nationality, sovereignty and integrity while remaining loyal to the nation; obey the Constitution and law; render compulsory service when the State requires it; and protect public property. Do not confuse these duties with the state's Part 4 obligations.

### How to analyze a restriction

Identify the right and its holder. Then read the specific limitation and ask whether a lawful measure satisfies it. Article 17's restrictions are not a blanket permission to limit any right for any reason. Equality's protective-measures proviso is not arbitrary favour. Article 273 also preserves specified rights during emergency, as [Unit 17](unit-17-emergency-powers.md) explains.

## 9.4 Constitutional remedies

Article 46 connects Part 3 rights to Articles 133 and 144. The Supreme Court has important review and extraordinary jurisdiction under Article 133; the High Court has jurisdiction under Article 144. Article 133(2) distinguishes fundamental rights, other legal rights with absent or inadequate remedies, and constitutional or legal public-interest disputes. Do not reduce all those categories to the same test.

| Writ | Typical purpose | What must be established |
|---|---|---|
| Habeas corpus | Test detention | The legal basis and conditions of custody |
| Mandamus | Require a public duty | A legally required act, not merely a preferred policy |
| Certiorari | Set aside a defective decision | An appropriate legal ground and jurisdiction |
| Prohibition | Prevent unauthorized proceedings | A threatened or continuing exercise beyond lawful authority |
| Quo warranto | Test entitlement to public office | The legal qualifications and authority for holding that office |

A writ is not an automatic substitute for every appeal. Read the jurisdiction, facts and applicable procedure. For the procedural detail already taught in the series, see [writ, review and revision](../procedural-law/unit-07-writ-review-revision.md).

## Nepal's law

Part 3, Articles 16–48, is the principal rights-and-duties source. Read remedies with Articles 133 and 144, compliance with Article 126(2), and emergency protections with Article 273. Specific statutory claims require the actual implementing Act; no unchecked section number is supplied here.

## Full 20-mark answers

??? example "Q1. Explain the development, importance and constitutional protection of fundamental rights in Nepal. (20 marks)"
    **Introduction**

    Fundamental rights protect people through the superior constitutional framework. Their importance lies in recognizing dignity and placing legal limits on the exercise of public power. They also support the conditions through which people can participate in political and social life. A complete account therefore explains both their historical development and their legal operation. Nepal's Constitution of 2015 contains a broad rights framework in Part 3, including freedoms, equality and social protections. Its significance cannot be understood by counting rights alone. The beneficiaries, limitations, implementation duties and remedies determine what each guarantee means for the person seeking protection.

    **Historical development**

    Rights developed through different challenges to unchecked authority and demands for legal protection. Earlier restraints often benefited particular groups and should not be mistaken for modern universal equality. The United States Bill of Rights, ratified in 1791, provides an example of constitutional protection through amendments. International development later included the Universal Declaration of Human Rights adopted in 1948 and the two major Covenants adopted in 1966. These instruments have different legal forms. A declaration, treaty and domestic constitutional guarantee cannot automatically be treated as identical sources of a court remedy. Their connection is the growing recognition of human dignity and constraints on power.

    **Nepal's constitutional development**

    Nepal's earlier constitutional instruments also contained rights provisions, although their qualifications and institutional protection differed. The 1948 document's promises, the rights and remedies of the 1959 Constitution, and the 1990 and interim frameworks belong to their own legal settings. The present Constitution is a later framework rather than a simple renumbering of every earlier rule. Historical comparison is useful because it distinguishes the statement of a right from effective constitutional restraint. It also prevents a judgment applying an old provision from being described as if it originally applied the 2015 Constitution. The current legal answer must begin with the current instrument being studied.

    **Liberty and protection against arbitrary power**

    Article 16 recognizes life with dignity, and Article 17 addresses personal liberty and specified freedoms. These protections matter because government could otherwise silence disagreement or interfere with liberty solely through official preference. Constitutional analysis nevertheless requires the particular provision and its conditions. Not every freedom has an identical limitation, and a general claim of public interest does not replace the required legal justification. The right holder must also be identified correctly. Some protections belong to every person, while others are expressly given to citizens. Accurate analysis preserves these distinctions instead of turning the rights chapter into a collection of unrestricted slogans.

    **Equality and protection against exclusion**

    Article 18 provides equality before law and equal protection, alongside its provisions concerning discrimination and authorized protective measures. Article 24 separately addresses untouchability and discrimination in public and private places. Other Articles recognize specific protections for women, children, Dalit, senior citizens and groups identified for social justice or security. These provisions show that equality includes attention to the actual conditions in which people participate. A constitutionally authorized measure responding to disadvantage is not necessarily arbitrary privilege. Conversely, a government cannot justify favouritism simply by describing it as inclusion. The measure must fit the relevant constitutional and legal authority.

    **Justice and safeguards against coercion**

    Articles 20–23 address important protections concerning accusation, trial, victims, torture and preventive detention. Article 20 includes the presumption of innocence and fair-trial safeguards. Their value is practical. A person facing state coercion needs a process in which an accusation is examined rather than automatically accepted. Exact clauses and exceptions remain important, including the production-before-authority provision. Crime victims also possess constitutional interests, so justice should not be described solely through the position of the accused. The framework seeks legal protection through independent institutions rather than allowing the executive alone to determine the consequences of its own allegations.

    **Social and economic conditions**

    The Constitution recognizes rights concerning education, employment, labour, health, food and housing. These protections reflect the fact that formal freedom can be difficult to use without basic social conditions. Their legal structures differ. Some provisions expressly refer to arrangements under law, and the precise duty cannot be inferred from the name of a right alone. Article 47 required necessary legal provisions for implementation within three years of commencement. That requirement is significant, but does not itself prove that every implementing measure was completed. Nor does it say that a right disappears if implementation is delayed. The constitutional duty and evidence of compliance are separate questions.

    **Responsibilities of citizens**

    Rights coexist with duties under Article 48. Citizens must safeguard nationality, sovereignty and integrity, obey the Constitution and law, render compulsory service when the State requires it and protect public property. These duties recognize responsibility within the constitutional community. They do not give an official an unrestricted power to withdraw rights from a person accused of disobedience. Any legal consequence needs the relevant authority and procedure. Duties must also be distinguished from the state's Part 4 obligations. Confusing the two could shift a governmental responsibility onto citizens or treat a citizen's alleged failure as a complete excuse for unconstitutional official conduct.

    **Remedies and implementation**

    Article 46 provides the right to constitutional remedies through Articles 133 and 144. Judicial authority matters because a guarantee needs a response when violated. Available writs include habeas corpus, mandamus, certiorari, prohibition and quo warranto in appropriate cases. Their purposes differ, and jurisdiction must be established. Article 126(2) also requires compliance with judicial orders and decisions. Implementation therefore involves more than printing rights or allowing petitions to be filed. A legally justified claim should be capable of producing effective protection. Accessible procedures and lawful institutional conduct are necessary if the constitutional framework is to matter to ordinary people.

    **Critical view and conclusion**

    Nepal's rights framework combines restraints on coercion with commitments addressing exclusion and social conditions. Its breadth is valuable, but practical protection requires legislation where necessary, functioning institutions and compliance with lawful remedies. A right should not be overstated as absolute, dismissed because implementation is difficult or confused with a policy that has a different enforcement structure. The strongest account connects each constitutional guarantee to its beneficiary, limitation and remedy. Fundamental rights protect dignity and participation by making public power answerable to superior law, while duties support responsible membership of the constitutional community within that same legal framework.

??? example "Q2. Discuss constitutional remedies and explain how rights and duties operate together. (20 marks)"
    **Introduction**

    A constitutional right needs a practical method of protection. Without an institution able to examine a violation and give an effective response, the guarantee may remain only a statement. Constitutional remedies connect rights with judicial authority. Nepal's Article 46 links enforcement of Part 3 rights to Articles 133 and 144. Citizens also have duties under Article 48, but those duties do not remove the need for lawful protection and fair process. The relationship is therefore one of rights, responsibility and legally controlled remedies. It is not a system in which officials may punish alleged disobedience by deciding that a person no longer has rights.

    **The constitutional basis**

    Article 46 provides a right to obtain constitutional remedies in the manner stated in Articles 133 or 144 for enforcement of Part 3 rights. The Supreme Court and High Courts therefore have important but specifically defined constitutional roles. Their authority must be explained through the relevant jurisdiction rather than a general claim that every public complaint permits any requested order. Article 1's constitutional supremacy gives the framework its legal importance. A dispute may concern the validity of the rule itself, not merely whether an official followed it. Remedy analysis begins by identifying both the right claimed and the constitutional authority through which protection is sought.

    **The Supreme Court's jurisdiction**

    Article 133(1) addresses review of inconsistent laws in the circumstances stated there. Article 133(2) concerns enforcement of fundamental rights, certain other legal rights and constitutional or legal questions in disputes of public interest or concern. These categories must be read carefully. The reference to absent, inadequate or ineffective remedies in the provision should not be turned into an identical requirement for every possible category without analysis. Article 133(3) identifies important writ powers and includes a specific limitation concerning legislative internal and privilege proceedings, except on absence of jurisdiction. Constitutional review is powerful, but its text also supplies boundaries.

    **Habeas corpus**

    Habeas corpus tests the legality of detention. Its focus is the authority and legal conditions supporting custody, rather than an assumption that every arrested person must be released without examination. The remedy is important because loss of liberty places the affected person under immediate coercive control. A court can require the detention to be legally justified through the appropriate proceeding. Article 273 separately protects the right to seek habeas corpus during emergency. This reinforces its constitutional importance while requiring attention to the precise issue. A lawful detention and an unlawful one cannot be distinguished merely by the detainee's political popularity or the official's description of the person.

    **Mandamus and the enforcement of duty**

    Mandamus requires performance of a public legal duty in an appropriate case. The claimant needs to identify the duty and the authority responsible for it. A preference that government adopt a particular policy is not automatically a mandatory duty. This distinction makes the remedy useful without turning the court into the administrator of every programme. A hypothetical example is an authority refusing to carry out a legally required step while denying that any decision is necessary. The legal question concerns the actual duty, its conditions and the appropriate direction. The court's response must follow the established legal obligation rather than the claimant's preferred outcome alone.

    **Certiorari and prohibition**

    Certiorari and prohibition address legally defective exercises of authority in different ways. Certiorari can set aside an appropriate challenged decision, while prohibition can prevent proceedings beyond lawful authority. The distinction is useful, but should not become a mechanical rule that ignores jurisdiction or the nature of the dispute. Neither remedy means that every disagreement with a decision is a constitutional wrong. The claimant must identify a legal defect and an appropriate basis for intervention. This prevents extraordinary remedies from being described as unrestricted additional appeals and preserves the difference between correction of legal authority and reconsideration of every factual or policy preference.

    **Quo warranto and public office**

    Quo warranto tests the legal entitlement to hold a public office. The relevant questions include the nature of the office, its legal qualifications and the authority through which it is occupied. Personal dislike of the office holder is insufficient. This remedy connects public appointments with the rule that authority must have a legal basis. It can protect institutional integrity without assuming that courts make every appointment themselves. A proper answer therefore identifies the legal condition allegedly absent and explains why the claimant's challenge falls within the court's authority. Unsupported allegations about competence or political disagreement do not replace that legal analysis.

    **Rights beyond formal court access**

    Judicial availability is only one part of effective protection. A person may face practical barriers of cost, delay or distance. Article 20(10) recognizes free legal aid for an indigent party in accordance with law, while Article 126(2) requires compliance with court orders and decisions. These provisions illustrate why accessibility and implementation matter together. A favourable order that is not obeyed may leave the violation continuing. At the same time, a discussion of access should not invent statutory eligibility limits or procedural deadlines that have not been checked. Constitutional principles guide the inquiry, while the applicable laws supply the specific procedural requirements.

    **Duties within the rights framework**

    Article 48 states four citizens' duties concerning national integrity, obedience to the Constitution and law, compulsory service when required by the State and protection of public property. These duties support a community in which rights can be exercised responsibly. They do not allow an official to replace adjudication with an accusation. A person alleged to have damaged public property still has the protection of applicable criminal-justice guarantees. The State may pursue lawful consequences through the proper process. It may not assume that the accusation itself removes the presumption of innocence, protection against torture or the right to an available constitutional remedy.

    **Critical assessment and conclusion**

    Constitutional remedies make rights legally consequential by giving courts defined powers to examine violations and provide appropriate protection. Their value depends on accurate jurisdictional analysis, access and compliance. Duties reinforce constitutional responsibility but remain within the same legal order that protects individuals. Nepal's framework should therefore be explained through the distinct purposes of the five writs, the authority of the relevant court and the relationship between lawful responsibility and continuing rights. A strong answer neither promises a writ for every grievance nor treats duties as a general exception to liberty. It shows how constitutional law controls both public power and the means by which protection is obtained.

## Test yourself

??? question "Are all rights in Part 3 limited to citizens?"
    No. The wording differs. Identify whether the specific Article protects every person, citizens or a defined group.

??? question "What does Article 47's three-year requirement prove?"
    It establishes an implementation duty. It does not prove completion or automatically end rights after three years.

??? question "Which Article connects rights with remedies?"
    Article 46, read with Articles 133 and 144.

??? question "Can an alleged breach of duty justify torture?"
    No. Duties do not authorize officials to disregard constitutional protections.

??? question "What is the difference between mandamus and a preferred policy?"
    Mandamus requires an established legal duty. A preferred policy is not automatically such a duty.

??? question "Does emergency eliminate habeas corpus?"
    No. Article 273(10) expressly preserves the right to seek that remedy.

## One-minute revision

- Rights developed through different domestic and international legal forms.
- Part 3 protects liberty, equality, participation and social conditions.
- Read the beneficiary, limit and remedy of each Article.
- Article 47 requires implementation; it is not an expiry clause.
- Article 48 lists citizens' duties.
- Articles 46, 133 and 144 connect rights with judicial protection.
- A duty allegation does not erase fair process or other rights.
