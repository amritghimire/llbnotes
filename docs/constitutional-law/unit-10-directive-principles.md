# Unit 10: Directive principles, policies and obligations

!!! abstract "In one line"
    Part 4 tells the State what to work toward and how to account for progress, while distinguishing policy implementation from an individual rights remedy.

## Why it matters for the exam

Distinguish principles, policies, obligations and citizens' duties. Explain Articles 49–55 as a complete arrangement, including reporting and monitoring. Do not stop at saying that the provisions are not directly enforceable.

**Prescribed cases used here:** [Surya Prasad Dhungel](cases/18-5-surya-prasad-dhungel.md) (18.5, stub). Stubs have no verified text yet.

## 10.1 Constitutional status and enforcement techniques

### What Part 4 requires

Article 49 makes the principles, policies and obligations guidelines for governance. The State must mobilize, or cause to be mobilized, resources and means for implementation. Their constitutional status is stronger than an ordinary party manifesto, but their enforcement structure differs from Part 3.

Article 50 identifies four broad objectives.

| Clause | Objective | Plain explanation |
|---|---|---|
| 50(1) | Political | Welfare governance through rights, inclusion, participation, justice and the federal democratic order |
| 50(2) | Social and cultural | An egalitarian society free from discrimination and exploitation, respecting diversity and social harmony |
| 50(3) | Economic | Sustainable development through public, private and cooperative participation and fair distribution |
| 50(4) | International relations | Sovereign equality and protection of independence, integrity and national interests |

Article 51 identifies thirteen policy fields. Learn the fields and an example of their purpose, not an invented promise that each produces an immediate personal entitlement.

| Clause | Policy field |
|---|---|
| (a) | National unity and security |
| (b) | Political and governance system |
| (c) | Social and cultural transformation |
| (d) | Economy, industry and commerce |
| (e) | Agriculture and land reform |
| (f) | Development |
| (g) | Protection, promotion and use of natural resources |
| (h) | Citizens' basic needs |
| (i) | Labour and employment |
| (j) | Social justice and inclusion |
| (k) | Justice and the penal system |
| (l) | Tourism |
| (m) | International relations |

These fields contain detailed directions. For example, Article 51(h) addresses basic needs, including education and health, while Article 51(g) addresses resources and environmental protection. A programme should be connected to the actual clause before its constitutional purpose is asserted.

Article 52 states the State's obligations, including protecting rights and pursuing the constitutional direction of a prosperous and progressive Nepal. It is not the list of citizens' duties, which is in Article 48.

### Enforcement through institutions

Article 53 requires an annual government report on steps taken and achievements in implementation. It goes to the President, who causes it to be laid before the Federal Parliament through the Prime Minister. Article 54 provides a parliamentary committee, in accordance with law, to monitor and evaluate progressive implementation.

Practical techniques include legislation, lawful budgeting, programmes, administrative measures, publication of progress and parliamentary examination. These are methods of constitutional implementation, not a claim that any proposed programme is automatically authorized or funded.

### The judicial limit

Article 55 says that no question shall be raised in any court about whether a matter in Part 4 has been implemented. This is a specific limit, not permission to ignore the Constitution generally. A separate Part 3 right must be analyzed on its own basis. Do not disguise a pure Part 4 implementation demand as an automatic rights claim; equally, do not dismiss a genuine rights claim merely because a related policy also appears in Part 4.

**Example.** Asking why a development policy has not been fully implemented raises a Part 4 issue. Challenging a particular discriminatory public decision under Article 18 raises a distinct equality question. The actual facts, claim and remedy determine the analysis.

## 10.2 Comparison of directive principles, policies and fundamental duties

This heading preserves the syllabus's reference to fundamental duties even though the unit title also includes state obligations.

| Point | Principles and policies | State obligations | Citizens' duties |
|---|---|---|---|
| Main provisions | Articles 49–51 | Article 52, within Part 4 | Article 48, in Part 3 |
| Main addressee | State institutions | State | Citizens |
| Function | Objectives and directions for governance | Constitutional responsibilities of the State | Responsibility toward the nation, law, service and public property |
| Implementation | Laws, budgets, programmes and accountability | State measures and Part 4 processes | Conduct and relevant implementing law |
| Enforcement caution | Article 55 applies | Article 55 applies to Part 4 | No general permission for unofficial punishment |

A fundamental right is a different category. Article 46 supplies remedies for Part 3 rights; it does not make every objective in Article 51 directly enforceable as an identical personal claim.

## Nepal's law

Read Articles 49–55 together, then compare Article 48 and the relevant Part 3 right. Article 53's annual report and Article 54's committee are the Constitution's express accountability mechanisms. Their existence does not establish that a particular report has actually been submitted or reviewed.

## Compare

| Nepal | India |
|---|---|
| Part 4, Articles 49–55 | Part IV, including Article 37 |
| Article 55 states the limit on court questions about implementation | Article 37 states that the principles are not enforceable by courts while fundamental in governance |
| Reporting and monitoring expressly appear in Articles 53–54 | Check India's own provisions instead of assuming identical machinery |
| Citizens' duties in Article 48 | Fundamental duties in Article 51A |

The comparison concerns constitutional design, not a claim that foreign enforcement doctrine automatically governs Nepal.

## Full 20-mark answers

??? example "Q1. Explain the constitutional status and implementation of Nepal's directive principles, policies and obligations. (20 marks)"
    **Introduction**

    Directive principles, policies and obligations state the constitutional purposes that public institutions should pursue. They connect governmental authority with welfare, equality, development and responsible public action. Their importance cannot be assessed simply by asking whether a person can obtain an immediate court order for every policy objective. Nepal's Constitution gives Part 4 a distinct arrangement of guidance, resource mobilization, reporting, monitoring and a judicial limit. A complete answer must explain that arrangement as a whole. The provisions are neither ordinary campaign promises nor identical to every individual fundamental right protected through Part 3 remedies.

    **Guidelines for governance**

    Article 49 establishes the role of Part 4 as guidance for governance and requires mobilization of resources and means for implementation. This supplies a constitutional direction to institutions exercising public power. An administration should therefore connect its policies and programmes with the framework rather than treat public resources as available solely for political convenience. However, guidance does not erase competence and procedure. A desirable programme still needs lawful authority, proper financial arrangements and compliance with other constitutional provisions. The policy objective explains why government should act, while the rest of the legal framework determines how the particular measure may lawfully be carried out.

    **Political objectives**

    Article 50(1) places welfare governance within a framework of rights, equality, inclusion, participation and social justice. It also connects the federal democratic republican order with local autonomy and cooperative federalism. These matters are related. Public participation can improve accountability, while legal rights protect those who question officials or lack political influence. The provision therefore cannot be summarized as development at any cost. It directs governance toward public purposes within a constitutional political order. An answer should show that relationship rather than listing democracy, welfare and inclusion as unrelated words with no effect on the responsibility of public institutions.

    **Social and economic objectives**

    Article 50(2) concerns an egalitarian society, removal of discrimination and exploitation, cultural diversity and social harmony. Article 50(3) addresses economic development through participation of public, private and cooperative sectors and a fairer distribution of achievements. These provisions recognize that constitutional government concerns social conditions as well as institutional arrangements. Their direction does not automatically dictate one lawful policy for every economic problem. Different measures may be debated, but government should be able to explain how its decisions serve the constitutional purpose and remain within legal limits. The existence of policy choice does not make constitutional direction meaningless.

    **International direction and detailed policies**

    Article 50(4) addresses international relations through sovereign equality and protection of national independence, integrity and interests. Article 51 then gives more detailed directions across thirteen policy fields. These include national security, governance, cultural transformation, the economy, land reform, development, natural resources, basic needs, labour, inclusion, justice, tourism and international relations. Their breadth makes accurate identification important. A programme should be linked to the particular constitutional policy it is said to implement. Merely calling an action a national policy does not establish that it satisfies every applicable requirement or that it can override a separately protected right.

    **State obligations**

    Article 52 places obligations on the State within the Part 4 framework. It connects constitutional direction with protection of rights and progress toward a prosperous and progressive Nepal. This must be distinguished from the citizens' duties under Article 48. Public institutions cannot shift their own constitutional responsibility simply by saying that people should behave responsibly. Likewise, a citizen's duty to obey the law does not authorize unlawful governmental conduct. Distinguishing the addressee makes accountability clearer. It identifies whether the question concerns the State's programme and institutions or a citizen's conduct under the particular legal duty being examined.

    **Legislation, finance and administration**

    Implementation can take place through legislation, budgets, programmes and lawful administrative decisions. A policy concerning education, for example, may require rules about institutions, funding and access. Resource mobilization matters because a stated goal without a practical means of action may remain ineffective. However, the objective does not itself prove that a particular appropriation exists or that an official may spend without legal authorization. The constitutional approach connects purpose with responsibility. Institutions should identify the measure taken, the authority supporting it and the way progress can be assessed. This makes implementation a matter of accountable public work rather than general expressions of goodwill.

    **Reporting and parliamentary monitoring**

    Articles 53 and 54 establish express accountability mechanisms. The government must submit an annual implementation report to the President, who causes it to be laid before the Federal Parliament through the Prime Minister. A parliamentary committee is to monitor and evaluate progressive implementation in accordance with law. The sequence matters because it makes progress a subject of institutional examination. Reporting should identify steps and achievements, while monitoring should assess what those claims show. The constitutional requirement alone does not establish that a particular government has fulfilled it. Evidence of submission and scrutiny remains a separate factual question.

    **Judicial enforcement and its limit**

    Article 55 prevents court questions about whether a matter in Part 4 has been implemented. The limit distinguishes policy implementation from the direct rights-remedy framework. It should neither be ignored nor exaggerated. A separate right under Part 3 still requires its own analysis. For example, an allegation that a particular decision discriminates unlawfully is different from a demand that the court complete every aspect of a development policy. The actual facts, legal basis and requested remedy matter. A claimant cannot automatically bypass Article 55 by attaching a rights label, while an authority cannot defeat a genuine rights claim merely by pointing to a related policy.

    **Critical view and conclusion**

    Part 4 combines constitutional ambition with an implementation structure that relies heavily on government action and parliamentary responsibility. Its effectiveness depends on lawful measures, meaningful reporting and scrutiny of progress. A weak account says either that every policy is immediately enforceable or that the provisions have no significance because courts cannot decide pure implementation questions. Both overlook the design. Articles 49–55 establish objectives, responsibilities and accountability while drawing a specific judicial boundary. Understanding their relationship explains how constitutional direction can guide state action without treating every broad policy commitment as the same kind of personal claim as a fundamental right.

    A policy may help explain why legislation matters, but the claimant must still identify the legal source of the particular remedy requested. This distinction keeps social commitments visible without misstating the court’s constitutional authority.
??? example "Q2. Compare fundamental rights, directive principles, state obligations and citizens' duties. (20 marks)"
    **Introduction**

    The Constitution contains different kinds of commitment because public life involves different kinds of legal responsibility. Fundamental rights protect defined interests of people, directive principles and policies guide governance, state obligations identify public responsibilities and citizens' duties address conduct within the constitutional community. These categories are related, but their addressees and enforcement methods differ. Confusing them can produce serious errors, such as promising a writ for every policy demand or treating an alleged breach of duty as a reason to remove all rights. Nepal's Articles 16–55 provide the framework through which those connections and differences should be explained.

    **The position of fundamental rights**

    Part 3 protects fundamental rights, including liberty, equality, justice and social conditions. The beneficiary and scope of each guarantee depend on the particular Article. Some belong to every person, others to citizens or a specified group. Article 46 provides a right to constitutional remedies through Articles 133 and 144. This gives the rights framework an express judicial dimension. However, a right's name is not a complete legal test. Restrictions, implementing-law references and the facts of the alleged interference still matter. A strong comparison therefore treats fundamental rights as enforceable constitutional protections without inaccurately presenting every one as absolute or identical.

    **The function of directive principles**

    Directive principles identify the broader purposes of constitutional governance. Article 50 addresses political, social and cultural, economic and international objectives. Their importance lies in orienting institutions toward public welfare, justice and responsible exercise of authority. They concern the kind of social and political order the State should work toward. This differs from identifying an immediate individual entitlement in every circumstance. Nevertheless, broad objectives can be significant in evaluating the purposes of laws and programmes. They provide a constitutional standard for public explanation and accountability even where the text does not authorize the same direct judicial remedy as a particular fundamental-rights provision.

    **Policies as more detailed directions**

    Article 51 supplies directions across thirteen policy fields. These range from security and governance to development, resources, basic needs, labour, inclusion and international relations. The difference between principle and policy is useful for learning. A principle states an overarching objective, while a policy gives a more particular field or direction of action. The two work together rather than creating a hierarchy in which any policy may override the rest of the Constitution. A measure said to implement a policy must still have legal authority and comply with rights and other applicable limits. Constitutional purpose does not create unrestricted administrative power.

    **State obligations and citizens' duties**

    Article 52 concerns the State's obligations, while Article 48 concerns duties of citizens. The addressee is the first major difference. A government must not avoid its responsibility by converting a state obligation into a demand that citizens solve the whole problem themselves. Citizens, in turn, have duties to safeguard nationality, sovereignty and integrity, abide by the Constitution and law, render compulsory service when required by the State and protect public property. These responsibilities support constitutional life, but consequences for a particular breach require the relevant legal authority. The text is not a general licence for officials to impose unofficial punishment.

    **Different implementation methods**

    Rights may require restraint, positive action or legislation, depending on their particular provisions. Directive policies commonly require laws, budgets, administrative programmes and assessed progress. Article 49 addresses guidance and mobilization of resources, while Articles 53 and 54 establish reporting and monitoring. Citizens' duties operate through conduct and relevant law. The distinction prevents a false choice between constitutional significance and immediate litigation. A commitment may remain constitutionally important even where its principal accountability mechanism is political or institutional. At the same time, political importance cannot replace an available judicial remedy where a particular right has been violated.

    **The judicial distinction**

    Article 55 limits court questions concerning implementation of matters in Part 4. Article 46, by contrast, provides a remedy for Part 3 rights through the specified jurisdiction. This is a legal difference, not merely a difference in how strongly the provisions are worded. A pure policy implementation demand cannot automatically be turned into an enforceable rights claim by relabelling it. Conversely, a decision that independently violates equality does not become immune merely because its subject also appears in a policy field. The correct method identifies the specific guarantee, the conduct challenged and the remedy requested before deciding what the enforcement limit means.

    **The relationship between freedom and welfare**

    Rights and policies can support one another. Education and health conditions may help people use political and personal freedoms meaningfully. A development programme can serve a policy objective while remaining subject to equality and fair legal treatment. This does not mean every objective produces one compulsory policy choice. It means that institutions should explain how their chosen measures respect the constitutional framework. Resources and priorities may be contested, but the debate takes place within rights and public responsibilities. Treating welfare as a reason to ignore liberty, or liberty as a reason to ignore every social commitment, would oversimplify the Constitution's combined design.

    **Comparison with India**

    India's Article 37 is a useful comparison because it states that directive principles are not enforceable by courts while describing them as fundamental in governance. India's fundamental duties appear in Article 51A. Nepal has its own wording and express reporting and monitoring provisions in Articles 53–54. The comparison illustrates a shared distinction between forms of constitutional commitment, but does not prove that every foreign doctrine governs Nepal. The legal question must return to Nepal's text. Comparative study is strongest when it identifies the similar concern and then explains the differences in constitutional arrangements rather than assuming complete identity.

    **Critical view and conclusion**

    These categories should be distinguished so that responsibility can be enforced through the appropriate mechanism. A citizen retains constitutional protection when accused of failing a duty, and the State remains responsible for lawful public action even where resources make implementation difficult. Fundamental rights, principles, policies, obligations and duties are therefore connected parts of a constitutional order with different legal roles. Nepal's framework combines judicial remedies for rights with institutional and political accountability for policy implementation. A complete answer explains both the difference in enforcement and the common purpose of responsible government that respects dignity, participation and public welfare.

    For example, public discussion of education funding can use Part 4 objectives, while a specific claim to a guaranteed educational entitlement must identify Article 31 and the applicable legal conditions. The two arguments perform different jobs.
