# Unit 11: Federal legislature

!!! abstract "In one line"
    Nepal's two Houses make laws, examine government and exercise constitutional privileges within defined limits.

## Why it matters for the exam

Learn the composition and different terms of the Houses, then explain their powers and limits. Parliamentary privilege and dissolution deserve full reasoning, not the claim that Parliament or the Prime Minister can do anything.

## 11.1 House of Representatives: composition and terms

Article 83 establishes a bicameral Federal Parliament, meaning two Houses. Article 84 provides **275 representatives**: 165 elected from territorial constituencies through first-past-the-post and 110 through proportional representation with the country as a single constituency.

The proportional-list provisions address specified groups, geography, regional balance and representation of persons with disabilities. Article 84(8) requires at least one third of the total elected members from each party representing in the Federal Parliament to be women, with the prescribed proportional-selection mechanism. It is not simply a claim that one third of every individual constituency's candidates must be elected women.

Under Article 85 the ordinary House term is five years unless earlier dissolved according to the Constitution. During emergency a Federal Act may extend it for no more than one year. The extended term ends six months after revocation of the emergency proclamation or order, under Article 85(3).

## 11.2 National Assembly: composition and terms

Article 86 makes the National Assembly permanent with **59 members**. Fifty-six are elected through provincial electoral colleges. From each Province the elected membership includes at least three women, one Dalit and one person with disabilities or from a minority. The college includes Provincial Assembly members and specified rural and municipal office holders, with voting weights determined by federal law.

The President nominates three members on the Government's recommendation, including at least one woman. Members have six-year terms, with one third retiring every two years. The initial allocation used lots for two-, four- and six-year retirement groups. A replacement serves the remainder of the vacant term, not automatically a new six years.

## 11.3 Functions

Parliament makes federal legislation within constitutional competence, authorizes public finance, examines government, debates public matters and performs specified constitutional functions such as amendment, impeachment and hearings. Articles 109–114 govern important legislative processes; Article 110 reserves introduction of Money Bills to the House of Representatives.

The Houses do not have identical roles in every matter. Confidence under Article 100 belongs to the House of Representatives, while constitutional functions may involve both Houses. Ordinary voting under Article 99 differs from special constitutional majorities. Always state which House and which denominator apply.

## 11.4 Privileges

| Protection or power | Constitutional basis | Limit |
|---|---|---|
| Speech and voting protection | 103(1), with the explanation in 103(5) | Applies to the constitutional parliamentary setting, not all speech everywhere |
| Internal business | 103(2) | Expressly subject to the Constitution |
| Protection of proceedings and against intentional distortion | 103(3) | Read its precise scope; it is not a general definition of immunity from all criticism |
| Participation by entitled non-members | 103(4) | Applies the specified protections to those persons |
| Authorized publications | 103(5) | The publication must have the House's authority |
| Protection against arrest during the stated period | 103(6) | Arrest on a criminal charge under federal law remains possible; notify the presiding person forthwith |
| Breach and contempt | 103(7)–(8) | House decision and the specified sanctions and pardon provisions |
| Other privilege matters | 103(9) | Federal law supplies further matters |

Article 103(8) permits admonition, warning, imprisonment up to three months or a fine up to Rs 10,000 following the House's decision. Article 105 restricts discussion affecting pending cases and judges' judicial acts, with its impeachment-debate exception.

Article 133(3)'s proviso generally prevents Supreme Court interference under that clause in internal legislative or privilege proceedings, **except on absence of jurisdiction**. Explain this alongside privilege. Do not promise unrestricted review of every internal irregularity or claim that Parliament is entirely outside constitutional law.

[Tuladhar's historical judgment transcription](https://www.nepalarchives.com/np/content/supreme-court-decision-1197-nepal/) is a source lead on Panchayat privilege and contempt. It concerns the 1962 framework. **Its primary-text verification and exact report citation remain incomplete**, so it does not establish a current Article 103 holding.

## 11.5 Committees

Article 97 provides committees and a joint-committee mechanism for specified functions. A joint committee under clause (2) has a maximum of 25 members, with a five-to-one House of Representatives–National Assembly ratio and inclusion. Committees permit detailed examination that a full chamber may not complete in debate. Their names, membership and particular powers require the applicable law and rules; this page does not invent a current list.

## 11.6 Dissolution

Dissolution ends the House early. Prorogation ends a session. Neither ends the permanent National Assembly. Article 76(7) must be read with government-formation stages, and Article 85 recognizes only dissolution according to the Constitution.

The [official Constitutional Bench report](https://supremecourt.gov.np/publication/materials/101616.pdf) contains Santosh Bhandari and connected petitions, decision 0026, printed pages 29–38, and the separate Deuba proceeding, decision 0028 beginning page 62. The former records restoration of the House after the challenged dissolution was invalidated. Do not merge the two proceedings.

[Raviraj Bhandari, paragraphs 86–87](https://nkp.gov.np/full_detail/8880), provides a historical explanation of reviewable legal questions despite political controversy. It applied the 1990 Constitution. See [Unit 5's dissolution analysis](unit-05-separation-of-powers.md#dissolution-as-a-test-of-institutional-limits).

!!! warning "Unverified"
    The readable original Hari Prasad Nepal judgment remains unavailable. Do not reconstruct its facts, bench or disposition. The Tuladhar mirror is not a verified original. Historical sources cannot be recast as applications of the 2015 Constitution.

## 11.7 Parliamentary control over the executive

Control includes questions and debate, committee scrutiny, financial authorization and Article 100 confidence procedures. Article 100(2) requires a confidence motion within 30 days if the Prime Minister's party splits or a coalition party withdraws support.

One fourth of the then total House membership may table a written no-confidence motion. Article 100(4) bars it during the first two years after appointment and for one year after a failed no-confidence motion. It must name a proposed successor. Adoption requires a majority of the then total membership, followed by appointment under Article 76. These restrictions do not erase the separate mandatory confidence rule in clause (2).

## Nepal's law

Read Articles 76, 83–114 and the Article 133(3) proviso together. Constitutional powers need their own procedures; privilege is not the same as legislative competence or freedom from all criminal law.

## Full 20-mark answers

??? example "Q1. Explain the composition, functions and control powers of Nepal's Federal Parliament. (20 marks)"
    **Introduction**

    Nepal's Federal Parliament is the representative federal legislature established by Article 83. It consists of the House of Representatives and the National Assembly. The two Houses participate in constitutional government through lawmaking, public finance, scrutiny and specified constitutional functions. Their powers are not identical in every field. A complete answer must distinguish composition, institutional continuity and executive responsibility rather than describing Parliament as one undifferentiated body. It must also explain that representative authority operates within the Constitution. Parliament's legal powers and privileges support its work, but do not make every legislative act or internal decision free from constitutional limits.

    **Composition of the House of Representatives**

    Article 84 provides 275 members. Of these, 165 are elected through territorial constituencies using first-past-the-post and 110 through proportional representation with the country treated as one constituency. The mixed arrangement connects territorial representation with a proportional channel for parties and inclusion. The constitutional provisions address specified groups, regional and geographical balance and persons with disabilities. Article 84(8) also requires at least one third of each party's total elected federal parliamentary members to be women through its prescribed mechanism. This requirement should not be inaccurately restated as an identical quota operating separately in every territorial constituency.

    **Composition of the National Assembly**

    Article 86 establishes a permanent House with 59 members. Fifty-six are elected through provincial electoral colleges comprising Provincial Assembly members and specified local office holders, with weighted votes under federal law. Each Province's elected group includes at least three women, one Dalit and one person with disabilities or from a minority. Three further members are nominated by the President on the Government's recommendation, including at least one woman. These provisions give the chamber a distinct representative structure. It should not be described as simply another House elected through the same system and for the same period as the House of Representatives.

    **Terms and institutional continuity**

    The House of Representatives ordinarily lasts five years under Article 85, unless earlier dissolved according to the Constitution. Emergency extension requires a Federal Act and is limited as stated there. The National Assembly, by contrast, is permanent. Its members ordinarily serve six years, with one third retiring every two years. A replacement fills only the remaining period of the vacant term. This difference matters when the other House is dissolved or its term ends. Constitutional continuity is not the same as giving the permanent chamber every ordinary power of the House of Representatives. The particular provision determines what function it may exercise in that situation.

    **Lawmaking and public finance**

    Parliament makes federal law within constitutional competence. Articles 109–114 provide important legislative arrangements, including the distinct treatment of Money Bills. Article 110 reserves their introduction to the House of Representatives. Other legislative matters follow their applicable procedures. Public finance is also a central parliamentary function because administration requires lawful authority to raise and use public resources. These powers should be connected to scrutiny rather than described merely as the ability to pass government proposals. A parliamentary majority remains subject to constitutional rights, the distribution of powers and the procedures that make legislative decisions legally valid.

    **Debate and committee scrutiny**

    Parliament can examine governmental conduct through debate and committee work. Committees permit closer study of information, expenditure and institutional performance than may be possible in a full chamber. Article 97 provides for committees and a joint mechanism for specified functions. A joint committee under clause (2) has a maximum of 25 members and the stated five-to-one membership ratio between the Houses, on an inclusive basis. The Constitution does not itself supply every present committee name or working rule. A particular committee's authority must therefore be checked against the relevant law and rules rather than invented from a general description of parliamentary oversight.

    **Confidence and no confidence**

    Article 100 connects executive survival with the House of Representatives. If the Prime Minister's party splits or a coalition party withdraws support, clause (2) requires a confidence motion within 30 days. A failure to obtain the required majority has the constitutional consequence stated there. A no-confidence motion is a different mechanism. One fourth of the then total House membership may table it in writing, subject to the two-year and one-year restrictions in clause (4). It must name a proposed successor. Adoption requires a majority of the then total membership. The restrictions on no confidence should not be used to erase the separate mandatory confidence trigger.

    **Other constitutional control functions**

    Parliament also performs specified functions concerning impeachment, amendment and hearings. The relevant voting rules differ. Ordinary voting under Article 99 is not the same as a special constitutional majority under Article 101 or Article 274. The competent House and denominator must be stated for each power. Parliamentary control is therefore a set of defined mechanisms rather than a general permission to remove or obstruct any institution at will. Article 76(10)'s ministerial responsibility reinforces this arrangement by connecting executive conduct with institutional accountability. Control works best when representatives examine reasons and evidence instead of treating party support as a substitute for scrutiny.

    **Dissolution and the limits of control**

    Dissolution can remove the representative chamber before its ordinary term and therefore directly affects executive accountability. Articles 76(7) and 85 require constitutional analysis rather than an assumed unlimited parliamentary-system power. The official report of the Santosh Bhandari proceeding records restoration of the House after invalidation of the challenged dissolution. The separate Deuba proceeding must be read in its own setting. Historical Raviraj reasoning also explains why legal questions can remain reviewable despite political controversy, but it arose under the 1990 Constitution. These distinctions prevent political convenience or a foreign convention from replacing the legal conditions governing Nepal's current House.

    **Critical assessment and conclusion**

    Federal Parliament combines representation with lawmaking and responsibility mechanisms. Its effectiveness depends on meaningful examination, accurate procedures and respect for the different roles of the Houses. A large majority can assist decision making while also weakening scrutiny if representatives never question the executive. Constitutional powers should therefore be evaluated through their operation rather than their existence alone. The House of Representatives and National Assembly have different composition and continuity, while confidence belongs to the former. Together they exercise important powers within a superior constitutional order whose procedures, rights and institutional limits remain binding on the legislature itself.

??? example "Q2. Explain parliamentary privilege and its constitutional limits in Nepal. (20 marks)"
    **Introduction**

    Parliamentary privilege consists of special protections and powers that allow Parliament to perform its constitutional work. Its purpose is institutional freedom and effective deliberation, not a personal exemption from all law for members. Nepal's Article 103 recognizes several privileges, while other provisions establish limits concerning judicial matters and review. A strong answer identifies the particular protection and its conditions before considering whether conduct falls within it. Statements that Parliament is completely immune from courts, or that a member can never be arrested, overlook the text. Privilege must be explained as part of constitutional government rather than as authority standing above the Constitution.

    **Freedom of speech and voting**

    Article 103(1) protects speech and votes in both Houses against the specified court proceedings. The explanation in clause (5) extends the constitutional understanding of House to joint sittings, committees and joint committees for the stated purposes. This protection matters because members need to deliberate and vote without ordinary litigation being used to intimidate them for parliamentary work. Its setting is important. A member's public speech outside the protected parliamentary context does not automatically receive the same immunity merely because the speaker belongs to Parliament. The legal question concerns the constitutionally protected activity, not personal status alone.

    **Internal proceedings**

    Article 103(2) gives each House authority to regulate and decide its internal business and the regularity of its proceedings. The provision expressly operates subject to the Constitution. This supports the ability to conduct legislative work without courts managing every internal disagreement about procedure. It should not be converted into a claim that the House may acquire powers the Constitution does not give it. The difference between an internal irregularity and absence of jurisdiction therefore becomes important. Explaining that difference respects both parliamentary autonomy and the superior framework within which parliamentary institutions possess their legal authority.

    **Proceedings and publications**

    Article 103(3) addresses the protection of proceedings and intentional distortion or misinterpretation of members' speech in publication and broadcasting. Its actual wording must be applied rather than replaced by a claim that all public criticism is prohibited. Clause (5) protects publication under the authority of a House of documents, reports, votes or proceedings. The authority requirement matters. Any person repeating material outside that framework is not automatically protected solely because the subject concerns Parliament. Clause (4) also applies specified protections to persons other than members who are entitled to participate. These details show that protection follows defined parliamentary functions and authorization.

    **Protection against arrest**

    Article 103(6) protects a member against arrest during the stated period from notice summoning a session to prorogation. The proviso preserves arrest on a criminal charge under federal law and requires immediate information to the presiding person. This is a central qualification. Parliamentary status does not create complete immunity from ordinary criminal accountability. The constitutional design protects the functioning of the House while recognizing a legally defined criminal exception. A proper answer should therefore state the protection and exception together. Separating them can leave a misleading impression that an elected representative possesses a general personal right to avoid lawful arrest.

    **Breach and contempt powers**

    Article 103(7) treats breach of privilege as contempt and gives the concerned House the specified authority to decide whether breach occurred. Under clause (8), following the House's decision, the presiding person may admonish, warn, impose imprisonment up to three months or a fine up to Rs 10,000. The proviso permits pardon, remission or commutation where an apology satisfies the House. These are defined powers with defined consequences. They should not be described as unlimited authority to impose whatever penalty an institution prefers. The presence of a punitive power makes careful identification of jurisdiction and constitutional conditions especially important.

    **Limits concerning courts and judges**

    Article 105 restricts discussion that may adversely affect justice in pending cases and discussion of judges' judicial acts in performing their duties. It preserves the stated exception for opinions about a judge's conduct during impeachment deliberations. This illustrates why speech protection must be read with the rest of the Constitution. The legislature has an important deliberative role, while courts have a distinct responsibility to decide cases without improper interference. Privilege therefore does not eliminate the constitutional allocation of judicial power. Equally, the judicial role does not authorize routine management of every parliamentary debate. The relevant boundary comes from the applicable provision.

    **The review limitation and exception**

    Article 133(3)'s proviso generally prevents Supreme Court interference under that clause with internal legislative proceedings and privilege proceedings and penalties, except on absence of jurisdiction. This must be included alongside Article 103. The provision rejects both unlimited judicial intervention and complete freedom from constitutional scrutiny. A complaint about every procedural disagreement is not the same as a claim that the House lacked jurisdiction to act. The actual grounds and source of power matter. A responsible explanation therefore asks whether the institution was acting within its constitutional field before promising judicial correction or denying any possibility of a court examining the question.

    **Historical comparison**

    The prescribed Tuladhar proceeding concerns privilege and contempt in the Panchayat constitutional setting. A public transcription has been located, but original-text and exact report verification remain incomplete. It cannot be presented as a verified judgment applying Article 103 of the 2015 Constitution. This distinction is legally important, because the institutional framework and relevant provisions changed. Historical material may help explain the continuing tension between legislative privilege and judicial authority. A current answer must nevertheless begin with the current constitutional text and use a historical proposition only to the extent supported by the actual source, with its historical law clearly identified.

    **Critical view and conclusion**

    Privilege protects Parliament's ability to deliberate, obtain information and conduct its work without improper obstruction. It can be misused if institutional protection is treated as a personal shelter or if contempt power is invoked without attention to constitutional authority. Nepal's provisions therefore need to be read as a connected framework of protections, exceptions and limits. Speech and voting immunity, authorized publications, arrest protection and contempt powers each have distinct conditions. Article 105 and the Article 133(3) proviso preserve important boundaries. Parliamentary privilege is strongest when it protects constitutional work while remaining accountable to the constitutional source and scope of that protection.

## Test yourself

??? question "How are the 275 representatives selected?"
    165 through constituencies and 110 through proportional representation under Article 84.

??? question "Does a National Assembly vacancy create a fresh six-year term?"
    No. Article 86(5) provides the remainder of the vacant term.

??? question "Can a member be arrested on a criminal charge?"
    Yes, under federal law, with the immediate notification required by Article 103(6).

??? question "Does Article 133(3) allow review of every internal irregularity?"
    No. Its proviso states the absence-of-jurisdiction exception to the specified non-interference rule.

??? question "What must a no-confidence motion include?"
    The proposed successor's name, in addition to satisfying Article 100's other requirements.

??? question "Is prorogation the same as dissolution?"
    No. Prorogation ends a session; dissolution ends the House early under constitutional authority.

## One-minute revision

- Parliament has two Houses with different selection and continuity.
- Representatives number 275; National Assembly members number 59.
- Confidence belongs to the House of Representatives.
- Privilege protects parliamentary work rather than personal lawlessness.
- Arrest protection has a criminal-charge exception.
- Article 133(3) preserves the absence-of-jurisdiction exception.
- Dissolution requires the constitutional conditions in Articles 76 and 85.
