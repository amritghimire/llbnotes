# Unit 12: Federal executive

!!! abstract "In one line"
    The President is head of state, while the Council of Ministers exercises executive power through the parliamentary constitutional framework.

## Why it matters for the exam

Distinguish the President from the Prime Minister and explain the sequence for forming government. Confidence, collective responsibility and individual ministerial responsibility are related but different concepts.

## 12.1 Head of state's constitutional role

Article 61 makes the President head of state, requires promotion of national unity and makes abiding by and protecting the Constitution the main duty. Article 62 provides election by an electoral college of federal and provincial parliamentary members with legally weighted votes.

The ordinary term is five years under Article 63, with continuation until the successor assumes office. Article 64 requires eligibility for federal parliamentary membership, at least 45 years of age and no legal disqualification; a person already elected President twice cannot stand again. These are rules for the office, not a description of a presidential executive system.

Article 66 requires the President to exercise powers under the Constitution and federal law. The usual recommendation-and-consent rule involves the Council of Ministers, conveyed through the Prime Minister, except where the Constitution or federal law specifies another recommending institution or official. Thus **not every presidential function uses the same recommendation**. Formal appointment by the President and the source of the required recommendation are separate questions.

## 12.2 Executive power

Article 75 vests executive power in the Council of Ministers, in accordance with the Constitution and law. It gives responsibility for direction, control and regulation of governance, and provides that federal executive functions are performed in the name of the Government of Nepal. Authentication follows federal law.

Executive power concerns governing and administering, but does not include a general authority to ignore Parliament, rights or courts. Ordinances under Article 114 are a specific conditioned power, not a permanent executive replacement for legislative lawmaking.

## 12.3 Constitution of the Council of Ministers

| Article 76 stage | Appointment basis | Important condition |
|---|---|---|
| (1) | Leader of the party commanding a House majority | Majority-party route |
| (2) | House member able to command support from two or more parties where no party has a clear majority | Confidence within 30 days under (4) |
| (3) | Leader of the largest parliamentary party if (2) cannot produce appointment within the stated period or its appointee fails confidence | Confidence within 30 days under (4) |
| (5) | A member presenting grounds to secure confidence after the (3) appointee fails | Confidence required through (6) |
| (7) | Dissolution in the circumstances stated there | Not an independent option available whenever politics becomes difficult |

Clause (3) refers to 30 days after final election results for its initial coalition-failure setting. Clause (8) requires appointment procedures to be completed within 35 days after final results or a vacancy. Read the stages together rather than inventing a fresh unlimited period at each step.

Under Article 76(9), the President forms a Council on the Prime Minister's recommendation, with a maximum of **25 ministers including the Prime Minister**, in accordance with inclusion, from federal parliamentary members. The explanation includes Deputy Prime Ministers, Ministers, Ministers of State and Assistant Ministers within minister for this purpose.

Article 78 permits the specified non-member appointment on the Prime Minister's recommendation, but the appointee must obtain federal parliamentary membership within six months. Failure bars reappointment during the same House term. A person defeated in the election to that House is excluded from this exception during its term. This exception does not make any non-member eligible to be Prime Minister.

## 12.4 Prime Minister's role

The Prime Minister chairs the Council, recommends ministers and can remove a minister under Article 77(2)(b). Article 81 requires information to the President concerning Council decisions, proposed Bills, the stated further information and national and foreign affairs. Article 82 concerns allocation and conduct of government business through approved rules.

These roles support coordination and leadership. They do not turn ministerial responsibility into a claim that the Prime Minister personally possesses every constitutional power. The President, Parliament, courts and other institutions retain their assigned functions.

## 12.5 Responsibility and accountability

Article 76(10) makes the Prime Minister and ministers collectively responsible to the Federal Parliament. Ministers are individually responsible for their ministries to the Prime Minister and Federal Parliament. A confidence vote under Article 100 is specifically a House of Representatives mechanism.

Article 77 identifies vacancies and provides continuity of the same Council until another is constituted. If the Prime Minister dies, the senior-most minister acts until appointment of a new Prime Minister. Continuity is not a new unrestricted mandate to disregard constitutional limits.

Questions, debate, committees, financial authority and judicial review provide other forms of accountability. Article 82(2) specifically limits court questions about whether the business rules in clause (1) were observed. Do not extend that rule into immunity for every unconstitutional executive act.

## Nepal's law

Use Articles 61–66, 74–82, 100 and 114, together with constitutional rights and judicial provisions. For dissolution, read Articles 76(7) and 85 with [Unit 5](unit-05-separation-of-powers.md) and [Unit 11](unit-11-federal-legislature.md).

## Full 20-mark answers

??? example "Q1. Explain the constitutional position of the President and Prime Minister in Nepal. (20 marks)"
    **Introduction**

    Nepal's parliamentary Constitution distinguishes the head of state from the head of government. The President occupies the constitutional head-of-state office, while the Prime Minister leads the Council of Ministers within a framework of parliamentary support and responsibility. The distinction is necessary because an elected President does not automatically make a system presidential. Articles 61–66 regulate the presidency, while Articles 74–82 establish important executive arrangements. A complete answer must connect the offices without merging them. Both derive authority from the Constitution, and neither may convert a constitutional duty or political mandate into unrestricted power over every other institution.

    **The President as head of state**

    Article 61 identifies the President as head of state, requires promotion of national unity and states the principal duty to abide by and protect the Constitution. These functions connect the office with the continuity and constitutional identity of the State. They do not place the President above the legal framework being protected. The responsibility must be exercised through powers and procedures actually provided by the Constitution and federal law. Describing the President merely as ceremonial can conceal constitutionally significant functions, while describing the office as an unrestricted personal executive would contradict the parliamentary allocation of authority.

    **Election, qualifications and term**

    Article 62 provides election through an electoral college comprising federal parliamentary and provincial assembly members, with voting weights under federal law. Article 64 requires the stated parliamentary eligibility, a minimum age of 45 and absence of legal disqualification. A person already elected President twice cannot again be a candidate. Article 63 provides a five-year term and continuation until another elected President assumes office. These provisions concern selection and continuity of headship. They should not be used to infer that the President has the same constitutional position as a separately elected presidential-system chief executive. The form of executive responsibility supplies a different legal test.

    **Recommendations and presidential functions**

    Article 66 provides the general framework for presidential action. The usual recommendation and consent come from the Council of Ministers through the Prime Minister, except where the Constitution or federal law identifies another recommending institution or official. This qualification matters. A formal appointment by the President does not mean the appointee is chosen through the same recommendation process in every case. For example, judicial appointments involve the particular constitutional councils. A sound answer therefore separates the person who formally appoints from the institution required to recommend. Constitutional roles remain distinct even where several institutions participate in completing one appointment.

    **Executive power and the Council of Ministers**

    Article 75 vests executive power in the Council of Ministers in accordance with the Constitution and law. It gives responsibility for directing and regulating governance and provides that federal executive functions operate in the Government of Nepal's name. This is the legal centre of the political executive. Its collective character is important. The Constitution does not simply say that every executive power belongs personally to the Prime Minister. Ministerial leadership and coordination operate within the Council's authority, specific legal powers and constitutional limits. Rights, legislative competence and court orders remain relevant to the legality of governmental action.

    **The Prime Minister's parliamentary basis**

    Article 74 expressly chooses parliamentary government, and Article 76 provides appointment routes linked to support in the House of Representatives. The majority-party leader, coalition-supported member, largest-party leader and later support-demonstrating member arise at different constitutional stages. Their conditions are not interchangeable. The Prime Minister's political authority therefore depends on the lawful formation process and the applicable confidence requirements. A general appeal to leadership or electoral popularity cannot replace those conditions. The distinction also explains why the President's election does not determine the government's ordinary parliamentary majority. The Constitution assigns those questions to different processes.

    **Leadership and ministerial appointments**

    The Prime Minister chairs the Council and recommends the formation of the ministry under Article 76(9). The maximum is 25 ministers including the Prime Minister, with inclusion and the constitutional explanation of ministerial categories. Article 78 provides a specific route for certain non-members to become ministers, subject to membership within six months and the stated exclusions. These details show that appointment is a legal power with conditions rather than unrestricted patronage. Article 77 also allows the Prime Minister to remove a minister. Individual leadership therefore has practical force, but it operates within constitutional rules about membership, office and responsibility.

    **Information and conduct of business**

    Article 81 requires the Prime Minister to inform the President about Council resolutions, proposed Bills, the specified further information and national and foreign affairs. This duty supports the President's constitutional role without transferring everyday administration into an independent presidential government. Article 82 addresses allocation and transaction of government business through approved rules. Its clause (2) limits court questions about observance of those rules. That specific limitation should not become a claim that every executive act is immune from constitutional examination. An internal business question and an allegation of violation of a separate constitutional right or power are not automatically the same issue.

    **Responsibility and continuity**

    Article 76(10) makes the Prime Minister and ministers collectively responsible to the Federal Parliament and ministers individually responsible for their ministries to the Prime Minister and Federal Parliament. Confidence under Article 100 is tested specifically in the House of Representatives. The institutions should not be confused. Article 77 provides for vacancies and continuation of the same Council until a new one is constituted, including the acting arrangement if the Prime Minister dies. Continuity prevents an administrative vacuum, but does not remove constitutional obligations. A continuing government still acts under law rather than gaining a fresh unlimited mandate merely because a successor has not yet assumed office.

    **Critical assessment and conclusion**

    The constitutional relationship balances state continuity, political leadership and responsibility. Problems arise when recommendation is treated as unlimited executive control or when a head-of-state duty is presented as independent authority to govern outside the parliamentary framework. Nepal's text instead allocates different roles and requires them to work together. The President represents the State and performs constitutionally assigned functions, while the Prime Minister leads the Council exercising executive power under parliamentary conditions. Understanding those roles through Articles 61–82 explains both their connection and their limits, and provides a stronger account than relying only on office titles or foreign political conventions.

    The President’s receipt of information under Article 81 helps constitutional office function. It does not convert the President into an alternative ministry with independent authority to administer every department.
??? example "Q2. Explain formation and accountability of the Council of Ministers under Nepal's Constitution. (20 marks)"
    **Introduction**

    The Council of Ministers exercises Nepal's executive power under Article 75. Its legitimacy depends on constitutional formation and continuing responsibility, rather than appointment alone. Article 76 provides a sequence for forming government when parliamentary support takes different forms, while Articles 77, 78 and 100 regulate office, membership and confidence. Article 76(10) distinguishes collective from individual responsibility. A complete answer must follow these provisions together. It should neither assume that every possible Prime Minister has identical confidence requirements nor treat temporary continuity as a power to govern without constitutional restraints. Formation creates authority, while accountability controls how that authority is used.

    **Majority-party government**

    Article 76(1) provides appointment of the parliamentary party leader commanding a majority in the House of Representatives. This is the first route where the electoral and parliamentary position supplies a clear majority-party basis. The Council is formed under the Prime Minister's chairpersonship. The constitutional rule should not be confused with a free presidential choice among any preferred political figures. The relevant support and party position identify the appointment route. Equally, this route should not automatically be given the same initial thirty-day confidence requirement stated for appointees under clauses (2) and (3). Later confidence obligations depend on the applicable constitutional trigger.

    **Coalition formation and the largest-party route**

    Where no party has a clear majority, clause (2) provides appointment of a House member able to command majority support from two or more parties. Clause (3) addresses failure of that process within its stated period or failure of the coalition appointee to obtain confidence. The leader of the largest parliamentary party is then appointed under the constitutional conditions. Clause (4) requires appointees under clauses (2) and (3) to secure confidence within thirty days of appointment. These stages connect formal appointment with demonstrated parliamentary support. They are not interchangeable options that may be selected merely because one would be politically more convenient.

    **The later support-demonstrating stage**

    Clause (5) addresses the situation where the clause (3) appointee fails confidence and a member presents grounds to secure the required support. Clause (6) requires confidence through the applicable thirty-day framework. Clause (7) then provides dissolution in the circumstances stated there, including failure of the clause (5) appointee to obtain confidence or inability to appoint a Prime Minister. The sequence matters because dissolution is not an independent escape from difficult parliamentary politics. Clause (8) also supplies the thirty-five-day appointment requirement following final election results or vacancy. An answer should read these periods together rather than invent unlimited fresh time for every political attempt.

    **Size and composition of the ministry**

    Under Article 76(9), the President forms the Council on the Prime Minister's recommendation, subject to a maximum of twenty-five ministers including the Prime Minister and the inclusive principle. The explanation includes Deputy Prime Ministers, Ministers, Ministers of State and Assistant Ministers within minister for this purpose. The limit therefore cannot be avoided merely by changing an office's label. Membership ordinarily comes from the Federal Parliament, while the Prime Minister's own appointment follows the House-based framework. Inclusion and numerical limits make formation a constitutional responsibility rather than a purely discretionary distribution of positions to those supporting the government.

    **The non-member exception**

    Article 78 allows the specified appointment of a non-member as minister on the Prime Minister's recommendation. The appointee must obtain federal parliamentary membership within six months after taking oath. Failure brings the stated bar on reappointment during that House's term. A person defeated in the election to the then House is also excluded from this exception during its term. These conditions should be stated together. The provision does not allow indefinite ministerial office without parliamentary membership, and does not itself make any non-member eligible for appointment as Prime Minister. Each office has its own constitutional route and conditions.

    **Collective and individual responsibility**

    Article 76(10) makes the Prime Minister and ministers collectively responsible to the Federal Parliament. Ministers are individually responsible for the business of their ministries to the Prime Minister and Federal Parliament. Collective responsibility concerns the executive as a government, while individual responsibility identifies accountability for a particular ministry. Neither removes legal responsibility for unconstitutional action. A minister cannot answer every allegation merely by saying that a decision was collectively supported. The constitutional and statutory authority for the act still matters. These forms of political responsibility operate alongside legal examination, financial authority and the distinct functions of other constitutional institutions.

    **Confidence and no-confidence controls**

    Article 100 provides the formal House of Representatives mechanisms. The Prime Minister may seek confidence, and must do so within thirty days where the represented party splits or a coalition party withdraws support. A written no-confidence motion may be tabled by one fourth of the then total membership, subject to the stated two-year and one-year restrictions. It must propose a successor, and adoption requires a majority of the then total House membership. The restrictions on no confidence do not cancel the separate mandatory confidence trigger. Accurate explanation of these differences shows how the Constitution combines responsibility with attempts to avoid unrestricted repeated removal motions.

    **Office, continuity and other scrutiny**

    Article 77 identifies the circumstances in which the Prime Minister's and ministers' offices fall vacant, and provides continuation until another Council is formed. If the Prime Minister dies, the senior-most minister acts until a new Prime Minister is appointed. Continuity maintains administration, but does not authorize disregard of legal limits. Parliamentary questions, debate, committees and financial examination provide continuing scrutiny beyond a single confidence vote. Judicial review can address constitutional legality within jurisdiction. These mechanisms answer different questions. A government may retain political confidence while a particular act is unlawful, just as a lawful decision may still be criticized as poor policy.

    **Critical view and conclusion**

    Nepal's executive formation framework seeks a government grounded in parliamentary support and capable of being held responsible. Its detailed stages prevent appointment and dissolution from depending only on political preference, while the ministry limit and membership rules constrain composition. Accountability includes both government-wide and ministry-specific responsibility, formal confidence tests and continuing examination of conduct. The practical challenge is to make these controls meaningful rather than formal exercises dominated by party convenience. A strong answer follows the constitutional sequence, distinguishes its different mechanisms and recognizes that lawful continuity serves administration without removing the executive's continuing duty to govern within the Constitution and law.

    No. It includes the Prime Minister and the ministerial categories in Article 76's explanation.
