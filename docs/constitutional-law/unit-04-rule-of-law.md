# Unit 4: Rule of law

!!! abstract "In one line"
    Rule of law means that public power must have lawful limits and that people can obtain protection when those limits are crossed.

## Why it matters for the exam

Explain Dicey's three meanings, then evaluate them against Nepal's written rights, judicial remedies and social conditions. A strong answer distinguishes a government acting through rules from a government itself controlled by law.

**Prescribed cases used here:** [Rajeev Parajuli](cases/18-9-rajeev-parajuli.md) (18.9).

## 4.1 Concept of rule of law

A lawful state must do more than issue commands in legal language. It must justify coercion through applicable law, respect constitutional limits and permit independent examination. **Rule by law** describes using rules as instruments of control. **Rule of law** asks whether the rulers themselves are controlled.

### Dicey's three meanings

Dicey's *Law of the Constitution*, Part II, Chapter IV, explains three connected ideas in the historical English setting. The following are paraphrases.

1. **Supremacy of regular law over arbitrary power.** Punishment requires an established breach of law and ordinary legal process, rather than the ruler's personal displeasure.
2. **Equality before ordinary law.** Officials and ordinary people are subject to law. Official position does not automatically remove responsibility.
3. **Rights protected through ordinary judicial decisions.** Dicey contrasted English liberties developed through remedies and decisions with systems relying primarily on written constitutional declarations.

The third idea is not a command that Nepal abandon written rights. Nepal's Part 3 expressly states fundamental rights, and Article 46 provides a right to constitutional remedies. Use Dicey as a comparative framework, not as a superior source of Nepal's law.

### Formal and substantive understandings

A formal account emphasizes accessible, reasonably clear, stable and prospective rules applied through fair institutions. A substantive account also asks whether rules protect rights and equality. This is an analytical distinction. A precise but discriminatory rule can be predictable while failing the constitutional standard of equality.

### Critical assessment

Dicey's resistance to broad official discretion highlights a real danger, but modern administration needs lawful discretion to address different circumstances. The answer is bounded power, fair procedure, reasons and review, not the claim that every discretion is illegal. Specialized tribunals are not automatically contrary to rule of law. Ask whether their authority and safeguards are adequate.

## 4.2 Prerequisites

| Requirement | Why it matters | Nepal connection |
|---|---|---|
| Superior constitutional limits | Prevent ordinary authorities from validating every act themselves | Article 1 |
| Lawful authority | An official must identify a power and observe its limits | Articles 2 and 126 |
| Equality and protection | Status should not determine whether a legal claim is heard | Article 18 |
| Fair criminal process | Liberty cannot depend only on accusation | Article 20 |
| Independent courts | The alleged wrongdoer must not control adjudication | Articles 126 and 128 |
| Remedies and compliance | Rights need an effective response to violation | Articles 46, 126(2), 133 and 144 |
| Accessible institutions | Cost, distance and delay can undermine formal protection | Article 20(10), subject to law, concerns free legal aid for an indigent party (one below the income limit specified by federal law) |
| Public responsibility | Scrutiny and lawful administration reduce hidden misuse | Parliamentary accountability and constitutional oversight |

**Worked example.** Assume a municipal officer refuses a licence solely because an applicant criticized the government. Identify the officer's statutory authority, the prescribed criteria and relevant rights. Article 17's expression protection and Article 18 may be relevant, but the exact claim depends on the facts and law. A court needs those facts; the slogan “rule of law” alone does not establish every element of a case.

## Nepal's law

Article 18(1) places citizens equally before the law and protects equal protection. Article 18(3) also permits specified special provisions for protection, empowerment or development of listed groups. Equality therefore does not prohibit every targeted measure.

Article 20 includes protections against retrospective criminal punishment, double jeopardy and compelled self-incrimination, together with the presumption of innocence and fair-trial guarantees. Read the exact clause for each claim. Article 20(3)'s production-before-authority rule contains stated exceptions; never describe it as an exception-free rule for every detention.

Article 133 provides Supreme Court review and remedies. [Rajeev Parajuli, NKP decision 7618](https://nkp.gov.np/full_detail/2166/), especially paragraphs 22–25 and 29, is a historical source on constitutional authority and judicial examination under the 1990 Constitution. It is not a decision applying the 2015 Constitution.

## Compare

| Rule of law | Rule by law |
|---|---|
| Public authorities must justify power under superior law | Rules may serve mainly to carry out official commands |
| Rights and review constrain official action | Formal legality may coexist with unchecked control |
| Officials answer for unlawful conduct | Status may shelter decision makers |
| Fair process and effective remedies matter | Enacting a rule is treated as enough |

## Full 20-mark answers

??? example "Q1. Explain Dicey's rule of law and critically assess its application in Nepal. (20 marks)"
    **Introduction**

    Rule of law means that government must act within legal limits and that people must have meaningful protection against unlawful power. It does not mean only that a government issues commands in the form of rules. The central question is whether the government itself remains answerable to law. A.V. Dicey's account provides a classic explanation through three connected meanings. Those meanings should be studied in their historical English context and then compared with Nepal's written Constitution. Nepal's legal position depends on constitutional supremacy, express rights and judicial remedies rather than on adopting every detail of Dicey's account unchanged.

    **Supremacy of regular law**

    Dicey's first meaning opposes arbitrary power. A person should not suffer punishment simply because an official dislikes that person's conduct. There must be a legally established basis and the prescribed process for imposing the consequence. This idea directs attention to both authority and procedure. A government cannot answer every objection by claiming that its purpose was beneficial. It must show that the law authorizes the measure and that constitutional conditions are respected. In Nepal, Article 1 strengthens the inquiry because an ordinary rule itself may be invalid if it conflicts with the Constitution.

    **Equality before law**

    The second meaning is equality before the ordinary law. Official status does not place a person beyond legal responsibility. The principle challenges the idea that government servants can always rely on position as a complete defence. Nepal's Article 18 supplies an express constitutional equality framework. Yet equality does not require mechanically identical treatment in every circumstance. The proviso to Article 18(3) permits specified special measures for protected purposes and listed groups. A complete answer therefore distinguishes arbitrary privilege from constitutionally authorized action addressing disadvantage. Otherwise, a statement meant to defend equality could incorrectly condemn measures the Constitution itself permits.

    **Rights and judicial remedies**

    Dicey's third meaning concerns the way liberties developed and were protected through ordinary law and judicial decisions in England. He contrasted this with reliance on constitutional declarations. The point emphasizes the importance of effective remedies, but should not be converted into a universal requirement that rights must remain unwritten. Nepal expressly recognizes fundamental rights in Part 3. Article 46 provides a right to constitutional remedies, with important jurisdiction under Articles 133 and 144. The comparison shows that constitutional texts and judicial protection can work together. A written right is valuable, but it needs a practical institution capable of protecting it.

    **Nepal's superior constitutional framework**

    Article 2 vests sovereignty and state power in the Nepali people and requires constitutional exercise of that power. This excludes an assumption that a political leader personally owns the state's authority. Article 1 makes the Constitution fundamental and inconsistent law void to the extent of inconsistency. These provisions make rule of law more than obedience to whatever command an ordinary authority issues. A measure must satisfy the superior framework as well as the relevant Act. The constitutional order therefore provides a standard by which legislation, administration and institutional action can be questioned.

    **Fair process and independent adjudication**

    Article 20 illustrates the legal protection of persons facing criminal accusation. Its guarantees include the presumption of innocence and protections concerning punishment, prosecution, evidence and trial. Exact claims must follow the relevant clauses and exceptions. Articles 126 and 128 establish judicial authority and the Supreme Court's interpretive position. Independence is essential because the legality of a coercive act should not be decided solely by the institution accused of acting unlawfully. Article 126(2)'s requirement to abide by court orders and decisions also matters. A remedy that officials may freely ignore would not provide effective legal protection.

    **Modern administration and discretion**

    A limitation of an overly rigid reading of Dicey is its treatment of administrative discretion and specialized arrangements. Modern government must make decisions concerning services, regulation and different factual situations. Some discretion is unavoidable. The real issue is whether the discretion has a lawful purpose, operates within limits and is subject to appropriate procedure and examination. Equally, a specialized tribunal is not automatically inconsistent with rule of law merely because it is not an ordinary court. Its authority, independence and safeguards must be assessed. This approach preserves Dicey's concern about arbitrariness without denying the practical needs of administration.

    **Formal legality and substantive justice**

    A formal conception stresses accessible, reasonably clear, stable and prospective rules applied by fair institutions. These conditions help people plan their conduct and challenge inconsistent official action. A substantive conception additionally examines rights and equality. The distinction matters because a clear rule can still discriminate unlawfully or deny basic constitutional protections. Nepal's written rights make it insufficient to praise a measure only because its wording is precise. Conversely, a policy with a worthy objective must still follow lawful procedures. Rule of law requires both legally disciplined methods and respect for the superior standards applicable to the action.

    **Historical illustration and practical limits**

    The Rajeev Parajuli proceeding, NKP decision 7618, is useful historical material on constitutionally allocated authority and judicial examination. It arose under the 1990 Constitution, so its provisions must not be replaced by 2015 article numbers. Its use illustrates the proper method of learning from precedent without erasing legal change. Practical difficulties can also weaken rule of law even where the formal framework is strong. Cost, delay and unequal access may prevent people from using remedies. These are risks that explain the importance of accessible institutions and legal aid, rather than proof that written guarantees have no value.

    The phrase 'to the extent of inconsistency' also deserves attention. Constitutional supremacy does not justify casually announcing that every part of a disputed legal arrangement has disappeared. The legal question concerns the actual conflict and the appropriate consequence under the constitutional framework. Careful identification of the offending rule protects both individual rights and legal certainty. It demonstrates that rule of law demands disciplined reasoning from the decision maker, including a court, rather than merely a desirable outcome reached without explaining its legal basis.

    **Conclusion**

    Dicey's account remains useful because it emphasizes lawful authority, equality and practical protection of liberty. Its historical assumptions require careful adaptation to a written rights-based constitutional order. Nepal supports rule of law through constitutional supremacy, express rights, independent adjudication and remedies. The strongest assessment neither accepts every feature of Dicey's account uncritically nor dismisses it as irrelevant. It asks whether a public decision can be justified under valid law, whether constitutional protections are observed and whether an affected person can obtain an effective response when power exceeds its limits.

??? example "Q2. Discuss the prerequisites of rule of law and distinguish it from rule by law. (20 marks)"
    **Introduction**

    A government may use legal rules while failing to respect rule of law. The distinction is between employing law as an instrument of control and accepting law as a limit on the government itself. Rule of law requires lawful authority, constitutional limits, fair institutions and effective protection. Its prerequisites are therefore interconnected. Clear legislation is useful, but cannot compensate for an institution that refuses to hear a claim. A court may be formally independent, but its decisions need compliance. Nepal's constitutional provisions should be examined through these practical connections rather than reduced to a list of attractive principles.

    **Constitutional supremacy and lawful authority**

    The first requirement is a legal order that controls public power. Article 1 makes the Constitution Nepal's fundamental law, while Article 2 requires state power to be exercised constitutionally. An official must identify the authority for an act and remain within its limits. If ordinary legislation could override any constitutional protection, the supposed limit would depend entirely on the authority it restrains. Constitutional supremacy therefore makes it possible to examine the validity of the law itself. Rule by law, in contrast, may treat the existence of an official rule as a complete answer without asking whether the rule satisfies superior limits.

    **Clarity, accessibility and reasonable stability**

    People need a fair opportunity to understand the rules governing their conduct. Secret, contradictory or constantly changing requirements can make compliance impossible and permit selective enforcement. Legal certainty therefore supports both liberty and responsible administration. It does not require that every legal provision be free from interpretation or that laws never change. Changes should follow lawful procedures and make their effects reasonably understandable. The underlying concern is that people should not be exposed to coercion through requirements they could not reasonably identify. An accessible legal framework also makes public criticism and judicial examination more effective.

    **Equality and protection against arbitrary treatment**

    Rule of law requires that legal protection not depend simply on wealth, office or political connection. Article 18 expressly addresses equality before law and equal protection. Its structure also recognizes that disadvantage may justify special measures within constitutional conditions. Thus equal protection is not the same as refusing every distinction. The relevant inquiry is whether differential treatment is lawfully justified or merely favours a powerful person. This prerequisite protects both individuals and the credibility of public institutions. A system that applies strict rules to opponents while exempting allies would undermine legality even if the rules themselves were clearly written.

    **Fair procedure**

    Procedure matters because legal consequences affect liberty, property and participation. An accusation should not automatically become proof. Article 20 contains important criminal-justice guarantees, including the presumption of innocence, legal representation and fair-trial protections. Different guarantees have different beneficiaries and exceptions, so their precise clauses must be read. Fair process also helps institutions reach better decisions by requiring attention to the relevant facts and law. Rule by law may focus only on the government's ability to secure a result. Rule of law asks whether the result was reached through a lawful process that respects the affected person's constitutional position.

    **Independent courts and reasoned decisions**

    A person needs an institution capable of deciding a legal claim without improper pressure from the alleged wrongdoer. Judicial independence is therefore a central prerequisite. Articles 126 and 128 provide important foundations for Nepal's judicial function and interpretive authority. Independence does not mean that judges possess unlimited power or are exempt from legal responsibility. Courts must remain within jurisdiction and explain decisions through legal reasoning. This combination protects both the claimant and the institution whose action is challenged. A reasoned decision allows the public to see why a power was upheld or restrained instead of requiring blind acceptance of personal authority.

    **Effective remedies and obedience**

    A constitutional protection needs a response when it is violated. Article 46 provides a right to constitutional remedies. Articles 133 and 144 establish important powers of the Supreme Court and High Courts. Article 126(2) requires compliance with court orders and decisions made in the course of hearing cases. These provisions explain why remedies and obedience belong together. Filing a case is not the final purpose of justice. If an unlawful detention continues despite an applicable release order, the availability of a formal remedy has not produced effective protection. Constitutional responsibility therefore extends to implementation as well as adjudication.

    **Access, public scrutiny and lawful discretion**

    Institutions must also be usable. Excessive cost, distance or delay can prevent a person from obtaining protection that exists in formal law. Article 20(10), concerning free legal aid for an indigent party (one below the income limit specified by federal law) in accordance with law, is relevant to this concern. Public scrutiny and parliamentary responsibility provide additional checks on misuse that litigation alone cannot address. Modern administration also requires discretion, but discretion should have a lawful purpose and boundaries. A power is not unconstitutional simply because judgment is required. It becomes problematic when legal limits are replaced by favour, hostility or an official's unreviewable personal preference.

    **Application through an example**

    Assume an officer refuses a licence because the applicant criticized the government. A rule-of-law analysis begins with the officer's statutory power and prescribed decision criteria. It then examines relevant rights, potentially including expression and equality, and the available remedy. The conclusion depends on the facts and applicable provisions. This method is stronger than merely declaring that all licence refusals are arbitrary. It shows how the prerequisites operate together. Authority, reasons, rights and review become concrete questions that can be examined, rather than separate headings repeated without connection to the decision affecting a person.

    The prerequisites also help distinguish an error from arbitrary government. An official may make a mistake within a system that permits correction through reasons, review and an effective remedy. That possibility differs from an arrangement in which the official denies any duty to justify the decision or accept examination. Rule of law does not promise that no institution will ever err. It requires lawful methods for preventing, identifying and correcting misuse so that the affected person's protection does not depend solely on official goodwill.

    **Critical view and conclusion**

    No single prerequisite is sufficient. Predictable rules can still violate rights, independent courts can be inaccessible, and well-written remedies can be ignored. Rule of law therefore requires institutions and constitutional conduct to reinforce one another. Dicey's account contributes the concern with arbitrariness, equality and judicial protection, while Nepal's written Constitution adds an express superior framework and rights structure. Rule by law asks whether officials have a rule through which to act. Rule of law also asks whether that rule and its application can survive independent examination under constitutional limits. That additional inquiry is what makes public authority legally accountable.

## Test yourself

??? question "What is wrong with defining rule of law as strict obedience to any enacted rule?"
    The rule may itself violate the Constitution. Article 1 supplies the superior standard.

??? question "Does Dicey's third meaning require Nepal to discard written rights?"
    No. It describes an English historical approach. Nepal expressly protects rights and remedies.

??? question "Is every administrative discretion unlawful?"
    No. Examine its legal basis, purpose, limits, procedure and review.

??? question "Does equality prohibit all protective measures?"
    No. The proviso to Article 18(3) authorizes specified measures within its terms.

??? question "Why does compliance with judgments matter?"
    Without compliance, a successful legal claim may fail to protect the person affected.

??? question "Can a predictable rule still be unconstitutional?"
    Yes. Clarity does not cure an infringement of constitutional rights or limits.

## One-minute revision

- Rule of law controls rulers as well as the public.
- Dicey addresses regular law, equality and protection through judicial decisions.
- Nepal protects written rights and constitutional remedies.
- Discretion must be lawful and bounded.
- Courts need independence, access and compliance.
- Equality permits constitutionally justified protective measures.
