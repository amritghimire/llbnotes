# Unit 2: Classification of constitutions

!!! abstract "In one line"
    Constitutions can be grouped by their form, amendment procedure, territorial structure, head of state and executive system.

## Why it matters for the exam

A classification answer needs a test for each category and an example. Nepal belongs to several categories at once. A written constitution can be federal, republican and parliamentary without contradiction.

## 2.1 Written and unwritten

The familiar textbook labels describe whether the central constitutional rules are collected in a special written instrument. **Codified and uncodified** are more accurate labels. The UK has many written constitutional rules, even though they are not collected in a single constitutional document. Nepal, India and the USA have codified constitutions.

Writing aids access and clarity, but cannot foresee every dispute. Judicial interpretation, legislation and political practice continue to matter. An uncodified constitution is not a state without law.

## 2.2 Rigid and flexible

Ask **how the constitution changes**, not whether it ever changes. A rigid constitution requires a procedure different from ordinary legislation. Article 274 gives Nepal a special amendment procedure. Its requirements vary with the subject, including provincial involvement in specified cases. Do not say every Nepal amendment needs approval by every Province.

The US Article V process is a clear comparative example of special amendment requirements. The traditional UK model allows constitutional legislation through ordinary parliamentary lawmaking. India has different amendment routes under Article 368 and related provisions. Therefore rigidity is a matter of procedure and degree, not an absolute absence of change.

## 2.3 Federal and unitary

Federalism distributes governing authority constitutionally. The central legislature does not create every territorial power through an ordinary revocable delegation. Nepal's Articles 56–58 and Schedules 5–9 establish three levels and allocate powers. Judicial resolution helps manage disputes about competence.

A unitary state can decentralize extensively. Decentralization means moving functions closer to people, but it does not by itself establish a federal division protected by a superior constitution. See [Unit 6](unit-06-federal-system.md).

## 2.4 Republican and monarchical

The central question is the position of the head of state. A monarchy has a monarch whose office normally follows hereditary rules. A republic does not. Article 4 describes Nepal as a federal democratic republican state, while Article 62 provides election of the President through an electoral college.

This classification does not itself measure democracy. A constitutional monarchy can have parliamentary democracy. A republic can lack genuine political freedom. Assess limits, participation and rights separately from the name of the state.

## 2.5 Parliamentary and presidential

A parliamentary executive depends on legislative confidence. Nepal's Article 74 expressly chooses a parliamentary form. Article 76 regulates government formation and Article 100 regulates confidence and no-confidence procedures. Article 76(10) states collective responsibility to the Federal Parliament and individual ministerial responsibility to the Prime Minister and Federal Parliament.

The US Constitution vests executive power in a President under Article II. Ordinary legislative disagreement does not operate like a parliamentary no-confidence vote. Congress still has legislative, financial and impeachment powers. Presidential government therefore means separate constitutional authority, not freedom from checks.

Do not classify Nepal as presidential merely because its head of state is called President. Distinguish the head of state from the prime minister who heads the government.

## Full 20-mark answers

??? example "Q1. Explain the main classifications of constitutions and classify Nepal's Constitution. (20 marks)"
    **Introduction**

    Classification makes a complicated constitutional system easier to understand by asking a series of different questions. Some questions concern the form of the rules, while others concern amendment, territorial organization or the relationship between institutions. The categories are therefore cumulative. A constitution can be written, rigid, federal, republican and parliamentary at the same time. None of these words alone proves that a state protects liberty or governs effectively. A useful answer states the test for each classification, explains its significance and then applies that test to the Constitution of Nepal 2015.

    **Written and unwritten constitutions**

    The first classification concerns whether the central constitutional framework is collected in a special written instrument. Nepal, India and the USA have such instruments. The traditional description of the UK constitution as unwritten needs qualification, because many of its rules exist in statutes and other written sources. Codified and uncodified are therefore more precise terms. A written constitution provides a visible reference for institutions and citizens, but does not remove all uncertainty. It still requires interpretation and supporting law. Nepal's Constitution is written, while constitutional law includes more than the constitutional document itself.

    **Rigid and flexible constitutions**

    The second classification concerns the legal procedure for constitutional change. A rigid constitution requires a procedure distinct from ordinary legislation. Flexibility means constitutional rules can be altered through the ordinary legislative route. Rigidity does not mean amendment is impossible, and flexibility does not mean government may act without law. Article 274 establishes Nepal's special amendment framework. Its protections and procedures must be read together, including provincial involvement for specified subjects. Thus Nepal is generally classified as rigid in the procedural sense. The precise requirements depend on the proposal rather than on a single formula applied to every amendment.

    **Federal and unitary constitutions**

    A federal constitution distributes authority constitutionally across levels of government. The relevant territorial institutions exercise powers recognized by the fundamental framework, rather than relying entirely on an ordinary delegation from the centre. Nepal's Article 56 establishes the Federation, Provinces and Local Level. Article 57 and Schedules 5–9 allocate powers, and Article 58 assigns residuary matters to the Federation. These arrangements justify the federal classification. A unitary system may also decentralize functions or provide territorial autonomy, but decentralization alone does not establish the same constitutional distribution. The real question is the source and protection of the territorial power.

    **Republican and monarchical constitutions**

    This classification concerns the head of state. A monarchy has a monarch, normally holding office through hereditary succession. A republic does not base its headship on that hereditary monarchical office. Article 4 identifies Nepal as a federal democratic republican state, and Article 62 provides for presidential election through an electoral college. Nepal is therefore republican. The classification must not be confused with a judgment about democracy. A constitutional monarchy can operate through representative institutions and responsible government. Conversely, merely calling a state a republic does not prove that citizens enjoy meaningful elections, equality or judicial protection.

    **Parliamentary and presidential constitutions**

    The relationship between executive and legislature supplies another classification. Parliamentary government depends on legislative confidence. Its executive leadership normally emerges through the representative chamber and remains politically accountable under constitutional procedures. Presidential government gives the executive a separate constitutional position and tenure, rather than making ordinary political survival depend on a parliamentary confidence vote. This difference does not eliminate checks on a President. The United States Constitution separately allocates legislative and executive powers and provides institutional controls, including impeachment. The labels identify the structure of executive responsibility, not a difference between controlled and uncontrolled government.

    **Nepal's parliamentary position**

    Article 74 expressly provides a parliamentary form of governance. Article 76 supplies the framework for appointing the Prime Minister and constituting the Council of Ministers. Article 100 addresses confidence and no-confidence procedures, while Article 76(10) states collective and individual responsibility. These provisions explain the classification more accurately than the existence of a President. Nepal's President is head of state, but that title does not turn the system into presidential government. The constitutional position of the Prime Minister and the Council of Ministers, particularly their relation to parliamentary confidence, determines the relevant category.

    **Advantages of using several classifications**

    Different classifications explain different risks. A special amendment procedure may protect institutions against temporary majorities. Federalism can bring decisions closer to communities and divide authority territorially. Parliamentary responsibility can connect executive power to elected representation. A written framework can make rights and institutional duties easier to identify. These advantages do not necessarily appear together in practice. For example, a written federal constitution may still operate through strongly centralized party decisions. Classification helps identify legal design, but its effect depends on implementation, political competition and the willingness of institutions to observe their limits.

    **Limits and critical assessment**

    The categories should not be treated as rigid descriptions of every institutional detail. Parliamentary systems differ in dissolution powers, appointment rules and confidence procedures. Federal systems differ in their allocation of residual subjects and financial powers. India illustrates that amendment procedures can vary with the provision affected. The UK illustrates that an uncodified system can contain extensive written law. Nepal itself combines constitutional federalism with residuary authority at the federal level. These distinctions improve an answer because they replace broad labels with the actual legal tests that explain a country's constitutional arrangement.

    For example, two states may both have written constitutions while differing on almost every other test. One may protect territorial powers through a federal structure, while another organizes them through a unitary arrangement. One may maintain a hereditary monarch, while another elects a head of state. The fact of writing does not determine these choices. Applying the tests separately therefore prevents a false chain of assumptions in which the student treats a single familiar feature as proof of the whole constitutional design.

    **Conclusion**

    Nepal's Constitution is best classified as written, procedurally rigid, federal, republican and parliamentary. Articles 1, 4, 56–58, 62, 74, 76, 100 and 274 provide the principal support for this description. The classifications are useful because each examines a different constitutional choice. Their value disappears if they are treated as slogans or automatic guarantees of good government. A complete answer therefore moves from definition to constitutional evidence and then to a limited evaluation. It explains both the category into which Nepal falls and the constitutional conditions that give that category practical meaning.

??? example "Q2. Compare parliamentary and presidential government. Why is Nepal parliamentary despite having a President? (20 marks)"
    **Introduction**

    The name of the head of state does not by itself classify a system of government. The decisive issue is how executive authority is constituted and made responsible. Parliamentary government connects the executive to legislative confidence. Presidential government gives the executive a separate constitutional position and a tenure that does not ordinarily depend on a confidence vote. Both systems can contain democratic institutions, written rights and judicial review. Nepal is parliamentary because its Constitution expressly adopts that form and establishes corresponding rules of government formation and responsibility. Its elected President performs a different role from a presidential-system chief executive.

    **Meaning of parliamentary government**

    In a parliamentary system, the government must possess the confidence required by the constitutional framework. The prime minister leads the political executive, while a separate head of state may perform constitutional functions. This arrangement connects executive authority with the representative institution. The legislature can question policy, scrutinize expenditure and use the applicable confidence procedure. Yet confidence is a legal and institutional relationship, not a promise that every member always agrees with every minister. The rules determining appointment, loss of confidence and replacement must be read from the particular Constitution rather than assumed from a foreign example.

    **Meaning of presidential government**

    A presidential system separately constitutes the executive and legislature. In the United States, Article I vests legislative powers in Congress and Article II vests executive power in a President. Ordinary political disagreement with Congress does not function as a parliamentary no-confidence vote removing the President. However, separate tenure does not create an unlimited executive. The constitutional framework includes legislative control, judicial authority and impeachment mechanisms. It is therefore misleading to define presidential government as government by one person without accountability. The distinction concerns the constitutional form of responsibility and removal, not the complete presence or absence of restraint.

    **Executive membership and institutional overlap**

    Parliamentary government commonly involves substantial connection between ministers and the legislature. Such overlap can support coordination because the executive's programme must obtain parliamentary support. It can also weaken scrutiny where a disciplined majority automatically supports the Cabinet. Presidential government gives the branches greater institutional separation, but this can produce disagreement over appointments, legislation or finance. Neither overlap nor separation alone determines the quality of government. The important questions are whether the institutions can perform their assigned functions and whether checks remain effective when political interests encourage one branch to dominate another.

    **Responsibility and continuity**

    Parliamentary responsibility allows a government that loses the constitutionally required confidence to be replaced through parliamentary processes. This can make political leadership responsive without waiting for the end of a fixed electoral period. It can also contribute to uncertainty where coalitions repeatedly change. Presidential tenure can provide continuity, but separate electoral claims may make conflict difficult to resolve. These are possible institutional consequences, not universal predictions. A well-designed confidence framework can seek stability while preserving accountability. Nepal's Article 100 must therefore be explained through its actual conditions, including restrictions on no-confidence motions, rather than as unrestricted removal at any moment.

    **Nepal's express constitutional choice**

    Article 74 settles the basic classification by adopting a multiparty competitive federal democratic republican parliamentary form of governance based on pluralism. Article 76 then provides different routes for appointing the Prime Minister according to parliamentary support. The Council of Ministers operates under the Prime Minister's leadership. Article 76(10) states collective responsibility to the Federal Parliament and individual ministerial responsibility to the Prime Minister and Federal Parliament. These provisions establish responsible government in legal terms. They are more important to the classification than the title used for the person occupying the presidency.

    **President and Prime Minister have different roles**

    The President is Nepal's head of state under Article 61. Article 66 regulates presidential functions and the role of recommendations and consent. The Prime Minister, by contrast, leads the Council of Ministers within the parliamentary framework. These offices should not be merged in an exam answer. An elected head of state makes Nepal republican, but republicanism concerns the headship of the state rather than the executive's dependence on confidence. Nepal can therefore be both republican and parliamentary. Confusing these categories would also wrongly suggest that every constitutional monarchy must have a non-democratic or presidential executive.

    **Confidence, dissolution and constitutional limits**

    Article 100 provides the relevant confidence and no-confidence arrangements. Dissolution is another distinct question. Articles 76(7) and 85 must be considered rather than assuming that every parliamentary prime minister has a free power to end the House. The existence of parliamentary government does not import all conventional powers attributed to another country's executive. A Nepal problem must be resolved through Nepal's text and applicable interpretation. This illustrates a broader principle of classification. A category describes a family of institutional arrangements, but cannot replace the precise constitutional provisions governing a disputed act.

    **Checks and balances in both systems**

    Both systems need checks on the use of public authority. In parliamentary government, scrutiny, financial approval, courts and constitutional institutions matter alongside confidence. In a presidential system, separate branches can check legislation, appointments and executive action through their assigned powers. Judicial independence remains important in either system because institutional disagreement can involve questions of legal authority. The aim is to prevent concentration without disabling government. For Nepal, describing executive-legislative overlap should therefore be followed by discussion of constitutional supremacy and judicial review, rather than a conclusion that separation of powers has disappeared entirely.

    A practical comparison should ask what happens when executive and legislative preferences diverge. In a parliamentary setting, the legal question may concern whether the government retains the required confidence and what process follows its loss. In a presidential setting, the issue may instead concern the separate constitutional powers of the branches. The same political disagreement therefore need not produce the same legal consequence. This is why a comparison should follow institutional relationships rather than the personal popularity or title of the leader.

    **Critical assessment and conclusion**

    Parliamentary government may provide responsive leadership, but party control can weaken parliamentary examination. Presidential government may provide continuity, but separate institutions can obstruct necessary decisions. Neither label alone proves effectiveness, liberty or stability. Nepal's constitutional answer is nevertheless clear. Its executive is organized through parliamentary support and responsibility under Articles 74, 76 and 100, while the President holds a separate constitutional head-of-state office. Nepal is parliamentary despite having a President because the relevant test is the structure of executive authority and accountability. The strongest comparison explains that structure before assessing its possible benefits and weaknesses.

## Test yourself

??? question "Does unwritten mean no written constitutional rules?"
    No. Uncodified is the more accurate term for the UK model.

??? question "Which article governs constitutional amendment in Nepal?"
    Article 274. The requirements depend on the proposed change.

??? question "Can a federal constitution have a strong centre?"
    Yes. Federalism concerns constitutionally allocated authority, not equal power over every subject.

??? question "Does having a President make Nepal presidential?"
    No. Articles 74, 76 and 100 establish parliamentary government.

??? question "Does a republic necessarily guarantee democracy?"
    No. Republican headship and democratic accountability are separate questions.

## One-minute revision

- Form, amendment, territory, headship and executive responsibility are different tests.
- Uncodified does not mean wholly unwritten.
- Rigidity concerns special amendment procedure.
- Decentralization alone does not establish federalism.
- Republic and democracy are different concepts.
- Nepal is written, rigid, federal, republican and parliamentary.
