# Unit 1: Introduction to constitutional law

!!! abstract "In one line"
    Constitutional law explains who may govern, how public power is limited and how people can challenge its misuse.

## Why it matters for the exam

This unit supplies the vocabulary for every later unit. Be ready to distinguish a Constitution from constitutional law and to explain the different sources of constitutional rules.

## 1.1 Definition of constitutional law

Start with three questions. Who has the power? What procedure must that person follow? What limits protect everyone else? Constitutional law studies the legal answers. Choosing a prime minister, dividing legislative subjects and protecting expression are constitutional questions.

A.V. Dicey describes constitutional law by reference to rules affecting the distribution or exercise of sovereign power. His *Introduction to the Study of the Law of the Constitution*, introductory discussion, distinguishes enforceable law from constitutional conventions. This is a paraphrase, not a quotation. In Nepal, Article 2 locates sovereignty and state power in the people and requires their exercise under the Constitution. An elected official therefore has constitutional authority, not unlimited personal authority.

Constitutional law is part of public law, which regulates public institutions and their relations with people. It overlaps with administrative law, but constitutional law establishes the framework within which administration operates.

## 1.2 Basic sources

A source is where a rule gets its authority or where we find reliable evidence of it. Separate a **binding legal source** from a **persuasive explanation**. The Constitution and valid legislation supply legal rules. A scholar's book may explain those rules without creating them. A political practice may guide conduct without allowing a court to enforce it directly.

For a Nepal problem, begin with the Constitution, identify any relevant Act and subordinate rules, then read applicable Supreme Court interpretation. Do not begin with an unsourced summary.

## 1.3 Constitution and statutes

Article 1 makes the Constitution Nepal's fundamental law. An inconsistent law is void to the extent of inconsistency. A statute is an enacted law dealing with subjects such as elections or the functioning of institutions. Constitutional importance does not give an ordinary statute the same rank as the Constitution.

**Example.** If an Act regulates an election, both the legislature's authority to enact it and its treatment of rights must fit the Constitution. Passing the Act by a large majority does not itself remove constitutional limits.

## 1.4 Secondary legislation and other rules

An Act can authorize another body to make detailed rules. These rules must stay within both the enabling Act and the Constitution. A minister cannot create authority merely by naming a document a guideline.

Distinguish these instruments from parliamentary procedure made under Article 104 and an ordinance under Article 114. An ordinance has the force and effect of an Act while valid, subject to constitutional conditions. It is not merely a departmental rule. Its expiry and approval rules must be examined separately.

## 1.5 Judicial decision

Courts decide disputes and interpret legal provisions. Article 128(2) gives the Supreme Court final authority to interpret the Constitution and laws. Article 128(4) requires compliance with its constitutional or legal interpretations and legal principles laid down in deciding cases.

Read the issue, material facts and reasoning before extracting a precedent. A party's argument printed in a judgment is not necessarily the court's holding. Historical judgments interpret the legal instrument applicable then, even when they help explain a continuing principle.

## 1.6 Non-legal rules, convention and custom

A constitutional convention is a rule of political conduct. Its enforcement generally depends on political responsibility rather than a court order. Dicey's distinction is useful, but its English setting must be stated. In Nepal, practices cannot override constitutional text.

Custom is an established practice. Whether it has legal force depends on recognition by the legal system, not simply its age. A repeated breach of the Constitution does not become valid through repetition. Distinguish an ordinary habit, a political convention and a legally recognized custom.

## 1.7 Books of authority

A carefully reasoned book may organize sources, explain history or offer an interpretation. Its persuasiveness depends on the author's reasoning, evidence and relevance. Dicey is useful for traditional English theory, but not a substitute for Article 76 when discussing formation of Nepal's government.

Use exact quotations only when checked. A paraphrase should identify the author without quotation marks. Do not attach an invented definition to a famous scholar.

## 1.8 Constitution and constitutional law differ

The Constitution is the fundamental instrument. Constitutional law is the wider legal subject, including relevant legislation and judicial interpretation. Political conventions help explain constitutional operation, but their inclusion in the subject does not turn every convention into enforceable law.

## Full 20-mark answers

These are practice answers in exam prose. Adapt the headings to the question. They are not verified past-paper transcriptions.

??? example "Q1. Define constitutional law and discuss its sources with reference to Nepal. (20 marks)"
    **Introduction**

    Constitutional law concerns the legal organization and control of public power. It explains the creation of institutions, their authority, their relations and the rights that limit their actions. A student can approach it through three connected questions. Who is entitled to act, what procedure governs the action, and what remedy exists if a limit is crossed? The sources of constitutional law answer these questions. In Nepal, those sources must be understood within constitutional supremacy rather than by assuming that every act of government is valid because it comes from a powerful institution.

    **Meaning and scope**

    A.V. Dicey explains the subject through rules affecting the distribution or exercise of sovereign power. He also distinguishes the law of the constitution from conventions governing constitutional conduct. His account helps identify the subject, but its English background matters. Nepal has its own written Constitution. Article 2 vests sovereignty and state power in the Nepali people and directs their exercise under that instrument. Thus, Nepal's constitutional law concerns legally allocated powers rather than an unlimited authority held personally by a monarch, prime minister or parliamentary majority. It covers institutions and their responsibilities as well as the protection of individuals.

    **The Constitution as the principal source**

    The Constitution of Nepal 2015 is the starting source. Article 1 declares it the fundamental law and makes inconsistent law void to the extent of inconsistency. Its importance lies in more than its title. It establishes the levels of government, recognizes fundamental rights and distributes legislative, executive and judicial authority. It also provides procedures through which government is formed and constitutional disputes are resolved. A legal answer should identify the relevant provision and explain its effect. Merely listing article numbers does not show whether the proposed action satisfies the constitutional condition attached to it.

    **Statutes as sources**

    Legislatures make statutes within their allotted authority. These laws can develop constitutional arrangements by regulating elections, institutions and the exercise of public functions. A constitutional provision often leaves details to legislation, because the fundamental instrument cannot contain every administrative step. Yet the Act remains subordinate to the Constitution. Its maker must possess legislative competence and its contents must respect constitutional rights and procedures. Consequently, a statute may be constitutionally important without becoming part of the superior constitutional text. A parliamentary majority cannot avoid Article 1 by describing an inconsistent Act as necessary or popular.

    **Secondary legislation and institutional rules**

    Secondary legislation consists of rules made under a power conferred by an Act. It supplies detail within the scope of that authorization. Its validity therefore depends on two levels of authority. The rule must comply with the Constitution and remain within the enabling legislation. An administrative instruction cannot amend the Constitution or enlarge the power granted by Parliament. Care is also needed in classifying instruments. Rules of parliamentary procedure under Article 104 arise from a specific constitutional authority. An ordinance under Article 114 has the force and effect of an Act while constitutionally valid. It should not be confused with an ordinary departmental regulation.

    **Judicial decisions**

    Judicial decisions explain how general provisions apply to actual disputes. Article 128(2) recognizes the Supreme Court's final interpretive authority, while Article 128(4) requires compliance with its interpretations and legal principles. Such decisions develop constitutional understanding without allowing a reader to ignore the underlying text. A proper use of precedent requires attention to the issue, legally relevant facts, reasoning and operative order. The judgment may reproduce submissions that the court rejects. It may also apply a previous Constitution. Those distinctions prevent an argument from being mistaken for a holding or historical law from being presented as current law.

    **Conventions and customs**

    Constitutional operation includes practices that legal texts do not fully describe. Dicey's account of conventions explains how political rules can guide constitutional actors even when courts do not directly enforce them as law. Their sanctions may include political criticism or loss of support. This does not give them authority to defeat written provisions. In Nepal, a claim based on parliamentary practice must be tested against the Constitution. Custom likewise needs careful treatment. Long usage may be relevant, but repetition alone does not establish legal validity. The student should ask whether the legal system recognizes the practice and whether it is consistent with superior law.

    **Books and scholarly explanation**

    Books of authority help arrange difficult material, explain the history of an institution and compare competing interpretations. Their value is persuasive rather than automatically binding. The quality of evidence and reasoning matters more than the repetition of a celebrated name. A work on English constitutional law can illuminate conventions but cannot replace Nepal's provisions on government formation. Quotations require particular discipline. If the exact words and edition have not been checked, the idea should be paraphrased honestly. Attributing a convenient invented sentence to a jurist weakens an answer instead of giving it academic support.

    **Nepal's position and practical method**

    The practical method is to move from the highest applicable rule to the specific legal question. For a government-formation problem, Articles 74–76 identify the system and appointment framework. For an institutional rule, the Constitution and relevant enabling authority must both be considered. For an unconstitutional law or rights violation, Article 133 provides important Supreme Court jurisdiction, subject to its terms. A simple hypothetical shows the method. If a minister claims that a circular permits a restriction, the reader asks whether an Act authorizes it, whether the restriction fits that Act and whether the resulting interference is constitutionally permissible.

    The same method also explains why sources have different jobs. A constitutional clause may establish a right, an Act may specify an institution's procedure and a judgment may resolve a disputed interpretation. A textbook can explain their relationship, but does not gain their legal rank by repeating them. The student should therefore identify not only what a source says but why it matters to the particular question. This prevents useful background material from being confused with an enforceable rule.

    **Critical view and conclusion**

    No source should be read in isolation. A written guarantee may be weakened by poor implementation, while a political custom may assist responsible government without becoming enforceable law. Constitutional interpretation must therefore combine accurate source identification with attention to institutional practice. The Constitution remains the superior legal standard in Nepal. Valid statutes, authorized rules and judicial interpretations develop its operation, while conventions and scholarly works help explain its working. Understanding this relationship produces a stronger answer than treating every official document, political habit and textbook passage as equally binding constitutional law.

??? example "Q2. Distinguish a Constitution from constitutional law and explain the place of conventions and judicial decisions. (20 marks)"
    **Introduction**

    A Constitution and constitutional law are closely related, but they are not identical. The Constitution is the state's fundamental legal framework. Constitutional law is the broader body of law concerning the organization, exercise and limitation of public power. The distinction matters because many practical constitutional questions cannot be answered by reading one clause alone. Relevant legislation and judicial interpretation may explain how the framework operates. Political conventions also influence conduct, although their role must be distinguished from binding law. For Nepal, every part of this discussion begins with the supremacy established by Article 1.

    **The Constitution as a fundamental instrument**

    The Constitution creates and limits institutions. It identifies the state's basic character, distributes authority and recognizes rights. Unlike an ordinary administrative order, it establishes the legal framework under which administrative power exists. Article 1 makes inconsistent law void to the extent of inconsistency. Article 2 locates sovereignty and state power in the people and requires constitutional exercise of that power. Together these provisions show that office holders derive authority from a legal arrangement. Holding high office or winning an election does not confer a general permission to disregard that arrangement whenever compliance becomes politically difficult.

    **The wider subject of constitutional law**

    Constitutional law examines the rules through which the fundamental framework operates. Dicey's treatment of rules affecting sovereign power helps explain this scope. It includes questions about how institutions are constituted, how powers are exercised and how legal limits are enforced. An election statute can therefore be relevant to constitutional law even though it is not itself the Constitution. Judicial decisions can clarify institutional powers, and authorized procedural rules can regulate legislative business. The subject is wider than the document because a constitutional system needs both a fundamental framework and legal arrangements that put its provisions into practical operation.

    **Differences in legal rank and amendment**

    The difference is also one of rank. An ordinary statute is valid only within constitutional authority. It does not acquire constitutional rank merely because its subject is important. Likewise, changing an administrative rule does not amend the Constitution. Constitutional amendment must follow Article 274, including the conditions relevant to the proposed change. This distinction prevents a legislature or executive from achieving indirectly what it cannot lawfully do directly. If an ordinary law could freely contradict the Constitution, constitutional supremacy would disappear. The superior framework would then depend entirely on the institution it was supposed to control.

    **Why judicial decisions matter**

    General legal language often raises questions when applied to specific events. Judicial decisions supply reasoned answers within the court's jurisdiction. Article 128(2) gives the Supreme Court final authority to interpret the Constitution and laws. Article 128(4) addresses compliance with the interpretations and legal principles it lays down. A decision can explain the meaning of a constitutional power without being a formal amendment. Its authority comes from the constitutional judicial function. The interpreter must still distinguish the legal principle actually decided from broader observations and from arguments merely reproduced in the judgment.

    **Reading precedent responsibly**

    The use of decisions requires attention to their setting. First identify the legal question and relevant facts. Then determine the applicable Constitution, the court's reasoning and its final direction. A historical dissolution judgment may illuminate the relation between political controversy and judicial review, but it does not automatically establish the meaning of Article 76 of the 2015 Constitution. Different texts may allocate powers differently. Similarly, a decision that rejects one particular claim does not necessarily reject every claim concerning the same institution. Reading the actual judgment avoids turning a narrow legal conclusion into an unsupported universal rule.

    **The meaning of constitutional convention**

    A constitutional convention is a political rule governing conduct that is not directly enforceable merely as a legal rule. Dicey's distinction between legal rules and conventions is useful because governments operate through both legal authority and accepted standards of responsibility. A convention can guide the use of a lawful discretion and help institutions cooperate. Its political importance does not make its breach identical to the breach of an enforceable constitutional duty. Where the Constitution itself specifies a requirement, however, compliance is a legal obligation. Calling that requirement a convention would wrongly weaken its authority.

    **Conventions, customs and habits**

    Not every repeated practice is a constitutional convention. A habit may reflect convenience without a belief that constitutional actors ought to follow it. A convention involves a normative expectation of proper conduct. A legally recognized custom raises another question, because its force depends on recognition within the legal system. These categories should not be collapsed. Nor can repeated unconstitutional conduct acquire validity merely because it occurs often. In Nepal, the written framework sets the legal limit. A political expectation can help explain conduct within that framework, but cannot supply a superior power to contradict it.

    **Nepal's constitutional position**

    The Nepal example is especially clear in government formation. Article 74 identifies a parliamentary system, while Article 76 provides the appointment framework. One cannot replace those provisions with a claim that every parliamentary prime minister elsewhere possesses a particular power. The legal answer must follow Nepal's text and relevant judicial interpretation. The same method applies to ordinances. Article 114 gives them the force and effect of an Act under stated conditions, but that does not place an ordinance above the Constitution. Classification, authority and limits remain separate questions, even when political actors prefer to discuss only the convenience of their chosen instrument.

    The distinction also affects how an answer is organized. Begin by identifying the constitutional question, then state the controlling legal source and explain how other materials assist its application. If an issue concerns an institution's power, a general scholarly account is useful only after the legal allocation is established. If the issue concerns practice, distinguish what officials commonly do from what they are legally permitted to do. This organization keeps the wider subject connected to the fundamental instrument without treating every source as interchangeable.

    **Critical view and conclusion**

    A Constitution can be formally strong yet poorly observed. Responsible practice therefore matters alongside legal enforcement. However, an answer should not respond to this practical truth by treating politics as a replacement for law. Constitutional law connects the framework, its legal implementation and its authoritative interpretation. Conventions and books help us understand operation and purpose, while courts and legal sources determine enforceable obligations within their proper authority. The Constitution is the central instrument, constitutional law is the wider legal subject, and a sound analysis explains both their connection and their different legal roles.

## One-minute revision

- Ask who may act, through what procedure and within what limits.
- Constitution is the fundamental instrument; constitutional law is the wider subject.

- Read books as explanation and verify every quotation.
