## Facts

Translated and summarised by us from the transcription. Paragraph numbers are the transcription's.

**The speech.** On 18 Asar 2035, during the debate on the royal address, Ganesh Kumar Pokharel, a member of the Rastriya Panchayat (राष्ट्रिय पञ्चायत), the national legislature of the Panchayat system, said it was "very sad that one can get true justice even up to the Supreme Court only with a bundle (पोको)". A "bundle" here means a bundle of money: a bribe. The Gorkhapatra daily printed this on 19 Asar (paragraph 2).

**The contempt petition.** Sarbagya Ratna Tuladhar, president of the Nepal Bar Association, asked the Supreme Court to act for contempt of court (अदालतको अवहेलना) under Article 68(2) of the Constitution and section 6 of the Supreme Court Act 2019. On 21 Asar a Division Bench ordered four people to show cause why they should not be punished: Ram Hari Sharma, chairman of the Rastriya Panchayat; Pokharel; the general manager of the national news agency; and the editor of Gorkhapatra (paragraph 2). This contempt case was criminal miscellaneous No. 92 (फौ.वि.नं. ९२).

**Pokharel's correction.** Pokharel wrote to the chairman that he meant to say "even high officials are said to be affected by bundles of money". He said the words "Supreme Court" slipped out by mistake. On the chairman's order, a correction was sent to Gorkhapatra on 21 Asar and printed on 22 Asar. The court noted this came only after its show-cause order (paragraphs 2 and 7).

**The privilege motion.** On 22 Asar the show-cause order was reported in the weekly *Rashtra Pukar*. That same day, member Radha Prasad Ghimire, seconded by Hari Narayan Rajauriya, moved that the House's privilege had been breached and the matter should go to a committee. The motion passed unanimously. On 25 Asar a Privileges Committee (विशेषाधिकार समिति) was formed under Matrika Prasad Koirala (paragraph 2).

**The committee acts against the judges and the petitioner.** The committee decided Pokharel need not appear before the Supreme Court and told the court so on 26 Asar. It also decided to:

- summon Tuladhar to appear before it at noon on 27 Asar, to answer a charge of breaching the House's privilege; and
- ask the Chief Justice and Justice Basudev Sharma, who had made the show-cause order, for a written reply within seven days on why action should not be taken for breach of privilege.

Acting deputy secretary Raj Bahadur Mishra sent these letters (paragraph 2).

**The writ and the interim order.** On 27 Asar Tuladhar filed this writ under Articles 16 and 71. He asked for an order of prohibition stopping any action against him. The same day the Division Bench issued a show-cause order and an interim order under rule 58(3) of the Supreme Court Rules 2021 that no action be taken against him until the petition was decided (paragraph 2).

**Arrest despite the interim order.** The chairman of the Rastriya Panchayat did not reply himself. Instead, the House secretary wrote to the court on the chairman's order, calling the court's order unconstitutional and accusing the bench of dragging the House in "lightly". The chairman also sent the Home Ministry a warrant for Tuladhar's arrest. Tuladhar was arrested, kept in police custody and brought before the committee on 23 Shrawan, where his statement was taken (paragraphs 2 and 6).

**The committee's finding.** The committee held Tuladhar guilty of breaching the privilege of the "supreme legislature". Because the matter also involved the Supreme Court, it recommended closing the matter there "if the status quo holds and nothing else happens". The House adopted this (paragraph 2).

**Meanwhile, the contempt case.** On 2035/4/5 the Division Bench closed the contempt case against Pokharel and others without punishment (placed on file, तामेलीमा). The court later explained it did so because Pokharel admitted his mistake and the newspaper apologised (paragraph 26).

**Split in the Division Bench.** Both judges agreed the House's action was unconstitutional. Justice Basudev Sharma would quash it and issue prohibition. Justice Heramba Raj held that no order was needed: the committee had reported and dissolved, and rule 145 of the Rastriya Panchayat Rules stopped a later session from taking up a breach from an earlier one. Because they differed, the petition went to the Full Bench under rule 33 of the Supreme Court Rules 2021 (paragraph 3).

**No appearance by the respondents.** The respondents were given notice of the hearing but did not appear or send a representative. The court decided on their written replies and the committee's report (paragraph 4).

## Issues

The Full Bench framed three questions (paragraph 10):

1. Was it constitutional for the Rastriya Panchayat to raise a privilege question over the Supreme Court's contempt proceedings, pass a motion, form a committee and proceed against the Chief Justice, Justice Basudev Sharma and the petitioner?
2. Could the Privileges Committee issue a summons? Was it unconstitutional for the chairman to issue a warrant and have the petitioner arrested and brought before the committee?
3. Should an order of prohibition be issued as the petitioner asked?

## Arguments

**For the petitioner** (Senior Advocates Shambhu Prasad Gyawali, Kusum Shrestha and Laxman Prasad Aryal; Advocates Jhulendra Prasad Chhatkuli and Daman Dhungana):

- The respondents had no power to raise privilege over a case pending in the Supreme Court. Doing so went against Articles 45, 51 and 68(2) (paragraph 5).
- Proceeding against a person for asking the court to act in contempt broke his rights to equal protection of law and not to be punished except under law, under Articles 10(1) and 11(3) (paragraph 5).
- Only the chairman, not the committee, could issue such orders, under Article 51(7)(b) and rule 147(4) of the Rastriya Panchayat Rules 2033 (paragraph 5).
- The words "subject to the provisions of this Constitution" in Article 68(2) qualify only "court of record", not the contempt power (paragraph 12).
- Privilege is objective. The House cannot widen it by interpreting the Constitution itself; the court decides (paragraph 19).
- The House's "status quo" condition meant the matter could be raised again, so prohibition and certiorari were still needed (paragraph 37).

**For the respondents** (from their written replies and the committee report; no one appeared):

- Privilege falls within Article 51, so the court cannot act. The interim order under rule 58 was itself against Article 51 (paragraph 8).
- The chairman and the committee may issue warrants and summonses, call witnesses and take statements. Rule 148 required giving the accused a chance to defend (paragraph 8).
- Article 51(1), (2), (5) and (6) gave complete immunity from court proceedings for anything said in the House (paragraph 13).
- The Division Bench's decision of 2035/4/5 had settled the matter in the House's favour (paragraphs 8 and 26).
- Courts in India, the United Kingdom, the United States, Australia and Canada had accepted legislative privilege or supremacy (paragraphs 24 to 31).

## Decision

1. **The privilege proceedings were wholly unconstitutional.** Tuladhar's contempt petition and the Supreme Court's contempt proceedings were lawful under Article 68(2) and the Supreme Court Act. They did not breach the House's privilege (paragraph 33).
2. **The summons and the warrant were unconstitutional.** Under Article 51(7)(b), only the person presiding over the Rastriya Panchayat could issue an arrest order or summons or take statements. The committee could not. The summons sent on its decision broke Article 51(7)(b), and the chairman's warrant, issued only because Tuladhar did not obey that summons, was unconstitutional too (paragraph 36).
3. **No writ issued.** The proceedings and the House's adoption of the report were void from the start (शून्य), so the charge against Tuladhar had no effect, and no certiorari was needed. Prohibition was not needed either: the proceedings had ended, and under rule 145 a later session could not take them up. The Full Bench agreed with Justice Heramba Raj's view (paragraph 38).

## Ratio decidendi

Our translation of the headnote points in the transcription:

1. Ignoring a court's order is not proper. (प्र.नं. ६)
2. Once a bench has issued an order, it cannot be left unserved. (प्र.नं. ७)
3. The Supreme Court's power to punish contempt cannot be read as restricted. (प्र.नं. १२)
4. Adding the word "full" to the privilege of free speech does not make it unlimited or beyond review. (प्र.नं. १५)
5. A court cannot accept an unconstitutional claim. (प्र.नं. १७)
6. Supreme Court judges are not answerable to the Rastriya Panchayat, so their conduct cannot be discussed there. (प्र.नं. १७)
7. The Rastriya Panchayat cannot itself interpret the Constitution to fix the form or nature of its privilege. (प्र.नं. १९)
8. A person who, against Article 45(3), says something in the House that is contempt of court cannot claim immunity from court proceedings under Article 51(1). (प्र.नं. २३)
9. No question may be raised in the House about a case pending in the Supreme Court, against Article 45(4). (प्र.नं. २३)
10. A court does not act from revenge or anger. (प्र.नं. २६)
11. A court does not inflame a situation or add to needless noise or dispute. (प्र.नं. २६)
12. The Constitution leaves no room to copy who accepted whose supremacy in other countries. (प्र.नं. ३१)

The core reasoning, in plain words:

- **"Subject to this Constitution" controls the whole clause.** Article 51(1) begins with "subject to the provisions of this Constitution and the rules of the National Panchayat". Under the rules of interpretation, that phrase governs both the free speech and the immunity from court. So both are limited (paragraph 15). No freedom is unlimited; "freedom without limit turns into arbitrariness and in the end destroys itself" (paragraph 15).
- **Article 45 directly limits debate.** It forbids discussing a judge's conduct in his duties and any case pending in a court. Speech that crosses those limits is not protected by Article 51(1). Otherwise the limits would be meaningless (paragraphs 16 and 17).
- **The House decides breaches; the court decides what privilege is.** Article 51(6) lets the House decide whether a privilege was breached and punish under 51(7). It does not let the House interpret the Constitution. When the scope of privilege itself is disputed, the court is the final interpreter. "The legislature's job is to legislate, not to interpret" (paragraphs 19 to 23).
- **Contempt power is not limited by place or office.** The court read Article 68(2) by its grammar: "subject to the provisions of this Constitution" sits in the first clause, about being a court of record, and does not reach the contempt power in the second clause (paragraphs 11 and 12).
- **Foreign examples do not fit.** India's Article 105 places free speech under the Constitution but gives separate, absolute immunity in clause (2). Nepal's Article 51(1) puts both in one clause under the same limit. British "dualism" grew because privilege there was never written down; in Nepal it was created and defined by the Constitution (paragraphs 24 to 30).

## Why it matters

- It is the leading Panchayat-era case on the **limits of legislative privilege**. Even "full" freedom of speech in the House was subject to the Constitution.
- It settled that **the court, not the legislature, decides the scope of privilege**, while the House keeps the power to decide whether a breach happened.
- It protected **judicial independence**: a legislature cannot use privilege to punish judges or a person who asks the court to act in contempt.
- It shows **separation of powers** and **constitutional supremacy** argued under a system where sovereignty lay with the King (paragraph 31).

### Then and now

| Then (as in the judgment) | Now (Constitution of Nepal 2015, text checked) |
|---|---|
| Art 51(1): full freedom of speech, **subject to this Constitution and the House rules**, and immunity from court for things said | Art 103(1): full freedom of speech and immunity from prosecution. The opening "subject to this Constitution" words are **not** in 103(1); Art 103(2) on internal business does say "subject to this Constitution" |
| Art 45: no discussion of a judge's conduct in duty or of pending cases | Art 105: no discussion that may affect justice in sub judice cases, or of judges' judicial acts, except in an impeachment motion |
| Art 51(6): breach of privilege is contempt of the House; the House decides breaches | Art 103(7): same idea, with "exclusive right" of the House |
| Art 51(7)(b): only the person presiding may issue arrest orders or summonses | Art 103(8): presiding person may admonish, warn, jail up to three months or fine up to Rs 10,000 after the House decides. Art 103(9): other matters by federal law |
| Art 68(2): Supreme Court is a court of record with power to punish contempt | Art 128(2) and (4): court of record; contempt proceedings "in accordance with law" |
| Supreme Court Act 2019, s 6: up to 1 year or Rs 2,000 for contempt | Not checked here; the Act was replaced. **Unverified** which statute now sets the penalty |

Whether today's courts would read Article 103(1) the same way, given its different wording, is not something this case decides. Do not cite Tuladhar as a holding on Article 103.

## Full 20-mark answer

??? example "Exam answer (20 marks)"
    *Practice question (not taken from a paper we hold):* "Parliamentary privilege is not absolute." Discuss with reference to Sarbagya Ratna Tuladhar v Chairman, Rastriya Panchayat.

    **Introduction**

    Parliamentary privilege means the special rights a legislature and its members need to do their work freely, such as free speech in the House and immunity from court for what they say there. The question is how far these rights go when they meet the courts' power to protect justice. In *Sarbagya Ratna Tuladhar v Chairman, Rastriya Panchayat* the Full Bench of the Supreme Court of Nepal answered that, under the Constitution of Nepal 2019 (1962), privilege was limited by the Constitution, and the court decides its scope.

    **Citation**

    Senior Advocate Sarbagya Ratna Tuladhar (for the Nepal Bar Association) v Chairman, Rastriya Panchayat et al., NKP 2035, D.N. 1197, Full Bench writ No. 35 of 2035, decided 2035/9/25. The order was written by Justice Bishwanath Upadhyaya.

    **Facts**

    During a debate in the Rastriya Panchayat on 18 Asar 2035, member Ganesh Kumar Pokharel said that true justice could be had "even up to the Supreme Court" only with a bundle of money. The Nepal Bar Association, through its president Tuladhar, asked the Supreme Court to act for contempt. The court issued a show-cause order to Pokharel, the House chairman and two press officials. The House then passed a privilege motion and set up a Privileges Committee. The committee summoned Tuladhar and asked the Chief Justice and another judge to explain why they should not be punished for breach of privilege. Tuladhar filed a writ for prohibition and got an interim order protecting him. Despite that order, the chairman issued a warrant; Tuladhar was arrested and brought before the committee, which found him guilty.

    **Issues**

    1. Could the House raise privilege over the court's contempt proceedings and act against the judges and the petitioner?
    2. Could the committee issue a summons, and was the chairman's warrant lawful?
    3. Should prohibition be issued?

    **Decision**

    The court held the whole privilege proceeding unconstitutional. The contempt petition and the court's contempt proceedings were lawful under Article 68(2) and section 6 of the Supreme Court Act 2019. Under Article 51(7)(b) only the person presiding over the House could summon or order arrest; the committee could not, so its summons and the warrant based on it were unconstitutional. Because the proceedings were void from the start and had ended, and rule 145 of the House rules stopped a later session reopening them, no writ was issued.

    **Ratio decidendi**

    1. Article 51(1) opened with "subject to the provisions of this Constitution and the rules". That phrase governs both free speech and immunity. The word "full" does not make privilege unlimited.
    2. Article 45 forbade discussing judges' conduct and pending cases. Speech that breaks those limits gets no immunity under Article 51(1).
    3. Article 51(6) lets the House decide whether a breach happened. It does not let the House interpret the Constitution to widen its privilege. The court is the final interpreter of the Constitution and of the scope of privilege.
    4. The Supreme Court's contempt power is not restricted by the place where contempt is committed or the office of the person.

    **Comment**

    The court relied on Wade and Phillips and on Erskine May: no House can create a new privilege by its own resolution, and privilege is "objective" and known to the courts. It distinguished India, whose Article 105 grants absolute immunity in a separate clause, and rejected the British idea of "dualism" between Parliament and courts because Nepal's privilege was created by a written constitution. It also showed judicial restraint: the contempt case against Pokharel had been closed once he admitted his mistake, and in this case the court declared the law but did not issue a writ that was no longer needed. A criticism is that a declaration without a writ leaves enforcement to the other body's good sense. The case was also decided under a constitution that vested sovereignty in the King, so its reasoning about the separation of powers works inside that system.

    **Significance and conclusion**

    The case is the classic Nepali authority that privilege is a shield for legislative work, not a sword against the courts. Today the Constitution of Nepal 2015 deals with privilege in Article 103 and limits debate on judges and pending cases in Article 105, while Article 128(4) gives the Supreme Court contempt power. Article 103(1) does not repeat the "subject to this Constitution" words that the 1962 Article 51(1) began with, so Tuladhar's reasoning on that phrase cannot simply be transferred. Its broader points, that a written constitution limits every organ and that the court interprets it, remain a strong argument that parliamentary privilege is not absolute.
