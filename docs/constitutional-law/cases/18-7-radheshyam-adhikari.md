## Facts

Translated and summarised by us from the Nepali judgment.

**The appointment.** Kalyan Bikram Adhikari was already the Royal Nepalese Ambassador (शाही नेपाली राजदूत) to France. On 2047/8/27, the petition says, the King also appointed him ambassador to Spain and Israel. The petitioner learned this from a notice in the government newspaper Gorkhapatra. The court says the notice was published in Gorkhapatra on 28 Marga 2047 (paragraph 15). The notice did not say the appointment was made on the advice and consent of the Council of Ministers (मन्त्री परिषद्), that is, the cabinet (paragraph 2).

**The minister's interview.** The weekly paper Matribhumi (मातृभूमि), year 23, issue 48, dated 2047/9/3, quoted Law and Justice Minister Nilambar Acharya. It reported him as saying the appointment did not follow the Constitution (paragraph 2).

**The comparison.** The petitioner pointed to another appointment. When the King named a member of the Judicial Council (न्याय परिषद्) under Article 93, Gorkhapatra's notice said it was done on the cabinet's advice. The ambassador notice said nothing of the kind (paragraph 2).

**The petition.** Advocate Radheshyam Adhikari, of Kathmandu Municipality Ward 10, filed a writ petition. He used Article 88(2), which let the Supreme Court settle a constitutional or legal question in a "dispute of public interest or concern" (सार्वजनिक हक वा सरोकारको विवाद). He asked the court to declare the appointment void (paragraphs 1 and 2).

**In the Supreme Court.** On 2047/11/23 a single judge held the preliminary hearing. He asked for written replies (लिखित जवाफ) from all respondents except the King's Principal Press Secretariat. Chief Secretary Damodar Prasad Gautam replied for the cabinet. The ambassador, Kalyan Bikram Adhikari, did not reply (paragraph 1).

## Issues

The court said this was the first case it was deciding under Article 88(2). So it wanted to explain the clause. It framed three questions (paragraph 8):

1. What kind of dispute is a "dispute of public interest or concern" under Article 88(2)? Why does the Constitution have this clause? Is the petitioner's dispute one the court should settle under it?
2. Who has standing (हकदैया, locus standi) to bring such a dispute to the court? Standing is the right to bring a case because one has a lawful interest in it. Does the petitioner have it?
3. Should the court issue the order the petitioner asked for?

## Arguments

**For the petitioner** (the petitioner himself, with Advocates Harihar Dahal and Badri Bahadur Karki):

- Under Article 35(2), the King does every act with the cabinet's advice and consent, unless the Constitution says the act is for the King alone, at his discretion, or on another body's recommendation. Article 120 says no such thing about ambassadors. So the cabinet must advise (paragraphs 2 and 5).
- The Judicial Council notice mentioned the cabinet's advice. The ambassador notice did not. With the minister's statement, this shows the advice was never given (paragraph 5).
- An ambassador answers to the cabinet through the Foreign Minister. Under constitutional monarchy the King himself answers for nothing. An appointment made without the cabinet leaves no one answerable. It also drags the King into controversy (paragraph 2).
- The petition said this harmed his fundamental rights under Articles 11(1) and 16. It relied on Articles 23 and 88(2) (paragraph 2).
- Once an appointment is made, the cabinet's recommendation cannot stay secret (paragraph 5).

**For the respondents** (Attorney General Moti Kaji Sthapit and Joint Attorney General Narendra Kumar Shrestha):

- The cabinet's written reply said Article 41 rules govern how the cabinet gives advice under Article 35(2). Article 41(2) bars any court from asking whether those rules were followed. Article 35(6) bars it too. The rules require such business to be kept secret, so Article 16 does not force disclosure (paragraph 4).
- Notices for ambassadors normally say only "under the Constitution of the Kingdom of Nepal 2047". That means Articles 120 and 35(2) were followed. Notices for honours (Article 123) and pardons (Article 122) also do not mention the cabinet, though both need its recommendation (paragraph 6).
- A question that cannot be raised in court cannot be decided by a court. Article 88(2) is only for questions the law can settle (paragraph 6).
- Acharya was not the cabinet's spokesperson. The interview was not official. The petition rested on suspicion and guesswork (paragraphs 4 and 7).

## Decision

The writ petition was dismissed (खारेज, paragraph 18). The court did not decide the petitioner's standing against him (paragraph 14). It held that the dispute was not one it could settle, because Article 35(6) barred it from asking what advice the cabinet gave the King (paragraph 18).

## Ratio decidendi

Our translation of the official headnote, point by point:

1. A dispute of public interest or concern is not limited to the private right or interest of a particular person. It concerns the collective right or interest of the general public or of a community. Whether a dispute is of public interest depends on whether it concerns the public's or a community's collective right or interest, or that of particular persons. (प्रकरण नं. ९)
2. Article 120(1), on appointing ambassadors, does not say the appointment is made by the King alone, at his discretion, or on another body's recommendation. So the appointment must be made with the cabinet's advice and consent under Article 35(2). (प्रकरण नं. १७)
3. The right under Article 16 to ask for and receive information on a matter of public importance is not unlimited. (प्रकरण नं. १८)
4. The petitioner presented this as a public interest dispute by guessing from a minister's unauthorised statement. It is not really a matter this court can settle under the Constitution. Article 35(6) bars the court from inquiring into it, so the court cannot check what process was followed for the appointment or reach a firm conclusion. (प्रकरण नं. १८)

### The reasoning in the body of the judgment

The headnote leaves out the standing test, which is the part most often cited. These points come from the order itself.

**Public interest is not new, but Article 88(2) is wider** (paragraph 8). Nepal's law already knew group disputes over shared irrigation channels (कुलो), boundaries, grazing land (गौचर), drainage and public roads. Article 88(2) went further. It gave the court the same extraordinary power for public interest disputes as for fundamental rights.

**"Right" and "concern" differ** (paragraph 10). A right (हक) is something the law or the Constitution clearly gives: a privilege, facility, immunity or claim. Concern (सरोकार) cannot be defined exactly. Security, governance, education, health, prices and social justice are all public concerns. But to come to court, the dispute must rest on law or the Constitution. Mere interest or curiosity is not enough. The dispute must be real, not imagined, and fit for a court to decide.

**Why the clause exists** (paragraph 11). Public bodies sometimes fail in their legal duties or misuse their powers, and the law gives no remedy. Shutting the court's door then would leave the public interest unprotected and could make government arbitrary. But the clause does not help people who want to test the legality of government acts as an intellectual whim, seek publicity, or test the court's patience. The court should discourage that.

**The standing test** (paragraph 12). Usually only a person whose own right is harmed can come to court; habeas corpus is the exception. Article 88(2) widened standing for public interest disputes, so the petitioner's own right need not be harmed. But not just anyone may sue. In the Chief Justice's view, the petitioner must have:

- a **meaningful relation** (सार्थक सम्बन्ध) or **substantial interest** (तात्विक सरोकार) in the subject of the dispute, and
- the ability to represent fairly the public whose interest is involved, and to satisfy the court of this.

If anyone could bring any public dispute, the flood of petitions could hurt the court's work and defeat the clause's purpose.

**Why Article 88(1) is different** (paragraph 13). Under Article 88(1), any citizen could challenge a law that unreasonably restricts fundamental rights or conflicts with the Constitution, even if not personally affected. If the framers had wanted the same open standing for Article 88(2), they would not have needed two separate clauses. Also, people who really have an interest should not have a court's solution forced on them by an outsider.

**This petitioner's standing** (paragraph 14). An educated citizen and lawyer would naturally be interested in whether the Constitution is followed. But the petition does not show why his interest in appointing one ambassador was substantial. It looked like ordinary curiosity. Still, the government did not object, and the case raised important constitutional questions. So the court chose not to decide the case on standing.

**Collective responsibility** (paragraph 17). The cabinet is a collective body (Collective body) led by the Prime Minister. It is collectively responsible to Parliament. One minister's personal view has no constitutional weight. Ministers must not make cabinet matters public without the cabinet's or Prime Minister's permission. The oath of office and secrecy under Article 40 reflects this, and the duty continues after leaving office. In any case, Article 35(2) says the advice reaches the King through the Prime Minister. So only what the Prime Minister submitted matters, not what a minister told a newspaper.

**Why the advice is kept from the courts** (paragraph 18). The relationship between the King and those who advise him must be confidential by nature. If who advised what, and who argued for or against, were made public, advisers might stop speaking freely or avoid responsibility. So the Constitution kept such advice outside judicial inquiry. When a final decision is made, the court must presume the King and the cabinet agreed. Any disagreement that remained could not be solved by a court. The Constitution treats this relationship as political, to be settled by constitutional convention and practical politics.

## Why it matters

- It was the first case decided under Article 88(2) of the 1990 Constitution (paragraph 8). It defined a dispute of public interest and set the "meaningful relation or substantial interest" test for standing. Later public interest litigation (PIL) in Nepal builds on these ideas.
- It shows that **winning on principle and losing the case can go together**. The court agreed with the petitioner that ambassadors must be appointed on cabinet advice (paragraph 17), but dismissed the petition because it could not inquire into that advice (paragraph 18).
- It treats the right to information as limited by law that requires secrecy (paragraph 18).
- It is an early statement that some questions between a head of state and the cabinet are political questions, not legal ones (paragraph 18). Compare the later dissolution cases in [Unit 5](../unit-05-separation-of-powers.md) and [Unit 11](../unit-11-federal-legislature.md).

### Then and now

| Then (as in the judgment) | Now |
|---|---|
| Art 88(2), 1990: settling a constitutional or legal question in a dispute of public interest or concern | Art 133(2), 2015: the same words, "any dispute of public interest or concern". Checked against the Law Commission English text saved on 2026-11-18 |
| Art 120(1), 1990: "His Majesty shall appoint the Royal Nepalese Ambassadors" | Art 282(1), 2015: the President may, on the basis of the principle of inclusion, appoint Nepali ambassadors |
| Art 35(2), 1990: the King acts on the cabinet's advice and consent, submitted through the Prime Minister, unless the Constitution says otherwise | Art 66(2), 2015: the President acts on the recommendation and consent of the Council of Ministers, submitted through the Prime Minister, except where the Constitution or federal law names another body |
| Art 35(6), 1990: no court may ask whether, or what, advice was given to the King (as quoted in paragraph 18) | No matching bar in Art 66(1)-(3), 2015. Our search of the 2015 English text for "questioned in any court" found nothing. Whether another provision or law has the same effect is not checked |
| Art 16, 1990: right to information on matters of public importance, with a secrecy proviso | Art 27, 2015: right to information on matters of one's own interest or of public interest; no one is compelled to give information that must be kept confidential by law |
| Art 41, 1990: rules for conducting government business, which courts could not question (Art 41(2), as the judgment describes it) | Not compared. The 2015 equivalent was not checked for this page |

## Full 20-mark answer

??? example "Exam answer (20 marks)"
    Practice question (not from a past paper): *Discuss the facts, issues, decision and ratio decidendi of Advocate Radheshyam Adhikari v Council of Ministers Secretariat et al. (NKP 2048). What is its importance for public interest litigation in Nepal?*

    **Introduction**

    A court usually hears a person whose own right has been harmed. Public interest litigation (PIL) lets a person bring a dispute that affects the public as a whole. The Constitution of the Kingdom of Nepal 2047 opened this door in Article 88(2). *Radheshyam Adhikari v Council of Ministers Secretariat* was the first case the Supreme Court decided under that clause. It explained what a public interest dispute is and who may bring one.

    **Citation**

    Advocate Radheshyam Adhikari v His Majesty's Government, Council of Ministers Secretariat, Singha Durbar, et al., NKP 2048, No. 12, D.N. 4430, Writ No. 989 of 2047. A Special Bench decided it on 2048/12/17. Chief Justice Bishwanath Upadhyaya wrote the order; Justices Surendra Prasad Singh and Gajendra Keshari Bastola agreed.

    **Facts**

    Kalyan Bikram Adhikari was the Royal Nepalese Ambassador to France. The King also appointed him ambassador to Spain and Israel, as a Gorkhapatra notice showed. The notice did not say the cabinet had advised the appointment. A weekly, Matribhumi, quoted Law Minister Nilambar Acharya as saying the appointment did not follow the Constitution. Advocate Radheshyam Adhikari filed a writ under Article 88(2). He asked the court to declare the appointment void because the cabinet had not given advice and consent under Article 35(2). The cabinet replied that Articles 35(6) and 41(2) barred courts from examining its advice, and that the minister's interview was not official.

    **Issues**

    The court framed three issues. First, what is a dispute of public interest or concern under Article 88(2), and is this such a dispute? Second, who has standing to bring such a dispute, and does the petitioner? Third, should the order sought be issued?

    **Decision**

    The petition was dismissed. The court agreed that an ambassador must be appointed on the cabinet's advice, because Article 120 did not make it a matter for the King alone. But the claim that no advice was given rested only on a minister's unauthorised statement in a newspaper. Article 35(6) barred the court from asking what advice was given. So the court could not decide the question.

    **Ratio decidendi**

    First, a public interest dispute concerns the collective right or interest of the public or a community, not a particular person's private interest. It need not affect most people. But it must rest on law or the Constitution, be real rather than imagined, and be fit for a court to decide. Curiosity is not enough.

    Second, standing in public interest disputes is wider than usual. The petitioner's own right need not be harmed. But he must show a meaningful relation or substantial interest in the subject, and that he can fairly represent the public concerned. This is narrower than Article 88(1), under which any citizen could challenge an unconstitutional law.

    Third, under Article 35(2) the King acts on cabinet advice unless the Constitution says otherwise, so ambassadors are appointed on that advice.

    Fourth, the right to information under Article 16 is limited. Information that law requires to be kept secret need not be given. Advice given to the King is protected in this way.

    **Comment**

    The judgment is careful. It opened the court to public interest cases but set limits so the court would not be flooded with publicity-seeking petitions. Some may criticise the result. The court admitted the petitioner's interest looked like mere curiosity, yet it went on to decide important questions anyway because the government had not objected. Its treatment of the King-cabinet relationship as political also left a constitutional duty, advice before appointment, without a court remedy. The 2015 Constitution keeps the same public interest words in Article 133(2). Article 66(2) keeps the advice rule for the President. We did not find Article 35(6)'s bar repeated in Article 66.

    **Significance and conclusion**

    Radheshyam Adhikari is the foundation of PIL in Nepal. Its definition of public interest and its "meaningful relation or substantial interest" test for standing are still taught and cited. It also shows the limits of judicial power: a court can state what the Constitution requires but still decline to examine matters the Constitution places beyond its inquiry.
