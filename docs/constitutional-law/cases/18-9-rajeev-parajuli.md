## Facts

Translated and summarised by us from the Nepali judgment on NKP.

**The royal takeover and the emergency.** On 2061/10/19 (Magh 19) King Gyanendra declared a state of emergency across the kingdom under Article 115(1) of the 1990 Constitution. The royal palace notice gave the reason as a grave crisis to the "sovereignty, integrity and security" of the kingdom. It suspended several fundamental rights, but kept the right to habeas corpus (paragraph 33).

**The Commission is created.** On 2061/11/5 the King issued an order under Article 115(7) creating the Royal Commission for Corruption Control (RCCC). Article 115(7) let the King issue orders "to remedy" an emergency, and said such orders work like law only while the emergency lasts (paragraph 34). Under section 2 of the order, the Commission could investigate and act against smuggling, revenue evasion, irregular contracts, commissions and other corruption. Section 2(4) gave it all the powers of a corruption investigator and of the Special Court under the Special Court Act 2059. Section 6 let it punish anyone it found guilty, and section 6(2) allowed an appeal to the Supreme Court (paragraphs 40 and 43).

**The emergency ends but the Commission continues.** On 2062/1/16 (Baisakh 16) the King withdrew the emergency under Article 115(11). The same day he issued an order under Article 127, the "removal of difficulties" (बाधा अड्काउ फुकाउने) power, to keep the Commission going (paragraphs 7, 37 and 43).

**Rajeev Parajuli.** Rajeev Parajuli had been a minister five times. The Commission for the Investigation of Abuse of Authority (CIAA, अख्तियार दुरुपयोग अनुसन्धान आयोग), set up by the Constitution, had already looked into his family's property. On 2061/1/23 it decided to shelve (तामेली) the matter (paragraphs 2 and 17). The RCCC then opened its own inquiry into him. It says it called him on 2061/12/18 and that he cooperated at first (paragraph 7). On 2062/5/13 he appeared for his statement, which was not finished that day, so he was sent to custody at the Lalitpur District Police Office. On 2062/5/15 the Commission ordered him to deposit Rs 51,00,000 as bail under section 7(d) of the Special Court Act 2059. He could not pay, so he was held in custody (paragraphs 2, 7 and 15).

**The petition.** His younger brother, Sanjeev Parajuli, filed a writ petition for habeas corpus, quo warranto and any other suitable order under Articles 23 and 88(1)–(2). It was heard with a similar writ (No. 57) by Advocate Santosh Mahato that also challenged the Commission (paragraph 28). On 2062/5/28 the Special Bench released Rajeev Parajuli on the personal guarantee of his lawyer, Shambhu Thapa, until the case was decided (paragraph 17). The bench heard arguments on many days from 2062/5/23 to 2062/8/29, from 39 lawyers in all (paragraph 18).

## Issues

The court framed five questions (paragraph 19):

1. What does the 1990 Constitution say about how state authority (राजकीयसत्ता) is used?
2. Can an act of the King be questioned in court and tested against the Constitution?
3. Is the dispute political or constitutional?
4. Were creating the RCCC and continuing it constitutional?
5. Should the order the petitioner asked for be issued?

## Arguments

**For the petitioner** (Senior Advocates Basudev Prasad Dhungana and Shrihari Aryal, with other advocates):

- The CIAA, a body the Constitution set up, had already cleared the property. Investigating it again broke the rule against being tried twice for the same offence in Article 14 (paragraph 3).
- Under Article 84, only courts and judicial bodies set up under the Constitution and law may use judicial power. The RCCC was not set up by law, and no qualifications were fixed for its members (paragraph 4).
- An Article 115(7) order lives only as long as the emergency. Article 127 cannot revive it once the emergency is withdrawn (paragraph 4).
- The RCCC took over work that Article 98 gives to the CIAA and that Article 85 gives to the courts (paragraph 4).
- One body that brings the case, investigates it and decides it breaks natural justice (paragraph 4).
- The order's appeal clause could not give the Supreme Court jurisdiction that the Constitution and Acts do not give (paragraph 4).

**For the respondents** (the Attorney General, government lawyers, and lawyers for the Commission and its members):

- The King acted in his own discretion under Nepal's constitutional custom. Article 31 bars questioning the King's acts in court, so the matter is not open to judicial review (paragraph 11).
- An Article 127 order is part of the Constitution, so it cannot be reviewed like an Act (paragraph 11).
- The RCCC was a tribunal of the kind Article 85 allows. Other Nepali laws, such as the Customs Act 2019, the National Parks and Wildlife Conservation Act 2029 and the Forest Act 2049, already let one body investigate and decide (paragraphs 10 and 11).
- Parajuli first accepted the Commission's process and challenged it only when he was detained. The "doctrine of election" stops a person from both accepting and rejecting the same process (paragraph 7).
- The Commission was created out of state necessity, to control widespread corruption, and the doctrine of necessity justified it (paragraphs 32 and 36).
- Some lawyers argued that the 1962 Constitution had not been repealed and that sovereignty remained with the King (paragraph 20).

## Decision

The Special Bench, unanimously:

- Declared the Article 127 order of 2062/1/16 that continued the RCCC void (अमान्य) from the date of judgment under Article 88(1). It did not rule on the 2061/11/5 order separately, because that order had already lapsed when the emergency was withdrawn (paragraph 49).
- Held that the RCCC could not continue and was dissolved (खारेज) from that day (paragraph 50).
- Issued habeas corpus, because the bail order and detention rested on the acts of an unconstitutional body. As Parajuli had already been released on 2062/5/28, nothing more was needed (paragraph 51).

## Ratio decidendi

Our translation of the official headnote, point by point:

1. Once the 1990 Constitution is in force, state authority and sovereignty are used only as that Constitution provides. It is wrong to argue that sovereignty still lies with the King as under the 1962 Constitution, or that the 1962 Constitution was never repealed. (प्रकरण नं. २२)
2. In a country with a written Constitution, the Constitution itself shares state power among the organs of the state. Breaking those rules is unconstitutional. (प्रकरण नं. २२)
3. When a constitutional dispute about the use of state power reaches the Supreme Court, the court must decide it. Article 88 gives it that duty, so it cannot refuse to hear a question of constitutionality. (प्रकरण नं. २५)
4. Hearing two writs on the same dispute together is proper case management, not a breach of law. (प्रकरण नं. २८)
5. A dispute about creating the RCCC must be decided under the Constitution's rules on state power. It cannot be labelled a political question. (प्रकरण नं. २९)
6. The doctrine of necessity cannot tear down the constitutional structure. It only allows dealing with an urgent situation that has no other solution, and never against a clear provision of the Constitution. (प्रकरण नं. ३८)
7. The CIAA is the body the Constitution chose to control corruption. Any act that bypasses or weakens a constitutional body's powers shakes the constitutional foundation and harms constitutionalism. (प्रकरण नं. ३९)
8. Under the order, both investigation and punishment rested with the RCCC. Saying that a separate unit inside the RCCC investigated has no legal weight. The Constitution keeps the investigating body and the deciding body separate, so giving both jobs to one body goes against its purpose. (प्रकरण नं. ४०)
9. The Constitution says an Article 115(7) order lasts only while the emergency lasts. Using another Article to continue such an order in effect continues the emergency, and is unconstitutional. (प्रकरण नं. ४२)
10. Earlier constitutions said a removal-of-difficulties order had the force of the Constitution. Article 127 of the 1990 Constitution does not, so such an order cannot be treated as part of the Constitution. (प्रकरण नं. ४३)
11. Article 127 may be used to end a constitutional vacuum or deadlock. It cannot be used to give power to take statements against a person's will, compel attendance or search property, because these affect fundamental rights. (प्रकरण नं. ४५)
12. The order gave the RCCC the powers of a court and the Special Court, so it carried the force of law. Not laying it before Parliament does not put it outside judicial review under Article 88(1)–(2). (प्रकरण नं. ४७)
13. The palace notices of 2061/11/5 and 2062/1/16 do not fit the purpose and spirit of Articles 84, 85, 88(3), 98, 115(7) and 127. The RCCC cannot continue and is dissolved. (प्रकरण नं. ४९, ५०)

## Why it matters

- **Rule of law over royal power.** The court tested an order of a reigning King against the Constitution and set it aside. It read Article 31 (no question about the King's acts in court) narrowly, relying on the Hari Prasad Nepal and Raviraj Bhandari dissolution cases (paragraph 23; see [Case 18.3](18-3-hari-prasad-nepal.md) and [Case 18.4](18-4-raviraj-bhandari.md)). See [Unit 4](../unit-04-rule-of-law.md).
- **Separation of powers.** One body could not investigate, prosecute and punish. Judicial power must go to courts or tribunals set up by law (paragraphs 40 and 43). See [Unit 5](../unit-05-separation-of-powers.md) and [Unit 13](../unit-13-judiciary.md).
- **Limits on emergency power.** Emergency orders die with the emergency. A removal-of-difficulties order cannot be used to keep them alive, or to take away fundamental rights (paragraphs 42 and 45). See [Unit 17](../unit-17-emergency-powers.md).
- **Political question and necessity.** Calling a dispute "political" does not take it out of court when it turns on the Constitution (paragraph 29). The doctrine of necessity is narrow (paragraph 38).

### Then and now

| Then (as in the judgment) | Now (Constitution of Nepal 2015) |
|---|---|
| Art 31: no question in court about the King's acts | No direct equivalent; the monarchy has been abolished |
| Art 84–85: judicial power used by courts and judicial bodies set up under the Constitution and law | Art 126 (courts to exercise powers relating to justice) |
| Art 88(1)–(2): Supreme Court's extraordinary jurisdiction | Art 133(2)–(3) |
| Art 98: CIAA investigates corruption and files cases in courts | Arts 238–239 (CIAA) |
| Art 115(1), (7), (11): emergency, orders to remedy it, withdrawal | Art 273 (emergency, by the President) |
| Art 127: King may issue orders to remove difficulties; laid before Parliament | Art 305: President, on the Government's recommendation, only until the first session of the Federal Parliament; orders go to Parliament for approval |

## Full 20-mark answer

??? example "Exam answer (20 marks)"
    *Practice question:* Explain the facts, issues and ratio of Rajeev Parajuli v Royal Commission for Corruption Control. How did the case apply the rule of law and the separation of powers?

    **Introduction**

    A constitution is only supreme if a court is willing to test the acts of the most powerful office against it. In Rajeev Parajuli v Royal Commission for Corruption Control, Nepal's Supreme Court tested an order of a reigning King. It declared that order void. The case is a leading Nepali authority on the rule of law, the separation of powers and the limits of emergency power.

    **Citation**

    Sanjeev Parajuli (for Rajeev Parajuli) v Royal Commission for Corruption Control, Harihar Bhawan, Lalitpur, et al., NKP 2062, No. 11, D.N. 7618, Writ No. 118 of 2062. A five-judge Special Bench decided it on 2062/11/01; Justice Min Bahadur Rayamajhi wrote the order and the others agreed.

    **Facts**

    On 2061/10/19 the King declared an emergency under Article 115(1) of the Constitution of the Kingdom of Nepal 2047, citing a grave crisis to sovereignty, integrity and security. On 2061/11/5 he created the Royal Commission for Corruption Control (RCCC) by an order under Article 115(7). The order let the Commission investigate corruption, use the Special Court's powers and punish offenders. On 2062/1/16 the King withdrew the emergency. The same day he used Article 127, the power to remove difficulties, to keep the Commission going.

    Former minister Rajeev Parajuli had already been investigated by the CIAA, which shelved the matter on 2061/1/23. The RCCC opened a fresh inquiry. On 2062/5/15 it demanded bail of Rs 51 lakh, and when he could not pay, he was detained. His brother Sanjeev filed for habeas corpus and quo warranto.

    **Issues**

    The court asked five questions. How does the 1990 Constitution allocate state authority? Can the King's acts be tested in court? Is the dispute political or constitutional? Were the creation and continuation of the RCCC constitutional? Should the writ be issued?

    **Decision**

    The bench declared the Article 127 order of 2062/1/16 void from the date of judgment and dissolved the RCCC. The original Article 115(7) order had already lapsed with the emergency, so the court did not need to strike it down. Because the detention rested on the acts of an unconstitutional body, the court issued habeas corpus. Parajuli had already been released on his lawyer's guarantee.

    **Ratio decidendi**

    First, after 1990 sovereignty and state authority are used only as the 1990 Constitution provides. The argument that sovereignty stayed with the King under the 1962 Constitution was rejected (paragraph 22).

    Second, Article 88 makes the Supreme Court decide constitutional disputes. Article 31 protects acts the King does in his own discretion under the Constitution. It does not shield acts that must be tested against the Constitution, such as this one. Calling such a dispute "political" does not change that (paragraphs 23–25 and 29).

    Third, an Article 115(7) order lasts only while the emergency lasts. Keeping it alive through Article 127 is in effect continuing the emergency, and that is unconstitutional (paragraph 42).

    Fourth, Article 127 is for ending a constitutional vacuum or deadlock where no other remedy exists. Unlike earlier constitutions, the 1990 text does not say such orders have constitutional force. Article 127 cannot be used to give power to compel statements, attendance or searches, because that affects fundamental rights (paragraphs 43–45).

    Fifth, the Constitution gives investigation of corruption to the CIAA under Article 98 and trials to the courts. Judicial bodies can be set up only by law under Articles 84–85. A body that both investigates and punishes goes against the Constitution's purpose (paragraphs 40 and 43).

    Sixth, the doctrine of necessity is narrow. It allows urgent action where there is no alternative, but never against a clear provision (paragraph 38).

    **Comment**

    *Rule of law.* Dicey's first meaning of the rule of law is that no one is punished except for a breach of law established before the ordinary courts. The RCCC was not created by any Act, its members had no fixed qualifications, and it acted as investigator and judge. The court refused to let detention rest on such a body. It also applied the rule of law to the head of state. That made the case important during the royal takeover.

    *Separation of powers.* The court relied on the 1990 Constitution's division of executive, legislative and judicial power (paragraph 21). Judicial power can only be exercised by courts and tribunals created by law. Combining prosecution and judgment in one body offends both the separation of powers and natural justice.

    *Judicial restraint.* The court was careful. It declared only the continuing order void, and only from the date of judgment. It did not strike down the lapsed emergency order. It also said that Article 127 has proper uses, such as forming a Council of Ministers or a Constitutional Council when the normal route is blocked (paragraph 44).

    *Weakness.* The judgment came after the detainee had already been freed and long after the Commission had acted. It did not say what happens to the Commission's earlier decisions, beyond saying that its acts "cannot get legal recognition" (paragraph 51).

    **Significance and conclusion**

    The 2015 Constitution has no monarchy, so Article 31's royal immunity has no direct equivalent today. But the lessons remain. Emergency powers under Article 273 are limited in time. The removal-of-difficulties power in Article 305 is now held by the President on the Government's recommendation, and only until the first session of the Federal Parliament. The CIAA's role is protected by Articles 238–239. Parajuli shows that the Supreme Court can hold even the highest office to the Constitution. That is the core of constitutionalism.
