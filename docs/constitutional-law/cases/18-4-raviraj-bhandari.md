## Facts

Translated and summarised by us from the Nepali judgment. Paragraph numbers are the judgment's own.

**A minority government.** Manmohan Adhikari of the CPN (UML) was Prime Minister. His government had been formed under Article 42(2) of the 1990 Constitution. The judgment describes 42(2) as the route used when no Prime Minister could be chosen under Article 42(1), which covered a majority party or two or more parties together. So his was a minority government.

**The opposition moves to remove him.** Members of the House of Representatives (प्रतिनिधि सभा) used Article 53(3) to petition for a special session. A no-confidence motion (अविश्वासको प्रस्ताव) under Article 59(2) had been registered. The King summoned the special session for 2052/3/2. The ninth regular session was also due later that month (paragraphs 2 and 107). One petition said 102 members, including the petitioner, had told the King they supported Sher Bahadur Deuba as Prime Minister under Article 42(1) (paragraph 8).

**The recommendation and dissolution.** On 2052/2/26 the Prime Minister recommended to the King under Article 53(4) that the House be dissolved. He made the recommendation public the same evening at a press conference at his residence in Baluwatar (paragraph 14). One stated reason was that, because his government was formed under Article 42(2), no alternative government could be formed under the Constitution. On 2052/2/30 the King dissolved the House on that recommendation and fixed a date for elections.

**The petitions.** Seven writ petitions were filed under Article 88(2). The petitioners included Advocate Raviraj Bhandari, Sher Bahadur Deuba as leader of the opposition, Lokendra Bahadur Chand of the Rastriya Prajatantra Party and Gajendra Narayan Singh of the Nepal Sadbhavana Party. The respondents were the Prime Minister, the Speaker Ram Chandra Poudel, the Cabinet Secretariat of His Majesty's Government and the Election Commission. On 2052/3/7 a single judge ordered the respondents to show cause why the order sought should not be issued (paragraphs 21 to 23).

## Issues

The court framed five questions (paragraph 83):

1. Is a dissolution made on the Prime Minister's recommendation under Article 53(4) justiciable (न्याययोग्य), that is, fit for a court to decide, even though it involves political matters?
2. Once a session has been summoned under Article 53(3), is it constitutional to dissolve the House under Article 53(4)?
3. Can a government under Article 42(1) be formed as an alternative to a government under Article 42(2)?
4. Was the recommendation free from bad faith (दुराशय)?
5. Should the order the petitioners asked for be issued?

The court treated the first question as a preliminary one (paragraph 84).

## Arguments

**For the petitioners** (paragraphs 2 to 20, summarised):

- The Prime Minister recommended dissolution to escape the no-confidence motion. That made Articles 53(3) and 59(2) meaningless.
- A Prime Minister facing a no-confidence motion, without the confidence of the House, had no power to recommend dissolution.
- An alternative government under Article 42(1) was possible. Saying it was not was a self-serving reading of the Constitution.
- They relied on the earlier dissolution case, Hari Prasad Nepal v Prime Minister Girija Prasad Koirala, writ 2304 of 2051 (paragraph 8).

**For the Prime Minister** (paragraphs 25 and 26, summarised):

- Recommending and ordering dissolution are both purely political decisions. A court can look only at their constitutional or legal validity.
- Article 53(4) contains no condition or restriction.
- The contents of the recommendation were a privileged communication under Article 35(6).

## Decision

By a majority of 8 to 3, the court held the recommendation contrary to the spirit and provisions of the Constitution (paragraph 107). It then:

- declared the dissolution of 2052/2/30 unconstitutional and void from the start (प्रारम्भदेखि नै ... शून्य). The House was revived to its position before the dissolution.
- issued mandamus (परमादेश) to the Speaker and the Cabinet Secretariat to arrange the special session and the ninth session, whose dates had lapsed.
- declared that the Council of Ministers stood as it was before the dissolution.
- saved the acts done during the dissolution period, so far as they were not clearly inconsistent with Nepal law and the Constitution.

Having found the recommendation unconstitutional, the majority said it did not need to decide the bad-faith question (paragraph 107).

**The dissent.** Justice Trilok Pratap Rana found no constitutional error in the recommendation. He said the government under Article 42(2) had been formed because a 42(1) government could not be. The Prime Minister had not resigned. He distinguished Hari Prasad Nepal because Prime Minister Koirala had resigned in that case. He treated the reasons for dissolution as a political question and the recommendation as privileged under Article 35(6). He would have dismissed the petitions.

## Ratio decidendi

Our translation of the official headnote, point by point:

1. A constitution is both a political and a legal document. A question does not become political just because it concerns a power the Constitution gives the executive or legislature. If a dispute contains constitutional or legal questions, the court cannot avoid its duty to decide them by calling the dispute political. (प्रकरण नं. 86)
2. The political question doctrine is not defined by the Constitution or law. Courts created it to avoid needless conflict between constitutional organs. If acts done by the King on others' advice were kept out of review under Article 31, ideas such as a written constitution, limited government, responsible government and rule of law would lose meaning. (प्रकरण नं. 86)
3. The Prime Minister may recommend dissolution under Article 53(4) to seek a fresh mandate, but the power is not unlimited. (प्रकरण नं. 87)
4. "Constitution" includes its preamble. Article 116(1) makes the preamble's basic structure unamendable. So Article 53(4) must be read with the rest of the Constitution and its spirit, not alone. (प्रकरण नं. 92)
5. The Constitution does not let a Prime Minister who has lost a no-confidence vote stay in office and dissolve the House. He must be relieved of office, and the search for an alternative becomes mandatory. Articles 36(4), 36(5)(b), 36(7) and 59 carry this responsibility of the cabinet to the House. Removing them would turn the system into an irresponsible autocracy. (प्रकरण नं. 93)
6. Members who call a session under Article 53(3) decide its agenda. Neither the court nor any other body can decide otherwise. (प्रकरण नं. 94)
7. Going to the people is not objectionable in itself, and sometimes dissolution is the only answer to a crisis. But the power must be used responsibly and within the Constitution. (प्रकरण नं. 97)
8. The House is the first judge of the government. A Prime Minister must face the House first. Recommending dissolution because a no-confidence motion is pending, or a session has been called for it, is not responsible government. (प्रकरण नं. 98, 99)
9. Dissolving the House is a matter of national concern. Hiding its reasons behind legal or technical grounds goes against responsible government and the spirit of Article 16. (प्रकरण नं. 100)
10. Dissolving the House without considering an alternative government that could win its confidence is against parliamentary practice. The recommendation wrongly said no other cabinet could be formed and used that as a main ground, so it was flawed. (प्रकरण नं. 102, 106)

## Why it matters

- It is the leading case for the rule that **political context does not stop judicial review** of a constitutional question.
- It treats **responsible government** (cabinet answerable to the House) as the core of the parliamentary system, and limits the Prime Minister's dissolution power by it.
- It reads one Article with the whole Constitution and its preamble, an early use of **basic structure** reasoning in Nepal.
- It shows a court **restoring a dissolved House**, the same remedy the Supreme Court later gave under the 2015 Constitution (see [Unit 5](../unit-05-separation-of-powers.md#dissolution-as-a-test-of-institutional-limits)).

### Then and now

| Then (as in the judgment) | Now |
|---|---|
| Constitution of the Kingdom of Nepal 2047, Art 53(4): Prime Minister may recommend dissolution to the King | Constitution of Nepal 2015, Art 76(7): dissolution in the specific case where a Prime Minister appointed under Art 76(5) fails to win confidence. Art 85: five-year term unless dissolved earlier under the Constitution. Not a general dissolution power, so not a direct equivalent. |
| Art 42(1) and 42(2): majority or multi-party government, else largest-party minority government | Art 76(1)-(5): staged government formation |
| Art 59(2): no-confidence motion; Art 36(5)(b): Prime Minister relieved when it passes | Art 100: confidence and no-confidence motions |
| Art 88(2): Supreme Court's extraordinary (writ) jurisdiction | Art 133(2)-(3) |
| The King acts on the Prime Minister's recommendation | The President acts on the Prime Minister's recommendation |

## Full 20-mark answer

??? example "Exam answer (20 marks)"
    *Practice question:* State the facts, issues and ratio of Raviraj Bhandari v Prime Minister Manmohan Adhikari. Is dissolution of the House a political question?

    **Introduction**

    In a parliamentary system the government stays in office only while it has the confidence of the elected House. The power to dissolve the House early can be used to seek a fresh mandate from the people. It can also be used to escape that accountability. Raviraj Bhandari v Prime Minister Manmohan Adhikari is the Supreme Court's leading decision on where that line falls under the 1990 Constitution.

    **Citation**

    Advocate Raviraj Bhandari et al. v Prime Minister Manmohan Adhikari et al., writ 3105 of 2052 with six connected writs. Special Bench of eleven judges, decided 2052/05/12. Chief Justice Bishwanath Upadhyaya wrote the majority order, 8 to 3.

    **Facts**

    Manmohan Adhikari led a minority government formed under Article 42(2). Opposition members used Article 53(3) to call a special session, and a no-confidence motion under Article 59(2) was registered. Before the session met, the Prime Minister on 2052/2/26 recommended dissolution under Article 53(4). He said no alternative government could be formed because his government was under 42(2). The King dissolved the House on 2052/2/30 and fixed elections. Seven writ petitions challenged the dissolution.

    **Issues**

    The court asked whether the dissolution was justiciable, whether the House could be dissolved after a session had been summoned under Article 53(3), whether a 42(1) government could replace a 42(2) government, whether the recommendation was in bad faith, and what order should issue.

    **Decision**

    The majority held the recommendation unconstitutional and the dissolution void from the start. It restored the House and issued mandamus to the Speaker and the Cabinet Secretariat to hold the special and regular sessions. Acts done during the dissolution period were saved unless clearly inconsistent with the law and the Constitution. Justice Trilok Pratap Rana, with two judges, dissented. He held the recommendation valid and the reasons political.

    **Ratio decidendi**

    First, a constitution is a legal as well as a political document. A question does not become political merely because it concerns a power of the executive. The political question doctrine is a self-made rule of restraint and cannot be used to avoid deciding constitutional questions (paragraph 86). Second, the power to recommend dissolution exists but is not unlimited (paragraph 87). Article 53(4) must be read with the whole Constitution and its preamble (paragraph 92). Third, responsible government is the soul of the parliamentary system. The House is the first judge of the government. A Prime Minister cannot recommend dissolution to avoid a pending no-confidence motion (paragraphs 98 and 99). Fourth, before dissolving the House, the possibility of an alternative government must be considered. The Prime Minister's claim that none could be formed was a wrong reading of the Constitution, and it vitiated the recommendation (paragraphs 102 and 106).

    **Comment**

    Is dissolution a political question? The court's answer is: partly. Whether going to the people is wise is political. Whether the Constitution allows it in the circumstances is legal, and the court must decide that. This fits the [rule of law](../unit-04-rule-of-law.md) idea that every power has legal limits, and the idea of [separation of powers](../unit-05-separation-of-powers.md) with checks and balances. The dissent shows the opposite view: the text of Article 53(4) had no conditions, and the court should not second-guess the Crown and the Prime Minister. A student may argue either way. In my view the majority's reading is better, because it keeps the House's role in holding the government to account from being defeated by a well-timed dissolution.

    **Significance and conclusion**

    The case settled that dissolution is open to judicial review in Nepal, and that a minority Prime Minister facing a no-confidence motion cannot use dissolution to escape it. It was decided under the 1990 Constitution. The 2015 Constitution now regulates dissolution more narrowly in Articles 76(7) and 85, and the Supreme Court again restored a dissolved House in the 2077-78 cases. The core lesson of Raviraj Bhandari, that political power is subject to the Constitution, still applies.
