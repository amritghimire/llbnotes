# Unit 6: Federal system

!!! abstract "In one line"
    Federalism gives different levels of government constitutional authority, while shared rules help them govern one country together.

## Why it matters for the exam

Learn the difference between constitutional division and ordinary decentralization. For Nepal, connect every explanation to Articles 56–60, Schedules 5–9 and the cooperation and dispute-resolution provisions.

## 6.1 Meaning and types

Federalism combines common government with constitutionally recognized territorial self-government. Dicey's Chapter III explains federalism through division of powers under a superior constitution and institutions that adjudicate limits. His historical framework helps identify the principle, but Nepal's own text determines its allocation.

Types can be discussed through different analytical tests. **Coming together** describes a federation formed by previously separate political units. **Holding together** describes constitutional division within an existing state. **Symmetrical** arrangements give constituent units broadly similar status; **asymmetrical** arrangements differentiate particular units. **Dual** analysis stresses separate fields, while **cooperative** analysis stresses shared tasks and coordination. These are explanatory models, not a closed constitutional list.

Nepal's transition from unitary constitutional arrangements to the 2015 federal structure fits a holding-together explanation. That label is an analytical description, not an Article's formal name for the system.

## 6.2 Essential features

A federal system usually includes a superior constitution, constitutionally allocated powers, territorial institutions, a protected amendment framework and an independent method of resolving competence disputes. Adequate financial arrangements matter because a function without resources may be ineffective.

No feature requires every level to have identical powers. Nor does federalism mean that a Province is a separate sovereign country. Article 4 describes Nepal as independent and indivisible, while Articles 56–58 allocate internal governing authority.

## 6.3 State structure and division of powers

Article 56 establishes the Federation, Provinces and Local Level. Schedule 4 identifies seven Provinces in the checked text. Article 56(4) includes rural municipalities, municipalities and district assemblies within the Local Level. A district assembly is not a fourth federal level. Article 57 links governing powers to five lists.

| Schedule | Constitutional allocation | How to remember |
|---|---|---|
| 5 | Federation | Federal subjects |
| 6 | Province | Provincial subjects |
| 7 | Federation and Province | Two-level concurrent subjects |
| 8 | Local Level | Local subjects |
| 9 | Federation, Province and Local Level | Three-level concurrent subjects |

Article 58 gives residuary powers to the Federation. Article 57(6) addresses inconsistency with federal law for laws under clauses (3) and (5). Article 57(7) addresses local-law inconsistency with provincial law under clause (5). **Do not convert these concurrent-field rules into a claim that any federal Act can erase every provincial or local power.** First identify competence and the applicable list.

The lists can include related subjects at different scales. A sector name alone is not enough to determine authority. Read the entry, constitutional context and applicable laws.

## 6.4 Federal, provincial and local interrelations

Article 232(1) requires cooperation, co-existence and coordination. Its other clauses contain specific directions and exceptional intervention arrangements. Those arrangements are conditioned powers, not a general permission for routine political takeover.

Article 234 establishes the Inter-Province Council for specified political disputes. Article 235 addresses coordination legislation and provincial coordination with local institutions. Article 137(2)(a) gives the Constitutional Bench jurisdiction over the listed intergovernmental competence disputes. Political negotiation and judicial adjudication therefore answer different kinds of problems.

Articles 59–60 regulate financial powers and revenue distribution. Article 60 includes equitable distribution, transfers, equalization and transparent allocation. Fiscal equalization addresses differences in expenditure needs and revenue capacity; it is not a promise that every unit receives the same amount.

## 6.5 Merits and demerits

| Possible benefit | Corresponding risk | What helps |
|---|---|---|
| Decisions closer to communities | Unequal capacity or local capture | Transparent responsibility and oversight |
| Recognition of territorial diversity | Disputes over boundaries and competence | Constitutional allocation and adjudication |
| More opportunities to participate | More institutional expense | Clear functions and accountable budgets |
| Local policy adaptation | Unequal service quality | Standards, cooperation and fiscal arrangements |
| Distribution of power | Confusion in shared sectors | Coordination and reasons for decisions |

These are institutional possibilities, not measured claims about every Nepal government.

## Nepal's law

Read Articles 56–60 with Schedules 4–9, Articles 137 and 231–237, and Articles 250–251 on the National Natural Resources and Fiscal Commission. A concrete dispute may also require legislation. This unit does not invent statutory section numbers for unconsulted Acts.

## Compare

| Federalism | Ordinary decentralization |
|---|---|
| Authority is distributed by the constitutional framework | Functions may be transferred through ordinary central arrangements |
| Competence disputes can concern constitutional limits | Disputes may primarily concern delegated powers |
| Constitutional amendment may be required to change protected allocations | Ordinary law may often reorganize a delegation |
| Shared government and territorial authority coexist | Local administration alone does not prove federalism |

## Full 20-mark answers

??? example "Q1. Explain federalism and the division of powers under Nepal's Constitution. (20 marks)"
    **Introduction**

    Federalism distributes governing authority constitutionally among common and territorial institutions. It allows different levels to govern within one constitutional order. The concept must be distinguished from ordinary decentralization, through which a central authority transfers administrative functions. Nepal's Constitution of 2015 establishes a federal structure with the Federation, Provinces and Local Level. Understanding that structure requires more than remembering the names of the levels. An answer must explain the source of their powers, the allocation of subjects, the treatment of shared fields and the arrangements through which disagreements can be resolved without undermining the constitutional distribution.

    **Meaning and theoretical features**

    Dicey's discussion of federalism emphasizes constitutionally divided authority under a superior framework. Its value is the recognition that neither ordinary central convenience nor territorial preference alone determines legal competence. Federal arrangements generally require a constitutional allocation, functioning institutions and a method of deciding disputes about limits. Financial arrangements are also necessary because legal responsibility may be ineffective without resources. These features do not demand identical power for every level. Nor do they turn each constituent unit into an independent country. The federal arrangement governs the internal distribution of authority within the state established by the Constitution.

    **Nepal's three-level structure**

    Article 56 provides the basic structure of the Federal Democratic Republic of Nepal through three levels. The Federation, Provinces and Local Level exercise state power in accordance with the Constitution and law. This is significant because the local constitutional position should not be described merely as an office of the provincial administration. Each level's authority must be identified from the framework. At the same time, Article 4 describes Nepal as independent and indivisible. Territorial allocation therefore operates within a common state rather than granting every level an unrestricted choice to disregard national constitutional institutions.

    **The constitutional lists**

    Article 57 connects the distribution to Schedules 5–9. Schedule 5 concerns federal powers and Schedule 6 provincial powers. Schedule 7 contains concurrent powers of the Federation and Provinces. Schedule 8 identifies local powers, while Schedule 9 contains subjects shared by all three levels. These five lists must be distinguished. A common examination mistake is to remember a sector name but overlook the scale or wording of its particular entry. Related subjects can appear in different lists for different purposes. The precise entry and its constitutional context matter more than a broad assumption that an entire sector belongs to one institution.

    **Concurrent powers and inconsistency**

    Shared subjects require rules concerning the relationship between laws. Article 57(6) addresses laws made under clauses (3) and (5) and their consistency with federal law. Article 57(7) addresses local laws under clause (5) and consistency with provincial law. Invalidity operates to the extent of inconsistency within the relevant constitutional setting. These provisions should not be transformed into a claim that any federal legislation automatically overrides every provincial or local function. The first inquiry remains competence. A valid explanation identifies the shared field, applicable law and actual inconsistency before drawing a conclusion about which provision cannot operate.

    **Residuary and financial authority**

    Article 58 gives the Federation power over matters not otherwise allocated as specified there. Residuary authority differs from concurrent authority. A shared subject is already allocated, while a residuary question concerns the absence of the relevant allocation. Articles 59 and 60 then address financial powers and revenue distribution. These provisions recognize that governmental responsibility requires budgets, revenue and transfers. Article 60 connects equalization with expenditure needs and revenue capacity. Financial distribution therefore involves both constitutional competence and the practical ability to provide services. It should not be described as a demand for equal transfers regardless of differing conditions.

    **Relations between the levels**

    Federalism does not require every institution to operate alone. Article 232(1) bases relations on cooperation, co-existence and coordination. Cooperation concerns working together, co-existence recognizes each level's constitutional place and coordination helps align connected activities. Other clauses establish particular powers and exceptional measures subject to constitutional conditions. They cannot be used as an unrestricted justification for central intervention whenever a political disagreement occurs. Article 235 also addresses coordination legislation and arrangements involving local institutions. These provisions show that division and collaboration are complementary parts of the constitutional design rather than mutually exclusive choices.

    **Dispute resolution**

    Different institutions address different kinds of disagreement. Article 234 establishes the Inter-Province Council for specified political disputes involving the Federation and Provinces or Provinces with one another. Article 137(2)(a) provides Constitutional Bench jurisdiction over the listed intergovernmental competence disputes. Negotiation can help settle policy differences, while adjudication can determine a legal boundary. Neither should be assumed to replace every function of the other. A government cannot create constitutional authority merely through political agreement where the text requires a different procedure. Equally, a court's determination of competence does not supply every practical detail needed to coordinate a shared public service.

    **Benefits and difficulties**

    Federalism can bring public decisions closer to communities, recognize diversity and create additional opportunities for participation. It can also distribute authority so that every significant decision does not depend on a single centre. Its possible difficulties include administrative expense, uneven institutional capacity and disputes over shared subjects. These are reasons to evaluate design and implementation carefully, not automatic proof for or against the system. For a person seeking a service, the practical concern is whether responsibility is clear and resources are available. A constitutional allocation fails its purpose if every level can disclaim responsibility when a service is delayed.

    A practical method is to begin with the action proposed rather than with a broad institutional preference. Determine the precise subject, locate the relevant Schedule entry and ask which level is authorized to make the particular decision. Then examine any shared-field relationship and applicable legal conditions. This sequence avoids assuming that the same institution controls every aspect of a subject simply because it performs one related task. It also helps identify whether the problem concerns competence, resources or coordination, which may require different constitutional responses.

    **Conclusion**

    Nepal's federal system rests on constitutionally recognized levels, allocated lists, federal residuary powers, financial arrangements and mechanisms for cooperation and dispute resolution. Articles 56–60, 137 and 232–235 supply the central framework for this answer. The strongest explanation avoids two opposite errors. It does not reduce local and provincial authority to ordinary central permission, and it does not describe the levels as independent states free from a common Constitution. Federalism combines shared government with constitutionally protected territorial responsibility. Its success depends on institutions exercising their own powers while respecting the lawful place of the others.

??? example "Q2. Discuss the types, advantages and difficulties of federalism, with Nepal's intergovernmental arrangements. (20 marks)"
    **Introduction**

    Federalism seeks to combine common government with territorial self-government. Its different types describe how a federal arrangement develops and how its powers are organized. Its advantages and difficulties depend on those choices and on their implementation. Nepal's Constitution makes the discussion concrete through three levels, allocated powers, financial distribution and mechanisms for cooperation. An exam answer should therefore connect general theory with actual constitutional arrangements. Calling federalism good because it is local, or bad because it creates more institutions, does not explain how its benefits can be secured or its risks controlled.

    **Types based on formation**

    One analytical distinction is between coming-together and holding-together arrangements. The first describes previously separate political units joining within a common constitutional system. The second describes distribution of authority within an existing state. These are explanatory models rather than legal categories imposed by a single constitutional clause. Nepal's movement from earlier unitary arrangements to the 2015 federal structure can be understood through the holding-together model. The label should not substitute for constitutional evidence. What matters legally is that Articles 56–58 and the Schedules establish the levels and their powers, whatever historical explanation is used for the transition.

    **Types based on status and operation**

    Symmetrical and asymmetrical arrangements concern whether constituent units have broadly similar constitutional status or differentiated powers. Dual and cooperative approaches emphasize different operational features. A dual account stresses separate fields, while a cooperative account recognizes that many public tasks require common action. These distinctions can overlap, and no short label describes every institutional detail. Nepal's concurrent lists and Article 232(1) make cooperation especially important to its explanation. The Constitution does not merely divide subjects and require silence between governments. It also supplies principles and institutions through which the levels can respond to connected problems.

    **Participation and recognition of diversity**

    One possible advantage is that decisions can be made nearer to people affected by them. Local institutions can provide more accessible opportunities to raise concerns and assess performance. Territorial government can also recognize different needs and circumstances within a common state. These are potential benefits rather than guaranteed outcomes. Proximity alone does not ensure that every person can participate, particularly where powerful local interests dominate. Participation therefore requires transparent procedures, inclusion and accountability. Nepal's constitutional recognition of the Local Level matters because it gives local authority a place in the framework, but that position still needs effective institutional practice.

    **Distribution of power and policy adaptation**

    Federalism can reduce dependence on a single centre by giving different levels constitutionally allocated responsibilities. It can allow policy responses suited to territorial conditions, while common institutions address national matters. Yet division is useful only if the allocation can be identified and observed. An institution that acts outside its competence can create uncertainty rather than innovation. In Nepal, Article 57 and Schedules 5–9 require attention to both exclusive and shared fields. Constitutional supremacy remains common to every level. A local or provincial policy does not become valid merely because it is locally popular, just as a federal policy is not valid merely because it is nationally promoted.

    **Expense, capacity and unequal conditions**

    More institutions can mean greater administrative expense and a need for additional expertise. Different units may also possess unequal revenue capacity or face unequal service demands. These difficulties do not by themselves establish that constitutional division is mistaken. They explain why financial design and clear responsibility are essential. Article 60 addresses revenue distribution, transfers and equalization. Equalization responds to differences in expenditure need and revenue capacity, rather than requiring identical amounts for every unit. An answer should connect resources to assigned functions. Otherwise, a legal promise of autonomy may coexist with practical dependence that weakens the ability to act responsibly.

    **Overlap and conflicting claims**

    Shared powers can create disputes about who should legislate, implement or pay for a programme. Article 57's concurrent lists make such questions part of the system rather than proof that all overlap is unlawful. Clauses (6) and (7) provide specified inconsistency rules, but the competence question must come first. The general statement that federal law always wins over every local decision is too broad. Similarly, a local institution cannot dismiss a valid concurrent-field requirement simply by invoking autonomy. The appropriate method is to identify the constitutional subject, applicable legal provisions and the particular conflict that prevents both rules from operating together.

    **Cooperation and political settlement**

    Article 232(1) requires cooperation, co-existence and coordination. These principles give a practical direction to intergovernmental relations. Article 234's Inter-Province Council addresses specified political disputes, while Article 235 concerns coordination arrangements and legislation. Political settlement can help institutions share information, align programmes and avoid repeated conflict. It must still respect constitutional authority. Agreement cannot amend a protected allocation through an informal bargain. Nor should the possibility of central directions be treated as a general power to replace provincial or local choice. The constitutional conditions of each mechanism determine when it can lawfully be used.

    **Judicial protection of the allocation**

    Article 137(2)(a) provides Constitutional Bench jurisdiction over listed intergovernmental competence disputes. Independent adjudication is important because disagreement about a legal boundary cannot always be resolved by bargaining between unequal institutions. Judicial determination supports the superior constitutional framework. It does not replace the need for administrative cooperation after the boundary is clarified. The difference between legal competence and practical coordination should therefore remain clear. A court may identify which institution possesses a power, while the responsible governments must still arrange lawful implementation. Federalism depends on both functions operating rather than on expecting one institution to solve every dimension of a public problem.

    For example, institutions dealing with a shared service may agree on a practical timetable while remaining bound by their separate legal responsibilities. Cooperation can reduce duplication, but the arrangement still needs clarity about the authority to decide, the source of funding and the duty to report. Otherwise a cooperative label may hide responsibility rather than improve it. The constitutional principles are therefore useful when translated into questions that an affected community can ask about a real programme and its accountable decision makers.

    **Critical assessment and conclusion**

    Federalism offers opportunities for participation, recognition of diversity and distribution of authority, while creating risks of expense, uneven capacity and overlapping responsibility. Nepal's answer combines constitutional lists with financial rules, coordination principles, political institutions and judicial protection. These arrangements should be assessed by whether they make lawful responsibility clear to the people who need government services. Federalism is weakened when autonomy becomes an excuse for unaccountability or cooperation becomes an excuse for domination. Its central aim is a common constitutional state in which different levels can govern effectively within their lawful fields and work together where their responsibilities meet.
