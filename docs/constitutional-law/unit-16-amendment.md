# Unit 16: Amendment of the Constitution

!!! abstract "In one line"
    Article 274 allows constitutional change through a special process while protecting stated fundamentals and providing a provincial role for specified proposals.

## Why it matters for the exam

Learn the sequence and separate the voting tests. Every amendment does not require unanimous provincial consent. Ordinary legislative power and constitutional amendment power are different.

## Key terms

| Term | Nepali | Plain meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Constitutional amendment | संविधान संशोधन | Alteration of the Constitution through its authorized procedure |
| Entrenched limit | संरक्षित सीमा | A constitutional restriction protected against ordinary change |
| Special majority | विशेष बहुमत | A voting requirement different from the ordinary majority rule |
| Provincial consent | प्रदेश सभाको सहमति | The provincial decision required for specified amendment proposals |
| Authentication | प्रमाणीकरण | The constitutionally required formal approval completing the process |
| Repeal | खारेजी | Removal of a legal provision through an authorized legal process |

## Power and limits

Article 274(1) prohibits amendment prejudicial to Nepal's sovereignty, territorial integrity, independence or sovereignty vested in the people. The proviso to clause (2) prevents amendment of clause (1). These are express textual protections.

Clause (2) allows a Bill to amend or repeal an Article to be introduced in either House, subject to clause (1) and other Articles. The reference to repeal does not authorize ignoring the protected limit. A constitutional amendment is also different from an ordinary Act merely because both involve Parliament.

## Procedure under Article 274

| Stage | Requirement | Timing or threshold |
|---|---|---|
| Introduction, (2) | Bill may begin in either House | Subject to constitutional limits |
| Public information, (3) | Public publication of the introduced Bill | Within 30 days of introduction |
| Provincial referral, (4) | Required for alteration of provincial borders or Schedule 6 matters | Speaker or Chairperson sends within 30 days of introduction |
| Provincial decision, (5) | Concerned Assembly approves or rejects | Majority of its then total membership; information within three months |
| Assembly absent, (5) proviso | Decision period follows formation | Within three months after the first meeting of the newly formed Assembly |
| No timely information, (6) | Originating House is not prevented from proceeding | Apply the clause, not an invented automatic veto |
| Provincial rejection, (7) | Bill becomes inoperative where the specified majority rejects | Notice within the stated period |
| Federal adoption, (8) | The Bill follows the applicable consent route | At least two thirds of then total membership in both Houses |
| Submission, (9) | Adopted Bill goes to President | For authentication |
| Completion, (10) | President authenticates; Constitution is amended from authentication | Within 15 days of receipt |

Clauses (5)–(8) must be read together. Clause (8) speaks of a Bill not requiring provincial consent or one accepted by a majority of Provincial Assemblies, while clause (6) addresses absence of timely information. Do not replace that wording with either unanimous provincial consent for every amendment or no provincial role at all.

The Federal Parliament threshold is not merely two thirds of those present and voting. Nor should the two Houses be casually treated as one combined membership in an unspecified joint vote. Use the constitutional requirement for both Houses.

## Why the procedure matters

Public publication permits examination of the proposed constitutional change. Provincial participation protects the territorial allocation where the specified subjects are affected. Special federal majorities require wider support than ordinary legislation. Presidential authentication completes the process at the time stated by the text.

**Illustration.** A Bill expressly altering a Province's constitutional border engages clause (4). A Bill on a different subject does not engage that clause merely because a Province has a political view about it. The actual subject and other constitutional requirements still need analysis.

## Amendment, ordinary law and interpretation

Ordinary law works within the existing Constitution. Amendment changes the text through Article 274. Judicial interpretation explains the Constitution within judicial authority; it is not a general permission to bypass the amendment procedure.

A desire for reform does not prove that a proposed method is lawful. Equally, rigidity does not mean the text can never change. The procedure attempts to combine stability with authorized adaptation.

## Federalism and constitutional supremacy

The Constitution distributes power among levels. Ordinary central authority should not be confused with a general power to rewrite that allocation. Article 274 gives specified provincial interests an express role while preserving a common constitutional process.

[Unit 6](unit-06-federal-system.md) explains the distribution being protected. Articles 1 and 128–133 remain relevant to constitutional supremacy and legal interpretation, but the exact scope of a disputed amendment challenge requires verified authority; this page does not invent a case outcome.

## Nepal's law

Article 274 is the principal source. Read its substantive prohibition, introduction, publication, provincial stages, federal adoption and authentication in sequence. Do not substitute ordinary voting under Article 99 for its special requirement.

## Full 20-mark answers

??? example "Q1. Explain the amendment procedure and limits under Article 274 of Nepal's Constitution. (20 marks)"
    **Introduction**

    Constitutional amendment permits the fundamental framework to respond to change through a legally authorized process. It differs from ordinary legislation because it alters the superior instrument under which ordinary public powers operate. Nepal's Article 274 combines a power to amend with substantive limits, publication, specified provincial participation, special federal adoption and presidential authentication. A complete answer must follow the sequence and distinguish the thresholds. Saying merely that two thirds can amend is incomplete. It overlooks protected matters, the subject of the Bill, the provincial stages and the legal event from which the Constitution becomes amended.

    **Express substantive limits**

    Article 274(1) prohibits amendment prejudicial to Nepal's sovereignty, territorial integrity, independence or sovereignty vested in the people. The proviso to clause (2) protects clause (1) from amendment. These are direct textual restrictions rather than optional political advice. They show that the amendment power is not described as an unrestricted permission to destroy every foundation of the constitutional order. A large majority therefore does not by itself answer every legal objection. The proposal must remain within the constitutional authority to amend as well as satisfy the relevant procedural requirements. Purpose and method both need examination under the actual text.

    **Introduction in either House**

    Clause (2) allows a Bill to amend or repeal an Article to be introduced in either House of the Federal Parliament, subject to clause (1) and other Articles. The word repeal does not remove the limitation attached to the power. Nor does the ability to introduce a Bill establish that it has already become a valid amendment. Introduction begins the constitutional process. This distinction matters because an ordinary Act or executive announcement cannot acquire amendment status merely by expressing a desire to change the framework. The institution must use the legal form and stages through which the Constitution authorizes its own alteration.

    **Public publication**

    Clause (3) requires public publication for information within thirty days of introduction. Constitutional change affects the framework governing institutions and people, so the proposal must not be treated as a wholly private parliamentary arrangement. Publication permits attention to the actual wording and consequences before completion. It does not prove that every person has agreed or that the Bill has satisfied all later conditions. The requirement should therefore be explained as one step in a larger process. A publicly popular proposal may still fail a protected limit, just as a properly introduced proposal may still require provincial referral before federal adoption.

    **When provincial referral is required**

    Clause (4) identifies two relevant subject areas: alteration of a Province's borders and matters in Schedule 6. The Speaker or Chairperson of the concerned House sends the Bill for provincial consent within thirty days of introduction. This is a specified federal protection, not a demand for unanimous provincial approval of every amendment on any subject. The actual contents of the Bill determine whether the clause applies. A constitutional border change clearly raises the referral question, while a different proposal cannot be assigned the same route solely because provincial politicians have expressed an interest in it. Other constitutional conditions must still be respected.

    **Provincial decision and timing**

    Clause (5) provides approval or rejection by a majority of the concerned Assembly's then total membership, with information to the Federal Parliament within three months. If an Assembly does not exist, the proviso sets the period by reference to its first meeting after formation. These requirements protect both participation and an identifiable process. They should not be replaced by a vague claim that the Province may respond whenever convenient. The denominator also matters. A majority of the then total membership is different from an ordinary majority of whoever happens to be present. Accurate explanation of this distinction is necessary before assessing whether a decision satisfies the constitutional stage.

    **Silence and rejection**

    Clause (6) states that absence of timely information does not prevent the originating federal House from proceeding. Clause (7) makes the Bill inoperative where the specified majority of Provincial Assemblies gives notice of rejection within the period. Clause (8) then addresses a Bill not requiring consent or accepted by a majority of Provincial Assemblies. These provisions must be read together rather than reduced to a slogan. Silence is not automatically the same as a veto, and referral is not irrelevant simply because Parliament remains involved. A difficult question about their interaction needs interpretation grounded in the actual text, not an invented universal consent formula.

    **Federal adoption and authentication**

    Clause (8) requires at least a two-thirds majority of the then total membership in both Houses for adoption through the applicable route. This is not merely two thirds of those present and voting, and should not be casually described as one combined joint membership. Under clause (9), the adopted Bill is submitted to the President for authentication. Clause (10) requires authentication within fifteen days of receipt and makes the Constitution amended from that event. The legal sequence therefore continues after the vote. Identifying the completion point prevents a proposal, a passed Bill and an authenticated amendment from being treated as the same legal state.

    **Relationship with other constitutional powers**

    Ordinary lawmaking, executive administration and judicial interpretation have different roles. They operate within the Constitution rather than providing unrestricted substitutes for Article 274. A policy preference cannot justify changing the framework through a circular, and a parliamentary majority using ordinary legislation must still observe constitutional limits. Judicial interpretation explains meaning within the court's authority, but should not be presented as general permission to rewrite the text without the amendment process. Likewise, a foreign amendment doctrine is comparative material until its relevance to Nepal is legally established. The express Nepal provisions should remain the foundation of the exam answer.

    **Critical view and conclusion**

    Article 274 attempts to combine stability with the possibility of reform. Protected interests limit what may be changed, while publication, provincial participation and special majorities structure how change occurs. The procedure can require broad agreement, but its difficulty does not prove that every proposal should be abandoned or that constitutional forms may be bypassed. The correct response is lawful use of the available process. A strong answer therefore follows the Bill from introduction to authentication, identifies the subject-dependent provincial route and states each threshold accurately. Constitutional amendment is authorized adaptation within a superior legal framework, not unrestricted ordinary government power.

??? example "Q2. How does Nepal's amendment framework balance constitutional stability, reform and federal participation? (20 marks)"
    **Introduction**

    A Constitution must provide continuity without making every necessary reform impossible. An amendment framework addresses that tension by allowing change through conditions different from ordinary legislation. Nepal's Article 274 also recognizes federal concerns where a proposal affects provincial borders or Schedule 6 matters. The result is a combination of substantive protection, public process, provincial participation and demanding parliamentary adoption. Its value should be assessed through those connections. Rigidity does not mean that the Constitution can never change, and reform does not mean that an institution may disregard the procedure whenever ordinary politics makes compliance difficult.

    **Why stability matters**

    Constitutional rules organize public power and protect people beyond a single government's period in office. If ordinary majorities could alter every limit without additional conditions, institutions and rights could depend entirely on temporary political convenience. Stability therefore supports predictable authority and continued public participation. Nepal's Article 1 identifies the Constitution as fundamental law, while Article 274 provides a distinct route for changing it. This does not remove democratic choice. It channels constitutional choice through a process intended to demand more than the support needed for an ordinary decision. The distinction explains why amendment power and ordinary legislative power should not be merged.

    **Why change remains necessary**

    Social circumstances, institutional experience and public demands may reveal a need for constitutional reform. A text with no lawful adaptation process could create pressure for informal evasion or disruption. Article 274(2) therefore permits amendment or repeal of Articles through the constitutional route. That permission is meaningful but conditional. A proposal should be evaluated by its substance and method rather than supported simply because it is described as reform. The existence of a legal path also weakens the argument that executive convenience is enough to change the framework informally. Institutions should use the authority provided instead of assuming a superior power outside the Constitution.

    **Protected fundamentals**

    Clause (1) protects sovereignty, territorial integrity, independence and sovereignty vested in the people against prejudicial amendment. The proviso to clause (2) protects clause (1) itself. These provisions establish that lawful change has express substantive boundaries. Their presence does not justify inventing an unlimited list of unamendable features from memory. Equally, the amendment power should not be treated as free from every limit once a special majority exists. The actual proposal and protected interests must be analyzed. A claim based on an implied foreign doctrine requires separate verified legal authority before it is presented as Nepal's settled constitutional rule.

    **Public scrutiny and responsibility**

    Clause (3)'s publication requirement makes the introduced Bill a matter for public information within thirty days. Publicity can expose the actual proposed wording, allow criticism and help representatives explain their choices. It is one part of constitutional responsibility rather than a substitute for the required votes. A public campaign does not itself enact an amendment, and the absence of universal approval is not the same as failure to meet a constitutionally required threshold. The legal inquiry remains specific. Publication, participation and adoption have different functions, and each must be assessed through the stage and condition actually provided by Article 274.

    **The federal dimension**

    Federalism constitutionally distributes authority rather than leaving every territorial power to ordinary central permission. Article 274(4) gives a provincial role where borders or Schedule 6 matters are affected. This helps explain why an ordinary federal majority should not be assumed to possess complete control over every protected allocation. The referral rule is nevertheless subject-specific. It does not require provincial unanimity for all constitutional amendments, nor does provincial political interest alone establish that the specified trigger is satisfied. A sound federal analysis therefore begins with the Bill's contents and the constitutional field it changes, then follows the applicable participation process.

    **Time and decision rules**

    Provincial participation also has an organized timetable. The concerned Assembly decides by a majority of its then total membership and communicates within three months, with the stated proviso where an Assembly does not exist. Clauses (6)–(8) address absence of information, rejection and the federal adoption route. These rules seek a process in which participation matters but unanswered proposals do not necessarily remain permanently suspended. Their interaction should be stated faithfully rather than simplified into either an unlimited provincial veto or complete provincial irrelevance. Constitutional design includes both the opportunity to decide and the legal consequence of the response or non-response described by the text.

    **Special federal adoption**

    The federal requirement under clause (8) is at least two thirds of the then total membership in both Houses. Its denominator makes it different from an ordinary present-and-voting decision. Requiring action in both Houses also connects change with the bicameral structure rather than treating the Houses as interchangeable. The threshold can encourage negotiation across a wider group of representatives. It can also make reform more demanding where political disagreement is strong. Those are possible institutional effects, not proof that any particular proposal has succeeded or failed. The legal question is whether the actual constitutional requirement was met in the particular process.

    **Completion and institutional boundaries**

    Presidential authentication under clauses (9)–(10) completes the process and determines when the Constitution becomes amended. A passed proposal should not be described as already effective before the required completion event. Nor can other institutions assume that their ordinary powers replace the amendment framework. Administrative rules work within existing law, and courts interpret through their assigned judicial authority. Neither role is a general alternative route for rewriting constitutional provisions. Maintaining these distinctions protects the stability that the special procedure is designed to provide while leaving the lawful reform route open to institutions able to meet its conditions.

    **Comparison and conclusion**

    The US Article V and Indian Article 368 frameworks demonstrate that special amendment procedures can combine national and territorial participation in different ways. They do not supply Nepal's periods, voting denominators or provincial triggers. Nepal's balance must be explained through Article 274 itself. It protects stated fundamentals while allowing an introduced, published and properly considered proposal to proceed through the appropriate provincial route, federal adoption and authentication. The arrangement treats constitutional reform as a distinct public legal act. Its success depends on respecting both the possibility of change and the conditions that prevent temporary political convenience from becoming unlimited power over the fundamental framework.

## Test yourself

??? question "Can an amendment Bill start only in the House of Representatives?"
    No. Article 274(2) permits introduction in either House.

??? question "Which proposals trigger the provincial referral in clause (4)?"
    Alteration of provincial borders or matters in Schedule 6.

??? question "Does every Province have an automatic veto through silence?"
    No. Clause (6) addresses failure to provide information within the stated period.

??? question "Is the federal threshold two thirds of those present and voting?"
    No. Clause (8) uses then total membership in both Houses.

??? question "When does the Constitution become amended under clause (10)?"
    From authentication, which the President must complete within fifteen days of receipt.

??? question "Can clause (1) be amended under clause (2)?"
    Its proviso expressly prevents that.

## One-minute revision

- Amendment is different from ordinary legislation.
- Clause (1) protects stated fundamentals and is itself protected.
- A Bill may start in either House and must be published.
- Provincial participation is triggered by borders and Schedule 6 matters.
- Read approval, silence and rejection together.
- Federal adoption uses the special total-membership threshold.
- Authentication completes the process.
