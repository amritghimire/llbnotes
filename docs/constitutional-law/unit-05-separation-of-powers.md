# Unit 5: Separation of powers and checks and balances

!!! abstract "In one line"
    Different institutions make law, execute law and decide disputes, while constitutional checks prevent any institution from controlling everything.

## Why it matters for the exam

## 5.1 Doctrine

Montesquieu, *The Spirit of Laws*, Book XI, Chapter 6, explains the danger when the same authority makes rules, executes them and decides disputes about them. The aim is protection against concentrated and arbitrary power. This is a paraphrase of the historical argument.

Three forms of separation help organize an answer. **Functional separation** distinguishes the tasks. **Institutional separation** gives them to different bodies. **Personnel separation** limits the same person's membership in different institutions. No simple statement that “all three must never meet” adequately describes a modern constitutional system.

**Example.** Parliament can enact a licensing law, a ministry can administer it and a court can decide whether a refusal was lawful. That is a teaching example, not a claim about a particular Act.

## 5.2 Checks and balances

Separation identifies roles; checks restrain their misuse. Legislative financial control, confidence procedures, judicial review and constitutionally regulated appointments are examples. A check is not a general power to obstruct another institution whenever politically convenient. Identify its constitutional basis and conditions.

| Nepal check | Provision | Limit to remember |
|---|---|---|
| Parliamentary confidence | Article 100 | Follow its triggers, time limits and restrictions |
| Accountability of ministers | Article 76(10) | Distinguish collective and individual responsibility |
| Impeachment | Article 101 | Grounds, initiators and voting requirements differ by office |
| Judicial review | Article 133 | Courts act within constitutional jurisdiction |
| Judicial independence | Articles 126 and 128 | Independence protects adjudication, not unlimited judicial authority |
| Parliamentary procedure and privilege | Articles 103–104 | Read the Constitution's limits and specific protected matters |

## 5.3 Presidential and parliamentary systems

The US Articles I, II and III establish separate legislative, executive and judicial authority. Institutional separation is accompanied by checks. The President cannot treat Congress or the courts as subordinate departments.

Nepal's Article 74 provides parliamentary government. Ministers and the legislature are connected through appointment and responsibility, particularly Articles 76 and 100. That overlap supports responsible government but can weaken scrutiny if party loyalty replaces independent examination. Nepal retains constitutional judicial functions and limits despite executive-legislative overlap.

## 5.4 Position under Nepal's Constitution

Articles 75, 83 and 126 identify central executive, federal legislative and judicial roles. Articles 128 and 133 support authoritative interpretation and review. Articles 76(7) and 85 govern the constitutional question of early dissolution. Do not infer a general dissolution power merely from the word parliamentary.

## Nepal's law

Article 76(7) addresses dissolution where the Prime Minister appointed under clause (5) fails to obtain confidence, or a Prime Minister cannot be appointed, with its stated election requirement. Read the preceding appointment stages. Article 85 sets the ordinary five-year House term unless earlier dissolved under the Constitution, together with emergency-extension provisions. It does not independently grant an unlimited discretion to dissolve.

## Full 20-mark answers

??? example "Q1. Explain separation of powers and checks and balances with reference to Nepal. (20 marks)"
    **Introduction**

    Separation of powers distributes governmental functions so that one authority does not control the making, execution and adjudication of law without restraint. Checks and balances make that distribution effective by giving institutions specific powers to restrain misuse. The two ideas are connected but different. Separation asks who should perform a function, while a check identifies how a particular excess can be questioned. Nepal's parliamentary Constitution combines executive-legislative connections with separate judicial authority and constitutional controls. Its position cannot be explained either as complete institutional isolation or as a total absence of separation.

    **The classical argument**

    Montesquieu's discussion in Book XI, Chapter 6 of The Spirit of Laws explains the threat to liberty when the same authority combines the principal powers. A body able to make oppressive rules, enforce them and decide complaints about its own conduct has little reason to respect limits. The historical argument supplies a reason for division, rather than a ready-made description of every modern institution. Its continuing value is the concern about concentration and arbitrariness. An exam answer should explain that purpose before listing the three organs, because the purpose shows why constitutional arrangements require both responsibility and restraint.

    **Functions, institutions and personnel**

    Three distinctions make the doctrine clearer. Functional separation identifies lawmaking, administration and adjudication as different tasks. Institutional separation gives those tasks to different constitutional bodies. Personnel separation concerns whether the same individuals hold positions in more than one body. These forms do not always coincide. A parliamentary minister may also be a legislator, yet the judicial function remains constitutionally distinct. Treating every overlap as a breach would misunderstand parliamentary government. The correct inquiry is whether the constitutional arrangement preserves the assigned responsibilities and prevents an overlap from becoming a claim of unlimited power.

    **Nepal's allocation of authority**

    The Constitution supplies the legal foundation for each function. Article 75 concerns executive power, Article 83 establishes the Federal Parliament and Article 126 concerns the exercise of powers relating to justice. Article 128 gives the Supreme Court final interpretive authority, while Article 133 provides important jurisdiction for review and remedies. The allocation operates within constitutional supremacy under Article 1. Thus none of these institutions may claim that its ordinary decisions stand above the Constitution. Their powers are authoritative within the framework because that framework creates and limits their legal position.

    **Parliamentary overlap and responsibility**

    Article 74 expressly adopts parliamentary government. Article 76 regulates formation of the Council of Ministers, and Article 76(10) states collective and individual responsibility. Article 100 provides confidence and no-confidence arrangements. These connections allow the representative institution to hold the political executive responsible. They also create a practical risk where a disciplined parliamentary majority accepts every executive proposal without scrutiny. The constitutional response is not to deny the parliamentary structure, but to preserve the operation of its checks and legal limits. Executive-legislative connection should make responsibility possible, rather than provide a reason to escape it.

    **Checks through parliamentary mechanisms**

    Parliamentary scrutiny and constitutional confidence procedures can restrain the executive. The legislature's role in legislation and finance also matters because administration cannot lawfully assume every necessary power for itself. These checks operate through defined procedures. Article 100 contains conditions and restrictions, so it should not be summarized as a power to remove a government at any instant. Article 101 provides impeachment arrangements with different requirements for different offices. Calling impeachment a check is therefore only the beginning of an answer. The grounds, authority to initiate and voting rules must be identified for the particular constitutional office concerned.

    **Checks through courts**

    Judicial review tests constitutional and legal authority within the courts' jurisdiction. It does not mean that judges become a general replacement government. A court may need to decide whether an institution possessed a power, observed a procedure or violated a right. The political importance of the dispute does not necessarily remove those legal questions. Independence is necessary because an institution should not be the final judge of every challenge to its own action. At the same time, reasoned decisions and jurisdictional limits keep judicial power within the constitutional arrangement. The judiciary protects the framework through adjudication rather than through unrestricted political supervision.

    **Dissolution as an illustration**

    House dissolution exposes the relationship between political authority and legal limits. The Raviraj Bhandari proceeding against Prime Minister Manmohan Adhikari arose under the 1990 Constitution. Paragraphs 86–87 of its original NKP text explain why constitutional legality can remain reviewable despite political controversy. That proposition must be kept separate from the current dissolution provisions. Under the 2015 Constitution, Articles 76(7) and 85 require examination. The official Constitutional Bench report's Santosh Bhandari proceeding records restoration of the House after invalidation of the contested dissolution. It illustrates judicial enforcement of a constitutional boundary, not judicial choice of the government's preferred programme.

    **Limits of absolute separation**

    Government needs coordination. Legislation often depends on executive proposals and administrative information. Courts interpret and apply laws enacted by legislatures. Financial and appointment procedures can also require participation by more than one institution. Complete isolation could prevent necessary action and weaken accountability. However, cooperation does not authorize one institution to seize another's constitutional function. A lawful check must be distinguished from obstruction or intimidation. This is especially important where institutional language is used to protect political convenience. The reader should always ask which provision authorizes the intervention and what conditions limit its exercise.

    A licensing example makes the distinction concrete. The legislature can establish the governing law, the executive can decide applications within that law and a court can hear an appropriate challenge to legality. A court reviewing the refusal is not necessarily administering the entire licensing programme. Equally, an executive implementing the Act cannot conclusively settle every question about its own constitutional authority. Each institution contributes a different function, and their interaction can preserve accountability without requiring that all decisions be made by one body.

    **Critical view and conclusion**

    Separation and checks work only when institutions respect the Constitution and exercise their responsibilities seriously. Formal division may fail if one political group controls every practical decision, while excessive rivalry can produce deadlock. Nepal's design seeks accountable parliamentary government within a superior constitutional order and an independent judicial framework. The strongest explanation therefore combines division, responsibility and review. Separation allocates functions, checks restrain misuse, and constitutional supremacy gives both a common legal standard. Their purpose is government capable of acting lawfully without permitting any institution to make itself the final authority over all public power.

??? example "Q2. Discuss separation of powers through Nepal's House dissolution disputes. (20 marks)"
    **Introduction**

    House dissolution is a useful test of separation of powers because it affects the relationship between the executive, the representative chamber and the courts. The dispute is politically important, but may also concern the meaning of constitutional authority. A careful answer must distinguish the political wish to seek a new mandate from the legal conditions allowing an existing House to be ended. Nepal's historical cases arose under different constitutional texts. Their general reasoning may remain useful, but their operative provisions cannot be transferred automatically into the Constitution of Nepal 2015.

    **The underlying institutional question**

    Parliament represents the people through an elected chamber, while the political executive governs within constitutional authority and responsibility. Dissolution can end that chamber before its ordinary term. An unrestricted executive power to do so could weaken scrutiny or avoid an inconvenient parliamentary process. Yet a constitution may permit dissolution in specified circumstances when government cannot be formed or maintained. The task is therefore not to declare dissolution always lawful or always unlawful. It is to identify the constitutional source of power, examine the conditions and determine which institution can decide a disputed legal limit.

    **Separation and parliamentary government**

    Nepal's Article 74 adopts a parliamentary form. Article 76 regulates government formation and Article 100 regulates confidence and no-confidence procedures. Executive-legislative connections are therefore part of the design. These connections do not mean that the executive can treat the House as its subordinate. Nor do they mean a court must avoid every dispute involving parliamentary government. Articles 126, 128 and 133 establish judicial functions, interpretive authority and review powers. Separation requires institutions to respect these different responsibilities. Political leadership operates through parliamentary support, while constitutional legality may require independent adjudication when an exercise of power is challenged.

    **The historical dissolution proceedings**

    The prescribed syllabus includes the Hari Prasad Nepal proceeding involving Girija Prasad Koirala and the Raviraj Bhandari proceeding involving Manmohan Adhikari. Both belong to the 1990 constitutional setting. The original Hari Prasad text has not been established in readable form for these notes, so its particular facts and disposition should not be reconstructed. Its place in the historical discussion can be identified through later judicial references without inventing a complete brief. This source distinction is essential. An answer gains nothing from presenting a confident but unsupported comparison merely because the two cases are often mentioned together.

    **Raviraj Bhandari and reviewability**

    The original Raviraj NKP text identifies the proceeding against Prime Minister Manmohan Adhikari and the judgment date 2052/05/12. Paragraphs 86–87 supply a specific usable principle. A controversy does not lose its constitutional or legal character merely because political questions accompany it. Courts may need to separate political choice from legal validity and decide the latter. This reasoning prevents the phrase political question from automatically excluding constitutional review. The text also contains separate judicial views, so a reader must not treat every submission or passage as the court's majority conclusion. The narrow proposition cited here is about reviewability.

    **The current constitutional provisions**

    Article 85 provides the House's ordinary five-year term unless earlier dissolved in accordance with the Constitution, together with stated emergency-extension arrangements. It is not a separate grant of unrestricted dissolution discretion. Article 76(7) must be read with the preceding government-formation provisions. It addresses the circumstances stated there concerning failure to obtain confidence by the Prime Minister appointed under clause (5), or inability to appoint a Prime Minister. The constitutional sequence matters. A claim that dissolution is available simply because parliamentary systems sometimes allow it cannot replace the requirements chosen by Nepal's own constitutional text.

    **The first 2021 dissolution proceeding**

    The Supreme Court's Constitutional Bench report for 2079, Part 3, issue 1 contains decision 0026 concerning Santosh Bhandari and connected petitions, including 077-WC-0028. The reported short order is dated 2077/11/11. Its operative discussion records invalidation of the contested dissolution and restoration of the House. This is a separate primary source under the 2015 constitutional framework. The institutional significance is that a politically consequential executive act remains subject to the Constitution. Judicial restoration in that setting addresses the legal existence of the House. It does not itself determine which political programme Parliament should subsequently approve.

    **The separate Deuba proceeding**

    The same official report contains decision 0028 beginning at printed page 62, involving Sher Bahadur Deuba and others in writ 077-WC-0071. It concerns a separate later dissolution dispute. The distinction matters because different events, claims and orders must not be collapsed into a single generic case. A complete case-specific answer should read that judgment's own reasoning and operative directions. For this institutional discussion, its separate identification shows why legal analysis must connect each decision to its particular constitutional stage and factual setting. A broad account of political conflict cannot substitute for that careful examination.

    **Judicial review and institutional restraint**

    Reviewing constitutional legality is compatible with separation of powers where the Constitution assigns that role to courts. Otherwise, the institution exercising a disputed power could conclusively determine its own limits. However, the court's function remains legal. It must identify authority, interpret conditions and give an appropriate remedy within jurisdiction. It should not assume every political disagreement is a constitutional violation or replace all parliamentary choices. The important distinction is therefore between judging the merits of political strategy and deciding whether constitutional authority exists. Dissolution disputes illustrate how these questions can arise together while still requiring different treatment.

    The applicable remedy also reveals the character of the question. If the defect concerns the legal ending of the House, restoring its constitutional position addresses that defect. It does not guarantee a government a particular majority or resolve every disagreement among political parties. Those matters return to the institutions and procedures that govern political responsibility. Keeping remedy and political consequence distinct makes it easier to explain how a judgment can be highly consequential without turning the court into the ordinary manager of parliamentary government.

    **Critical view and conclusion**

    Dissolution can promise a fresh electoral mandate while simultaneously removing an existing forum of executive responsibility. That tension explains the need for precise constitutional rules. Nepal's history also shows why old and new legal frameworks must be kept distinct. Raviraj supplies a checked historical discussion of reviewability, while Articles 76 and 85 and the separate 2021 decisions address the current framework. Separation of powers is respected when the executive, Parliament and courts each act within their assigned authority. It is weakened when political importance is treated as a general exemption from constitutional limits or when legal review becomes unlimited political supervision.
