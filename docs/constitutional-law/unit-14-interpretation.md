# Unit 14: Constitutional interpretation

!!! abstract "In one line"
    Constitutional interpretation identifies the legal meaning, actual effect and limits of public power without letting labels or procedural devices defeat the Constitution.

## Why it matters for the exam

For each doctrine state the problem, the test, an example and the limit. Label foreign authority by jurisdiction. A useful comparison is not proof that Nepal has adopted every detail of the foreign rule.

!!! warning "Unverified"
    Original 2078 and 2080 questions remain unavailable. Prompts are practice. ## 14.1 Direct and inevitable effect of fundamental rights

The syllabus wording concerns the effect **on** fundamental rights. Look at what the challenged measure necessarily does, not just its title or professed purpose. In India's *Bennett Coleman & Co. v Union of India*, judgment 30 October 1972, AIR 1973 SC 106, the Court examined newsprint restrictions and their effect on expression and circulation.

**Illustration.** A rule described as paper allocation may directly restrict publication. Calling it economic regulation does not settle the freedom-of-expression question. The claimant must still establish the actual effect and applicable right. A remote or speculative disadvantage is not automatically the same as a direct consequence.

For Nepal, identify the particular right, restriction and evidence under Part 3 and the Article 133 framework. Do not call the Indian case a Nepal Article 17 judgment.

## 14.2 Fraud on the Constitution

This doctrine concerns evasion of a constitutional restraint through a device. It is not merely an accusation that a politician acted dishonestly.

In India's *D.C. Wadhwa v State of Bihar*, decided 20 December 1986, AIR 1987 SC 579, systematic ordinance re-promulgation without ordinary legislative enactment was examined as a means of bypassing constitutional limits. The judgment contains qualifications concerning exceptional legislative circumstances. Do not restate it as a rule that every fresh ordinance is automatically invalid.

Nepal's Article 114 provides its own conditions, legislative tabling and termination rules. Under Article 114, an ordinance must be tabled at the next session and ceases to operate in the specified circumstances, including expiration of sixty days after both Houses meet; where they meet on different dates, count from the later date. The Article also permits earlier repeal and ends an ordinance not passed by both Houses. An Indian six-week rule is not Nepal's rule. The useful question is whether a constitutional power is being used to defeat a limit it was meant to preserve.

## 14.3 Harmonious construction

Read provisions together before declaring one meaningless. Start with their wording, setting and purpose. Give both an effective field where possible, while respecting an express priority or limitation. Harmony does not authorize a judge to rewrite a clear provision.

India's *Sri Venkataramana Devaru v State of Mysore*, AIR 1958 SC 255, judgment 8 November 1957, considered the relationship between constitutional religious protections and temple-entry reform provisions. Its discussion explains the need to give effect to both provisions where possible.

**Nepal illustration.** Rights and their own lawful restrictions must be read together; Article 18's equality guarantee must be read with its protective-measures proviso. This is a textual reading exercise, not a claimed Nepal holding importing the entire Indian religious-freedom doctrine.

## 14.4 Severability

If part of a law is invalid, ask whether the valid part can operate independently and whether preserving it respects the legislative scheme. Mechanical deletion is insufficient where it creates a different law.

India's *R.M.D. Chamarbaugwalla v Union of India*, AIR 1957 SC 628, discusses separability, legislative intention and independent operation. A severability clause can help but does not answer every question by itself.

Nepal's Article 1 makes inconsistent law void to the extent of inconsistency. Article 133(1) supplies the relevant review power and possible temporal effect. These provide Nepal's textual starting point, not automatic adoption of every foreign test.

**Illustration.** A separate unlawful eligibility condition may be removable from an otherwise workable programme. If removal leaves no coherent programme or changes its essential design, broader invalidity may need examination.

## 14.5 Colourable legislation

Look beyond the apparent label to the law's true legal character and the enacting legislature's competence. A body cannot obtain a forbidden power merely by changing the name of what it is doing.

India's *K.C. Gajapati Narayan Deo v State of Orissa*, AIR 1953 SC 375, explains that the central question is competence, not the legislature's good or bad motive. An unpopular but competent law is not automatically colourable. It may still face a separate rights challenge.

For Nepal, begin with Articles 57–58, the applicable Schedules and Article 133. A claimed local fee that is actually an exercise of an exclusively allocated different power is a hypothetical competence problem, not a verified finding against any particular Nepal law.

## 14.6 Political questions

Political importance and legal reviewability are different. A court should not choose a party's preferred programme merely because parties disagree. But the existence of political controversy does not remove a constitutional question about authority.

Nepal's historical *Raviraj Bhandari v Manmohan Adhikari*, paragraphs 86–87, expressly addresses that distinction under the 1990 Constitution. Current judicial authority comes from the 2015 framework, including Articles 128 and 133. The separate 2021 dissolution proceedings are discussed in [Unit 5](unit-05-separation-of-powers.md) and [Unit 11](unit-11-federal-legislature.md).

## 14.7 Eclipse

In India's *Bhikaji Narain Dhakras v State of Madhya Pradesh*, AIR 1955 SC 781, the Court explained the effect of supervening constitutional inconsistency on a pre-Constitution law and removal of that inconsistency by amendment. The idea is limited operation under a constitutional obstacle, not an automatic repeal-and-reenactment story.

Do not say every void law always revives when a right changes. Distinguish a previously valid law affected by later constitutional change from legislation made without authority, and examine the jurisdiction's actual rules.

For Nepal, Articles 1, 133 and 304 govern relevant questions of inconsistency, judicial effect and continuity of existing laws. Article 304 continues existing laws until repeal or amendment, but its proviso makes inconsistent law void to the extent of inconsistency after one year from the first session of the Federal Parliament under this Constitution. That specific transitional rule is not interchangeable with India's Article 13.

## Compare

| Doctrine | Main question | Common error |
|---|---|---|
| Direct effect | What does the measure necessarily do to the right? | Trusting its label alone |
| Fraud | Is a device defeating a constitutional limit? | Treating any political disagreement as fraud |
| Harmony | Can the provisions work together? | Rewriting text to manufacture agreement |
| Severability | Can lawful parts survive coherently? | Deleting words without examining the scheme |
| Colourability | Does substance exceed competence? | Equating bad motive with lack of power |
| Political question | Is the disputed issue legal or a policy choice? | Excluding every politically important dispute |
| Eclipse | What is the effect of later inconsistency on existing law? | Promising revival of every invalid law |

## Full 20-mark answers

??? example "Q1. Explain the prescribed doctrines of constitutional interpretation and their limits. (20 marks)"
    **Introduction**

    Constitutional interpretation determines the meaning and legal effect of the fundamental framework. It becomes important where a measure's form differs from its practical effect, provisions appear to conflict or invalidity affects only part of a legal arrangement. The prescribed doctrines organize these problems, but do not replace constitutional text or evidence. Several examples in this unit come from Indian judgments and must be identified as comparative authority. Nepal's own legal starting points include Articles 1, 128 and 133. The correct method explains the problem each doctrine addresses and then asks whether its reasoning fits the applicable provision and jurisdiction.

    **Direct and inevitable effect**

    The effect-based approach examines what a measure necessarily does to a protected right rather than accepting its official description as decisive. Bennett Coleman, decided by the Indian Supreme Court in 1972 and reported as AIR 1973 SC 106, considered newsprint restrictions and expression. A measure described as economic allocation can still have a direct effect on circulation and publication. The doctrine does not mean that every remote consequence proves a violation. The actual effect, right and constitutional restriction need examination. For Nepal, that inquiry must begin with the relevant Part 3 provision and the facts, not a claim that the foreign judgment itself applied Article 17.

    **Fraud on the Constitution**

    A constitutional limit can be undermined through an apparently formal device. Fraud on the Constitution addresses that evasion rather than merely personal dishonesty. D.C. Wadhwa, decided in India in 1986 and reported in 1987, examined systematic re-promulgation of ordinances without ordinary legislative enactment. The concern was executive use of a temporary power to bypass the constitutional role of the legislature. The judgment's qualifications should also be respected. It is not enough to say that every new ordinance is invalid. In Nepal, Article 114 supplies the actual conditions and termination rules, which differ from the Indian provision discussed in that case.

    **Harmonious construction**

    Apparent conflict between provisions should first be examined through their wording, context and purpose. Harmonious construction seeks an interpretation that gives each a meaningful operation where possible. The Indian Devaru judgment, reported as AIR 1958 SC 255, explains this approach in relation to religious protections and reform provisions. Harmony is not a general permission to rewrite a clear rule. An express exception or priority must be respected. In Nepal, reading Article 18's equality guarantee with its protective-measures proviso illustrates the method. The text itself requires the relationship to be explained, rather than selecting one sentence and treating the rest as if it did not exist.

    **Severability**

    Partial invalidity raises the question whether lawful provisions can survive independently. R.M.D. Chamarbaugwalla discusses the legislative scheme, intention and separability. The inquiry is practical as well as textual. Removing words may leave a workable law, or may create a different arrangement that the legislature did not enact. A severability clause can assist but does not conclusively resolve every problem. Nepal's Article 1 makes inconsistent law void to the extent of inconsistency, and Article 133(1) supplies review authority. These provisions give the Nepal inquiry a constitutional foundation while leaving the actual extent and effect of invalidity to proper legal analysis.

    **Colourable legislation**

    Colourability concerns the true character of legislation and the competence of its maker. K.C. Gajapati Narayan Deo explains the Indian doctrine as a question of authority, not the legislature's subjective good or bad motive. A legislature cannot acquire a power denied to it merely by choosing a misleading label. Equally, an unpopular purpose does not by itself establish absence of competence. The law may face another constitutional objection, but that is a different inquiry. In Nepal, the allocation under Articles 57–58 and the Schedules must be identified before concluding that a particular enactment has crossed into a field outside the enacting institution's authority.

    **Political questions**

    Courts do not decide every disagreement about political wisdom. However, political consequences do not automatically remove a legal question from adjudication. Raviraj Bhandari paragraphs 86–87 provide a checked Nepal discussion of this distinction under the 1990 Constitution. The important inquiry is whether the challenge concerns constitutional authority or merely a preferred political choice. Current judicial power must be traced to the 2015 Constitution. Dissolution disputes demonstrate why this matters. Ending a representative chamber can be politically significant and also raise a precise question about the legal conditions for exercising that power. The political setting does not answer the constitutional question by itself.

    **Eclipse**

    Eclipse concerns the effect of a later constitutional obstacle on a previously existing law. In Bhikaji Narain Dhakras, the Indian Court explained how inconsistency with rights affected a pre-Constitution law and how amendment removed the obstacle. This should not become a universal rule that every invalid law automatically revives. A law originally outside legislative authority and an existing law affected by supervening inconsistency present different questions. Nepal's Articles 1, 133 and 304 provide its own framework for inconsistency, judicial effect and continuity. The specific transitional proviso in Article 304 must be considered rather than replaced by an assumed identity with India's Article 13.

    **Applying the doctrines together**

    The doctrines can address different parts of one dispute. A court may identify the actual effect on a right, determine legislative competence, read related provisions together and then decide the appropriate extent of invalidity. They are not rival slogans requiring a choice before the legal problem is understood. The order of reasoning matters. Identifying a constitutional defect comes before deciding whether part of a statute can survive. A conclusion about motive does not replace competence analysis, and a conclusion about political sensitivity does not establish absence of jurisdiction. Each doctrine must answer a defined question supported by the relevant text and facts.

    **Critical view and conclusion**

    Interpretive techniques protect constitutional effectiveness when used with disciplined reasoning. They become unreliable if foreign rules are imported without examining Nepal's text or if broad labels replace evidence. The cases discussed provide specific comparative explanations, while the Nepal application must be grounded in the Constitution and verified local authority. A strong answer therefore defines each doctrine, explains its purpose and acknowledges its limit. Constitutional interpretation should prevent legal forms from defeating constitutional substance while preserving the proper roles of courts, legislatures and political institutions. Its authority comes from reasoned application of the legal framework, not from the attractive name of a doctrine.

??? example "Q2. How should a court examine a law whose label, practical effects and constitutional basis are disputed? (20 marks)"
    **Introduction**

    A constitutional challenge may involve several different allegations. The law's title may conceal its actual effect, its maker may lack competence and only some provisions may be defective. A political controversy may also surround the dispute. The court should separate these questions instead of assuming that one interpretive label answers them all. The starting point is the applicable Constitution, the measure's terms and evidence of operation. In Nepal, Articles 1, 128 and 133 provide the central legal framework, while the relevant rights and allocation provisions determine the particular issue. Comparative doctrines assist reasoning without replacing that framework.

    **Identify the actual legal claim**

    The first task is to determine what the claimant says is unlawful. A complaint that a policy is unwise differs from a claim that an institution lacks constitutional authority. A restriction on expression differs from a dispute about territorial legislative competence. The identity of the decision maker, the legal source invoked and the requested remedy should therefore be made clear. This prevents a politically charged disagreement from being either dismissed without examining its legal content or transformed into judicial control over every policy preference. The question must be concrete enough for the court to identify a legal standard and an appropriate form of adjudication.

    **Examine practical effects**

    A measure's title is relevant but not conclusive. Bennett Coleman illustrates the importance of actual effect in constitutional rights analysis. Newsprint regulation could affect expression and circulation despite being presented as economic administration. A Nepal court would need the particular Part 3 guarantee, the measure's operation and its lawful limitations. Direct effect should not be confused with every possible indirect inconvenience. Evidence matters because an alleged restriction may be substantial, remote or not established at all. The approach protects rights from evasive labels while also requiring the claimant to explain the actual constitutional interference rather than relying on speculation.

    **Determine legislative competence**

    The next question may concern who had authority to legislate. Nepal's Articles 57–58 and Schedules identify relevant fields. A hypothetical measure called a fee might in substance exercise a power allocated elsewhere. The colourability doctrine directs attention to legal character and competence, as explained in K.C. Gajapati Narayan Deo. Bad motive alone is not the same as lack of competence. Conversely, a beneficial purpose does not give an institution a subject that the Constitution assigns to another level. The legal allocation must therefore be established independently of the political attractiveness or unpopularity of the proposed programme.

    **Read the constitutional provisions together**

    Apparent conflict requires examination of context and structure. Harmonious construction seeks to give related provisions meaningful operation where their text permits. The Devaru discussion illustrates the need to avoid treating one constitutional provision as cancelling another without analysis. In Nepal, a rights guarantee must be read with its actual qualification, and a general institutional description with the specific conditions governing a power. Harmony is constrained by wording. It does not authorize the court to remove an express limitation because that would make a preferred policy easier. Interpretation should explain how the provisions interact rather than silently rewriting one of them.

    **Look for evasion through procedure**

    An institution may use an available form in a way that defeats its constitutional purpose or limit. D.C. Wadhwa examined systematic ordinance re-promulgation as an executive means of bypassing legislative lawmaking. The Nepal inquiry would begin with Article 114, including its own tabling and termination framework. The Indian six-week rule must not be substituted for Nepal's period. The point is to test the use of authority against the limit attached to it. Neither an accusation of dishonesty nor the mere repetition of an act establishes the entire legal case without examining the constitutional conditions and relevant facts.

    **Distinguish legal review from political choice**

    A court should not decide which party's programme is most desirable merely because the issue is important. Yet Raviraj paragraphs 86–87 show why political controversy does not automatically exclude a constitutional question. The distinction protects both judicial authority and the role of representative institutions. If a legal limit is disputed, the court may need to decide it within jurisdiction. If the matter is only a lawful policy preference, the political process ordinarily supplies the relevant forum. The current source of Nepal judicial authority is the 2015 framework, even where a historical judgment helps explain the nature of the distinction.

    **Decide the scope of invalidity**

    Finding a defect does not automatically answer what must be set aside. Severability requires examination of whether valid provisions can operate independently without producing a different legislative scheme. R.M.D. Chamarbaugwalla provides a comparative discussion. In Nepal, Articles 1 and 133(1) supply the relevant constitutional starting points, including invalidity to the extent of inconsistency and the stated temporal alternatives. A court should explain the consequence rather than mechanically delete words. Preserving lawful provisions can respect legislative work, but preservation is not justified where the remaining arrangement cannot coherently operate or would require the court to legislate a new scheme.

    **Existing law and later constitutional change**

    A further distinction concerns when the law was made and how the constitutional obstacle arose. The eclipse discussion in Bhikaji Narain addresses a previously existing law affected by later rights inconsistency. It should not be applied as a promise that every void enactment revives automatically after amendment. Nepal's Article 304 contains its own continuity rule and transitional proviso. Those terms, together with Articles 1 and 133, determine the proper inquiry. Keeping timing and legal origin separate prevents a doctrine about supervening inconsistency from being confused with an institution's original absence of power to enact the law.

    **Conclusion**

    A disciplined constitutional examination moves through the actual claim, effects, competence, related provisions and appropriate remedy. The doctrines organize those inquiries, while text and evidence control their application. Foreign judgments can clarify the reasoning problem but cannot silently become Nepal holdings or replace Nepal's own periods, powers and transitional provisions. The result should explain why the measure is lawful or unlawful and why the remedy follows. This approach protects constitutional substance from misleading forms without allowing courts to treat every political dispute as a general invitation to redesign public policy.

    A student should identify the exact constitutional obstacle before discussing its removal. If the problem is lack of legislative competence rather than a later rights restriction, describing it as eclipse may conceal the real defect.

## One-minute revision

- Effects matter alongside labels.
- Fraud concerns evasion of a constitutional restraint.
- Harmony reads related provisions together within their wording.
- Severability asks whether a lawful remainder can survive coherently.
- Colourability concerns competence, not merely motive.
- Political controversy does not erase a legal question.
- Eclipse requires careful attention to timing and the relevant constitutional rules.
